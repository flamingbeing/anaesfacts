# Adductor Canal Block

Saphenous nerve block in the adductor canal, beside the femoral artery under sartorius, for medial leg and knee analgesia.

## Distribution
- Skin of the medial leg and foot
- Sensory block, but a large volume in the subsartorial space can partly block vastus medialis (its femoral nerve branch often runs in the canal)
  - Take care advising unsupported walking after a proximal saphenous block

## Indications
- Medial foot or ankle surgery, added to a sciatic block
- Saphenous vein stripping or harvest
- Knee surgery analgesia, as part of multimodal analgesia

## Anatomy
- Adductor canal: from the apex of the femoral triangle to the adductor hiatus
- Apex of femoral triangle: where the medial border of sartorius crosses the medial border of adductor longus
- Adductor hiatus: where the superficial femoral vessels dive deep towards the popliteal fossa, away from sartorius
- Injection in the aponeurotic compartment proximal to the apex is by definition a femoral triangle block
- Sartorius runs lateral to medial across the thigh and roofs the canal in the lower thigh; trapezoid shape under the subcutaneous fat
- Side walls: vastus medialis laterally; adductor longus or magnus medially (depends on level)
- Saphenous nerve usually anterior to the femoral artery; small, round, hyperechoic
- Femoral vein runs with the artery and nerve
- Near the knee the nerve is close to the descending genicular artery and its saphenous branch

## Equipment
- Linear transducer
- 80 mm needle
- Local anaesthetic 5–10 mL
  - Purely sensory nerve, so high concentrations are not needed and may delay walking if they reach quadriceps motor branches

## Considerations
- Partial quadriceps weakness possible with a more proximal subsartorial injection or a large volume (20-30 mL)
- Educate patients and assist walking
- Perform as distally as practical

## Ultrasound-guided technique
- Position: supine, thigh abducted and externally rotated to expose the medial thigh
- Transducer
  - Linear, transverse on the anteromedial thigh at the junction of the middle and distal third, or a little lower
  - Find the femoral artery in the canal, usually 2–3 cm deep
  - If not obvious, trace it down from the inguinal crease with colour Doppler
  - The nerve need not be seen (often poorly imaged): 5–10 mL next to the artery in the plane between sartorius and vastus medialis is enough
  - Colour Doppler can show the saphenous branch of the descending genicular artery so it is not punctured
  - Trace the artery distally to the adductor hiatus, where it goes deep away from sartorius and vastus medialis towards the back of the thigh and becomes the popliteal artery
- Level
  - Can be done low in the canal but above the hiatus
  - Distal approach: least vastus medialis block; at the lowest level where the artery is still directly under sartorius
  - Proximal approach (mid-thigh): better knee analgesia by blocking posteromedial branches of the nerve to vastus medialis; some call this a distal femoral triangle block
  - Avoid going above the midpoint: likely to become a femoral triangle block with spread to other femoral motor branches
- Clean and drape
- Needle insertion
  - In-plane, lateral to medial, towards the femoral artery
  - Tip anterior to the artery; must pierce the subsartorial fascia
  - Aspirate, inject 1–2 mL to confirm; look for spread around the artery, reposition if not
- Out-of-plane option
  - Through the belly of sartorius
  - Tip may not be visible, so give small 0.5–1 mL boluses while advancing to locate it

## Transsartorial landmark approach
- Success rate high, almost 100%
- Supine
- Feel sartorius just above the knee with the leg straight and actively lifted
- Insert 1 finger-width above the patella over the sartorius belly
- Aim slightly posterior to the coronal plane and slightly caudad through the muscle
- Advance to loss of resistance in the subsartorial fat, typically 1.5 to 3.0 cm deep
- Aspirate, then inject 10 mL

## Flashcards
- Q: Boundaries of the adductor canal?
  - A: From the apex of the femoral triangle (sartorius crossing adductor longus) to the adductor hiatus
- Q: Where should the needle tip go in an ultrasound adductor canal block?
  - A: Anterior to the femoral artery, deep to sartorius, through the subsartorial fascia
- Q: Usual volume for an adductor canal block, and volume that risks quadriceps weakness?
  - A: 5–10 mL; weakness more likely with 20-30 mL or a proximal injection
- Q: Transsartorial landmark approach: insertion point, depth and volume?
  - A: 1 finger-width above the patella over sartorius; 1.5 to 3.0 cm to loss of resistance; 10 mL
