# Peripheral Nerve Blocks Overview

Local anaesthetic placed around nerves after they leave the neuraxis: types, contraindications, complication rates, drug concentrations and benefits.

## Classification

- By type
  - Plexus blocks, e.g. cervical, brachial, lumbar
  - Single nerve blocks, e.g. ulnar, femoral
  - Plane blocks (between muscle layers), e.g. transversus abdominis plane
- By location
  - Truncal: intercostal, TAP, ilioinguinal, paravertebral
  - Upper limb: supraclavicular, infraclavicular, axillary
  - Lower limb: popliteal, saphenous, femoral, ankle

## Contraindications

- Absolute: as for spinal anaesthesia
- Relative (no cardiovascular contraindications, unlike neuraxial blocks)
  - Coagulopathy, especially for deep blocks
  - Pre-existing neurological deficit
  - Surgery where nerve injury or compartment syndrome must be monitored (block masks assessment)
  - Block-specific side effects that are too risky for the patient, e.g. phrenic nerve palsy or pneumothorax from a brachial plexus block in pre-existing lung disease

## Complications

- Block failure: 10%
- Haematoma: in high bleeding-risk sites follow neuraxial anticoagulation guidance
- Infection
- Damage to nearby structures depending on block (e.g. pneumothorax, bowel perforation)
- Nerve injury
  - Temporary: 1:200; 95% resolve within 1-2 weeks
  - Lasting beyond 6 months (permanent): 1:5000
- Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST), allergy
  - Seizure 1:10,000
  - Cardiac arrest 1:33,000

## Technique

- Landmark, ultrasound or nerve stimulator
- Afterwards test sensory distribution and motor block
- Ultrasound evidence: higher success, less LAST

### Local anaesthetic concentrations

- Surgical muscle relaxation: lidocaine 2%, ropivacaine 0.75%, bupivacaine 0.5%
- Surgical analgesia: lidocaine 1%, ropivacaine 0.3-0.5%, bupivacaine 0.25%
- Postoperative analgesia: ropivacaine 0.2%, bupivacaine 0.125%
- Catheters: ropivacaine 0.2%
  - Interscalene: 2 ml/hr plus 2 ml patient bolus every hour
  - Others: 5 ml/hr plus 5 ml patient bolus every hour
- Some mix lidocaine with ropivacaine for faster onset
- Most blocks last about 12 hours

### Checklist when describing a block

- Position and ergonomics
- Probe: linear or curvilinear
- Surface landmarks
- Structures on ultrasound
- Target site for local anaesthetic, tips
- In-plane or out-of-plane; needle direction (lateral to medial, posterior to anterior)
- Needle length, e.g. 80mm echogenic needle
- Volume and concentration, e.g. 15ml of 0.5% ropivacaine
- Indications and complications

## Postoperative care

- Protect the numb limb
- Plan analgesia for when the block wears off

## Benefits compared with general anaesthesia

- Avoids general anaesthesia and its airway instrumentation
- Sympathetic block: better AV fistula maturation and angioplasty success
- Outcomes (ASRA)
  - Better acute pain control than IV opioids
  - May reduce chronic pain by limiting central sensitisation
  - Less PONV (compared to both spinal and PNB, as written in the note)
  - More haemodynamic stability (compared to both spinal and PNB, as written in the note)
  - Fewer airway complications: useful when airway management will be hard
  - Fewer respiratory complications: useful with borderline lung function
    - But some upper limb approaches risk phrenic palsy or pneumothorax
  - Less postoperative cognitive dysfunction
  - Faster return of bowel function
  - Less urinary retention
  - Shorter PACU stay (less nausea, vomiting and severe pain)
  - Less blood loss
  - Lower DVT risk
- Mortality: in theory lower than neuraxial or general anaesthesia; only anecdotal evidence
- Controversial: less cancer recurrence
- Outside surgery
  - Less fracture pain
  - Lower analgesic requirements
  - Fewer nursing interventions

## Awake or asleep blocks

- Awake
  - Needle–nerve contact is painful, which may reduce intraneural injection
  - Early warning of LAST
- Asleep
  - More comfortable, higher satisfaction
  - Easier teaching
  - Downsides: task fixation; epidurals are easier sitting up
- No official ANZCA position beyond an adequately trained practitioner
- General advice: avoid asleep injections where possible; use sedation and analgesia

## Nomenclature

- Standard block names: ASRA/ESRA 2024 consensus (upper and lower limb) and ASRA/ESRA 2021 Delphi consensus (abdominal wall, paraspinal, chest wall)
