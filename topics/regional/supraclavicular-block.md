# Supraclavicular Block

Brachial plexus block at the trunks and divisions over the first rib, beside the subclavian artery; the widest sensory block of any brachial plexus approach.

## Distribution of block
- Whole upper limb (arm, elbow, forearm, hand), often including the shoulder, since all trunks and divisions can be reached here
- Never covers the proximal medial arm skin (intercostobrachial nerve, T2) with any brachial plexus technique; add a subcutaneous injection just below the axilla if needed

## Indications
- Surgery from mid-humerus down to the hand (arm, elbow, forearm, hand)
- Shoulder analgesia possible but less ideal

## Considerations
- Closeness to the pleura made it unpopular until ultrasound, which shows plexus, rib, pleura and subclavian artery and so improved safety
- Trunks and divisions are tightly packed over the first rib, giving the most extensive and reliable block of all brachial plexus approaches
- Now a common choice for upper limb surgery distal to the shoulder

## Complications
- Pneumothorax: the most common serious complication
  - About 1% with paraesthesia/nerve stimulator techniques
  - Usually delayed rather than immediate
  - Keep the needle tip in view at all times
  - Ultrasound safety drove its comeback
  - Best kept for inpatients; not for novices until ultrasound skills are built on safer blocks
- Phrenic nerve paresis in up to 30%: consider avoiding in pulmonary impairment
- Vascular puncture or injection: very vascular area
  - Subclavian artery and dorsal scapular artery (often crosses the plexus here) lie close
  - Suprascapular and transverse cervical arteries also nearby
  - Colour Doppler strongly advised before needling and injecting
- Nerve injury
  - Never inject against high resistance; failure to start injection at an opening pressure below 15 psi may mean intrafascicular injection
- Sparing of the inferior trunk / ulnar nerve
  - Usually when local anaesthetic was not placed in the corner pocket

## Equipment
- Linear transducer (8–18 MHz)
- 50mm needle
- Local anaesthetic: 20–25 mL (e.g. 20ml syringe), e.g. 0.5% ropivacaine
- Nerve stimulator optional: 0.5 mA, 0.1 msec; motor response in arm, forearm or hand, but may be absent despite correct placement

## Positioning
- Supine, semi-sitting or slight lateral; usually supine with the head of the bed raised, head turned away, arm adducted
- Bump under the scapula to create working space
- Ask the patient to reach for the ipsilateral knee: depresses the clavicle and opens the anterolateral neck
- Needle hand (right) lateral to the patient: face the head for a left block, face the feet for a right block

## Ultrasound anatomy
- Linear probe transverse, just above the clavicle at its midpoint, in line with it, tilted slightly caudally (as if to look into the chest)
- Get a cross-section of the subclavian artery (round, anechoic)
  - It crosses the first rib between the anterior and middle scalene insertions, behind the midpoint of the clavicle
- First rib: linear hyperechoic line just lateral and deep to the artery, with an anechoic shadow beneath
- Pleura: hyperechoic line in front of or behind the rib, lung deep to it; confirm by sliding with breathing
- Plexus: cluster of hypoechoic ovals ("bunch of grapes") lateral to the artery
  - Depth typically 1- to 2-cm (needle path 1–3 cm deep, so keep the needle angle shallow)
  - Surrounding fascial sheath often visible
  - Probe adjustment shows upper, middle and lower trunks separately as they converge towards the costoclavicular space
  - Lower trunk: turn to a sagittal plane until the first rib is seen deep to plexus and artery
- Often two clusters, sometimes split by a vessel
  - Superficial/lateral: C5–C7 (shoulder, lateral arm and forearm), traceable up to the interscalene level
  - Deep/medial: C8 and T1 (hand, medial forearm)
  - Both need local anaesthetic for a complete arm and hand block
- Dorsal scapular artery often runs through or near the plexus: always scan with colour Doppler for it and the transverse cervical and suprascapular arteries in the needle path

## Technique
- Aim: needle inside the plexus sheath, posterior and superficial to the subclavian artery, spreading around trunks and divisions
- Skin local anaesthetic, then in-plane, lateral to medial
- Entry into the sheath often gives a palpable "pop"
- Usually 2 injection sites (NYSORA)
  - 1) Corner pocket (between artery and first rib): blocks the inferior trunk and ulnar nerve, and lifts the plexus off the pleura and rib
  - 2) Between the upper and middle trunks (NYSORA)
  - Alternative: inject above the plexus so it is sandwiched in local anaesthetic, staying outside it (ASRA)
- Aspirate, then give 1–2 mL to confirm position; if the plexus is pushed away, advance 1–2 mm closer
- Typical total 20–25 mL (10+10ml); lower volumes have been suggested in older patients

## Continuous supraclavicular block
- Much like an interscalene catheter; aim for the catheter near the trunks and divisions beside the subclavian artery
- Three phases: (1) needle placement; (2) catheter advancement; (3) securing the catheter
- Ultrasound guides the first two in most patients
- Needle in-plane lateral to medial, tip just posterior to the sheath, then pierce the sheath and thread the catheter
- Tunnelling downsides: dislodgement while tunnelling, scarring; commercial securing devices available
- Starting infusion: 5-8 mL/h of 0.2% ropivacaine or bupivacaine 0.125%, with 3-5 mL patient-controlled boluses hourly
- Catheter-over-needle technique also described

## Flashcards
- Q: Most common serious complication of supraclavicular block and its incidence with older techniques?
  - A: Pneumothorax, about 1% with paraesthesia/nerve stimulator techniques; often delayed
- Q: Why inject into the corner pocket?
  - A: To block the inferior trunk (ulnar nerve) and lift the plexus off pleura and rib
- Q: Typical local anaesthetic volume?
  - A: 20–25 mL (e.g. 10+10ml), e.g. 0.5% ropivacaine
- Q: Incidence of phrenic nerve paresis after supraclavicular block?
  - A: Up to 30%
- Q: Which skin area is never covered by any brachial plexus block?
  - A: Proximal medial arm (intercostobrachial nerve, T2)
