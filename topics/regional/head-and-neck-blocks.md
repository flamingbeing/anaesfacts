# Nerve Blocks of the Head and Neck

Trigeminal anatomy, complications specific to head and neck blocks, trigeminal ganglion block and the scalp block.

## Trigeminal anatomy
- Fifth cranial nerve: sensory and motor
- Trigeminal (semilunar, Gasserian) ganglion sits in Meckel's cave, a dural pocket near the petrous apex of the temporal bone in the posterior cranial fossa
- Three divisions leave the ganglion
  - Ophthalmic (V1): sensory; splits into lacrimal, frontal and nasociliary nerves, enters the orbit via the superior orbital fissure
    - Supplies forehead, eyebrows, upper eyelids, front of the nose
  - Maxillary (V2): purely sensory; leaves via foramen rotundum, crosses the pterygopalatine fossa, reaches the orbital floor and emerges at the infraorbital foramen
    - Supplies lower eyelid, upper lip, lateral nose and septum, cheek, roof of mouth, maxillary bone, teeth and sinus, soft and hard palate
  - Mandibular (V3): sensory and motor (muscles of mastication); leaves via foramen ovale
    - Supplies front of the ear, temple, anterior two-thirds of the tongue, and skin, mucosa, teeth and bone of the mandible
- Block sites: at the skull base exit (V2 and V3) or more distally and superficially where they leave the facial bones (V1, V2, V3)
- The whole face can be anaesthetised with five simple nerve blocks (skin procedures, and perioperative analgesia for orthopaedic, craniofacial and cancer surgery)

## Complications of head and neck blocks
- Subarachnoid or epidural spread: high spinal and brainstem anaesthesia
- Intravascular injection: seizures possible with tiny intra-arterial volumes (0.5 mL or less), since head and neck arteries feed straight to the brain
- Bruising and haematoma: reduce with a 25- or 27- gauge needle for superficial blocks and firm pressure straight after injection
- Respiratory distress: phrenic or recurrent laryngeal nerve block, pneumothorax, or loss of airway sensation or motor function

## Trigeminal ganglion block
- Reserved for trigeminal neuralgia not responding to drugs
- Ganglion or division blocks are used as a diagnostic test before neurolysis

## Scalp block
### Anatomy
- Classically seven nerves, from cervical rami and the trigeminal nerve (Figure-17: scalp innervation)
  - Greater occipital, lesser occipital and great auricular nerves: ventral and dorsal rami of C2 and C3
    - Greater occipital runs up to the vertex; lesser occipital supplies skin behind the ear
  - Supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves: from V1 via the frontal nerve; forehead back to the lambdoid suture
  - Zygomaticotemporal nerve: one of two zygomatic nerve branches from V2; small area of forehead and temple
  - Auriculotemporal nerve: from V3; posterior temple skin

### Indications
- Adults and children: head and neck procedures, neurosurgery, chronic pain diagnosis and treatment (e.g. muscular or neural headache)
- Scalp lacerations, foreign body removal, wound exploration, abscess or subdural haematoma drainage
- Craniotomy: blunts haemodynamic responses to pins (Mayfield head holder), scalp incision, craniotomy and dural opening, and adds postoperative analgesia
- Essential for awake craniotomy where the patient must cooperate with testing (epilepsy surgery, deep brain stimulation for Parkinson's disease, lesions in or near eloquent motor, cognitive or sensory cortex)

### Ring (infiltration) technique
- All scalp nerves become superficial above a line from the occipital protuberance to the eyebrows along the top of the ear
- Circumferential infiltration along this line with local anaesthetic containing 1:200,000 adrenaline
- About 30 mL needed

### Regional blocks technique
- Supraorbital and supratrochlear: intradermal needle where they leave the orbit
- Auriculotemporal: 1.5 cm in front of the ear at tragus level
- Postauricular branches of the great auricular: 1.5 cm behind the ear at tragus level
- Greater and lesser occipital: infiltrate along the superior nuchal line, about halfway between occipital protuberance and mastoid

### Complications
- Haematoma at the injection site is the most common
- Aspirate carefully: scalp is very vascular
- Dilute solutions with 1:100,000 or 1:200,000 adrenaline are usually considered safe against toxic plasma levels

## Flashcards
- Q: Why can tiny intra-arterial doses cause seizures in head and neck blocks?
  - A: Arterial flow goes straight to the brain; 0.5 mL or less can be enough
- Q: Which foramina do V2 and V3 exit through?
  - A: V2 foramen rotundum; V3 foramen ovale
- Q: Volume for a scalp ring block?
  - A: About 30 mL with 1:200,000 adrenaline
- Q: Where is the auriculotemporal nerve blocked?
  - A: 1.5 cm in front of the ear at tragus level
- Q: Most common complication of scalp block?
  - A: Haematoma at the injection site
