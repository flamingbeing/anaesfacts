# Lumbar Plexus (Psoas) Block

Deep posterior block of the lumbar plexus within psoas: covers femoral, lateral femoral cutaneous and obturator nerves.

## Overview
- Also called psoas compartment block (PCB) or posterior lumbar plexus block (PLB)
- Blocks the main ipsilateral lumbar plexus branches: femoral nerve (FN), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN), obturator nerve (OBN)
- Used alone or with a sciatic block for hip or lower limb surgery

## Anatomy
- Psoas has two parts
  - Larger anterior part: from the anterolateral vertebral bodies and discs
  - Thinner posterior (accessory) part: from the front of the transverse processes
- Near the vertebral bodies the two parts are separated by a wedge-shaped space next to the intervertebral foramen: the lumbar paravertebral space (LPVS), holding the nerve root, lumbar artery branches and ascending lumbar vein
- Each root runs steeply caudally from the LPVS and joins the psoas compartment one level lower: so the L3 contribution lies opposite the L4 foramen and L4 root
- Formed from the anterior primary rami of L1, L2, L3 and most of L4 within psoas; variable input from T12 (subcostal nerve) and L5
- Lies in an intramuscular fascial plane (psoas compartment) in the posterior one-third of psoas, close to the transverse processes
- Triangular: narrow cranially, wide caudally; branches fan out with LFCN outermost, OBN innermost, FN in between

## Indications
- Hip, knee or femoral shaft surgery
- Below the knee: add a sciatic block

## Complications
- Epidural spread: inject slowly at low pressure
- Intrathecal injection and total spinal: dural cuffs extend laterally, so do not aim too medial near the foramen
- LAST: psoas is very vascular
- Injury to intra-abdominal organs
- Intravascular injection (lumbar arteries nearby)
- Retroperitoneal haemorrhage

## Ultrasound-guided technique
- Curvilinear probe; depth needs low-frequency ultrasound (5–10 MHz)
- Patient lateral

### Shamrock method (transverse, Sauter)
- Probe transverse in the flank just above the iliac crest
  - Find the L4 transverse process by scanning up from the sacrum in a parasagittal plane, then rotate to transverse
- Shamrock with three leaves: psoas major anteriorly, erector spinae posteriorly, quadratus lumborum at the tip of the transverse process
- Plexus: hyperechoic oval in the posteromedial psoas near the transverse process
- Tilt the probe caudally until the L3 transverse process disappears, allowing an in-plane posterior-to-anterior approach; aim to just miss the transverse process
- Insertion point as for the landmark method: about 4 cm lateral to the midline (junction of lateral third and medial two-thirds of a line from the L4 spinous process to a line through the PSIS parallel to the spine)
  - Or estimate it from the ultrasound depth from the probe to the posteromedial psoas
- Advance anteriorly to the plexus or until a suitable twitch
- Inject 20-30ml of local anaesthetic

### Paramedian transverse oblique approach (Kirchmair)
- First described in cadavers
- Probe transverse, 4 cm lateral to the midline on the intercristal line just above the iliac crest, angled slightly medially (paramedian transverse oblique scan, PMTOS)
- Find psoas at L3–L4 or L4–L5, scanning between two transverse processes to avoid their acoustic shadow over psoas and the foramen
- Needle in plane from the medial end of the probe (4 cm from midline, as for landmark technique), to the back of psoas; confirm by needle–nerve contact and/or quadriceps contraction
- Alternative: in plane from the lateral end, heading anteromedially
- After negative aspiration, give 20–25 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine or levobupivacaine in aliquots over 2–3 minutes; monitor closely

### Longitudinal approach (trident view)
- Lateral decubitus, block side up
- Probe paramedian sagittal, 4 cm lateral and parallel to the spine, marker cranial
- Transverse process shadows form the "trident sign"
- Psoas seen through the trident windows as longitudinal hyperechoic striations on a hypoechoic background; plexus nerves as longitudinal hyperechoic lines in posterior psoas
- Needle in plane from the caudal end, through the window between the L3 and L4 transverse processes into posterior psoas, until needle–nerve contact or quadriceps contraction
- After negative aspiration, 20–25 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine or levobupivacaine in aliquots over 2–3 minutes; monitor closely

## Landmark technique (Winnie)
- Lateral, curled, block side up
- Insertion: 4 cm lateral to the midline on the intercristal (Tuffier) line
  - Draw a line parallel to the midline about 4cm lateral (about two-thirds of the way to the PSIS line); insert where it crosses Tuffier's line
- Needle perpendicular to skin
- Contact the transverse process at about 4-7cm, then walk slightly cephalad or caudad
- Plexus at about 6-9cm; end point is femoral stimulation with quadriceps contraction (dancing patella)

## Flashcards
- Q: Which nerves does a lumbar plexus block cover?
  - A: Femoral, lateral femoral cutaneous and obturator nerves
- Q: Where is the landmark insertion point for a Winnie lumbar plexus block?
  - A: 4 cm lateral to the midline on the intercristal (Tuffier) line
- Q: Local anaesthetic volume for an ultrasound-guided lumbar plexus block?
  - A: 20–25 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine or levobupivacaine over 2–3 minutes (shamrock: 20-30ml)
- Q: What are the three leaves of the shamrock view?
  - A: Psoas major anteriorly, erector spinae posteriorly, quadratus lumborum at the tip of the transverse process
- Q: Why is epidural or intrathecal spread a risk?
  - A: The block is close to the foramen and dural cuffs extend laterally; inject slowly at low pressure and do not aim too medially
