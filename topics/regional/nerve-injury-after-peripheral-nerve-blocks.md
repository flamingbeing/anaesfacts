# Nerve injury and residual block after peripheral nerve blocks

Most nerve deficits after a block are temporary; know the risks, causes, red flags and how to prevent and follow up injury.

## Red flags
- Compartment syndrome
- Cauda equina syndrome

## Incidence
- Temporary damage (neurapraxia): 1:100
  - 95% are neurapraxia, resolving in 4-6 weeks, most in the first week
- Permanent damage: 1:5,000
  - 2-5% of deficits are permanent
- Surgery carries a much higher risk of nerve injury than regional anaesthesia

## Risk factors
- Patient
  - Elderly
  - Obese
  - Vasculopaths: smoking, microvascular disease (diabetes)
  - Pre-existing neuropathy (double-crush vulnerability)
- Surgical
  - Traction or stretch, vascular disruption, local inflammation, haematoma
  - Positioning
  - Prolonged tourniquet ischaemia
  - Tight casts
- Anaesthetic
  - Needle or catheter trauma
  - Accidental intrafascicular injection (high opening pressure)
  - Chemical neurotoxicity of local anaesthetic
  - Ischaemia from adrenaline additive
  - Landmark technique compared with ultrasound guidance
  - Block type
    - Upper limb blocks injure nerves more often; interscalene has the highest risk
    - Proximal blocks carry more risk: more neural tissue relative to protective connective tissue (epineurium)
    - Supraclavicular = 0.03%; femoral = 0.3%; interscalene = 3%

## Causes
- Ischaemic
  - Intrafascicular injection (high pressure, paraesthesia)
  - Hypotension
  - Haematoma compressing the nerve
  - Adrenaline in the local anaesthetic
  - Surgical: prolonged tourniquet occluding flow (keep under 120min)
- Mechanical
  - Needle tip entering the nerve (use ultrasound, in-plane)
  - Positioning: pressure or stretch (ulnar nerve, brachial plexus, common peroneal nerve)
  - Surgical stretch or direct injury
- Chemical: high local anaesthetic concentration is neurotoxic
- Double crush: an already damaged nerve (e.g. diabetic neuropathy, carpal tunnel) tolerates a second insult (block or positioning) poorly, so a permanent deficit is more likely

## Intrafascicular injection
- Main cause of serious nerve damage from injection: local anaesthetic inside a fascicle leads to axonal degeneration
- High pressure inside the nerve causes ischaemia
- Warning signs
  - High injection pressure
  - Pain or paraesthesia on injection
  - Nerve swelling on ultrasound during injection
  - Motor response at 0.2mA on nerve stimulation
- Stop injecting

## Managing a persistent deficit
- Recognise early
- Red flags
  - Complete loss of sensation
  - Persistent or worsening sensory loss
  - Anything beyond sensory symptoms (e.g. motor block)
- Manage by severity
  - Ask about job and hand dominance
  - Mild and resolving (e.g. partial sensory loss only): reassure, clinic review in 2-3 week
  - Severe, persistent or worsening (including motor): refer to neurology; nerve conduction studies at 14-21 days
  - After a spinal: think of cauda equina red flags
  - Unexplained painful deficit: consider postoperative inflammatory neuropathy and refer to neurology
- Investigations
  - Neurology referral if concerned (e.g. 2 weeks)
  - Early MRI; nerve conduction studies and EMG at 6 weeks
  - NCS/EMG are unhelpful early: Wallerian degeneration and denervation potentials (fibrillations) take 2–3 weeks to show

## Red flag conditions
- Cauda equina or conus medullaris compression
  - Severe back pain, bilateral leg weakness, bladder or bowel dysfunction (retention, overflow incontinence)
  - Emergency spinal MRI and urgent spinal surgical review; decompression window <8hours
- Limb ischaemia or compartment syndrome
  - Lost distal pulses, slow capillary refill, pain out of proportion, tense swelling
  - Tell the surgical team at once: emergency fasciotomy or removal of dressings

## Prevention
- Preoperative: select patients; beware double crush
- Technique
  - Block awake, watching for pain on injection
  - Ultrasound: see the needle tip at all times
    - Operator dependent; cannot tell intrafascicular from extrafascicular
  - Nerve stimulation to exclude intrafascicular placement
    - Hydrodissect with 5% dextrose; current 0.2mA
  - Injection pressure monitoring
    - Low resistance (<20psi) suggests extrafascicular tip
    - Inline devices or compressed air technique
    - Highly sensitive, poorly specific: high pressure has other causes (e.g. blocked needle)
- Equipment and drugs
  - Blunt, short-bevel needles
  - Lower local anaesthetic concentration
  - Avoid adrenaline
- Other contributors: avoid hypotension, tourniquet <120m, careful positioning
- Postoperative: assess and recognise early

## Flashcards
- Q: Incidence of temporary and permanent nerve damage after peripheral nerve block?
  - A: Temporary 1:100 (most resolve in 4-6 weeks); permanent 1:5,000
- Q: Signs of intrafascicular injection?
  - A: High injection pressure, pain or paraesthesia, nerve swelling on ultrasound, motor response at 0.2 mA
- Q: Injection pressure that suggests an extrafascicular needle tip?
  - A: Below 20 psi
- Q: Why are nerve conduction studies unhelpful in the first days?
  - A: Wallerian degeneration and denervation potentials take 2–3 weeks to appear
- Q: Which block carries the highest nerve injury risk?
  - A: Interscalene (proximal, upper limb)
