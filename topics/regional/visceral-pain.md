# Visceral Pain and Sympathetic Blocks

Chronic abdominal and pelvic visceral pain: poorly localised, linked to sensitised afferents and psychological trauma; treat somatic, neuropathic and visceral parts, with sympathetic axis blocks and neurolysis for refractory or cancer pain.

## Incidence
- Mostly chronic pelvic and abdominal pain
- Each affects 10 million US adults; more common in women

## Pathogenesis
- Visceral afferents become sensitised after injury, inflammation and stress
- Psychological trauma (including physical or sexual abuse before adolescence) is strongly linked to:
  - Functional abdominal pain in children
  - Multiple somatic complaints in adults, such as irritable bowel syndrome, chronic pelvic pain and fibromyalgia

## Symptoms
- Poorly localised, from complex innervation and viscerosomatic convergence
- Dull, aching, crampy
- Associated abdominal distension
- Visceral autonomic dysfunction (sexual, urinary, bowel) often coexists
- Worse with stress

## Evaluation
- Exclude treatable pathology
  - Bowel, bladder or sexual dysfunction, including interstitial cystitis, irritable bowel syndrome, adhesion colic
  - Opioid-induced bowel dysfunction (distension from constipation; sphincter dysfunction)
- Look for somatic contributions from the abdominal wall or pelvic floor
  - Rectus sheath, oblique muscles, pelvic floor spasm, trigger points
  - Sensory loss, hyperaesthesia or allodynia suggesting scar neuroma or intercostal, ilioinguinal or genitofemoral neuralgia after surgery
  - No abdominal wall findings points to visceral pain
- History of past psychological trauma and current distress

## Treatment
- Somatic component (abdominal wall or pelvic floor)
  - NSAIDs, muscle relaxants, physiotherapy, TENS, topical lidocaine, trigger point injections
- Neuropathic component (abdominal wall or pelvic floor)
  - Antineuropathic drugs
  - Peripheral nerve blocks: ilioinguinal, genitofemoral, pudendal, transversus abdominis plane (TAP)
- Visceral component
  - Minimise opioids to limit opioid-induced bowel dysfunction
  - Gabapentin or pregabalin, TCAs, SNRIs, other antineuropathics for visceral hypersensitivity
  - Coeliac plexus block (pancreatitis, renal disease, upper abdominal surgery, hypersensitivity)
- Psychological therapy (cognitive behavioural therapy, stress reduction) is critical

## Sympathetic axis blocks
- Coeliac plexus block (CPB)
  - Level: L1 (retrocrural or transcrural)
  - Upper abdominal pain: pancreas, liver, distal oesophagus to transverse colon
  - Gold standard for pancreatic cancer pain
  - Risks: orthostatic hypotension, diarrhoea
- Splanchnic nerve block
  - Level: T11–T12, anterolateral vertebral bodies
  - Similar to, or an alternative to, CPB when tumour or anatomy distorts the coeliac space; safer
  - Targets preganglionic fibres; often needs less neurolytic
  - Usually posterior approach; can be done transgastrically by gastroenterologists
- Superior hypogastric plexus block (SHPB)
  - Level: L5–S1 (aortic bifurcation, sacral promontory)
  - Pelvic pain from descending colon to rectum, bladder and urogenital organs
  - Purely sympathetic; less bladder and bowel dysfunction than lower blocks
- Inferior hypogastric plexus block (IHPB)
  - Level: S2–S4 (deep presacral pelvis)
  - Lower pelvic and perineal visceral pain (prostate, vagina, bladder base)
  - Mixed sympathetic and parasympathetic; technically hard, used less because of urinary retention and sexual dysfunction
- Ganglion impar block (GIB)
  - Level: sacrococcygeal junction (ganglion of Walther), the end of the sympathetic chains
  - Usually approached through the sacrococcygeal ligament
  - Perineal, anal and coccygeal (tailbone) pain; also pelvic, rectal and genital pain
  - Can progress from block with steroid to ablation if it helps

## Neurolytic procedures
- Diagnostic block first
  - Small dose of local anaesthetic; good pain relief without motor or sensory loss means the block works
- Ablation days later
  - 50-100% ethyl alcohol, 6-10% phenol, or radiofrequency ablation
  - Relief usually lasts 3 to 6 months (nerves regenerate); can be repeated

## Coeliac plexus anatomy
- Supplies gut from the gastro-oesophageal junction to the splenic flexure
- Sympathetic input from the T5-12 splanchnic nerves
- Lies below the diaphragmatic crus, anterior to the coeliac artery at T12

## Coeliac plexus block complications
- Postural hypotension and diarrhoea (unopposed parasympathetic activity)
- Pneumothorax, retroperitoneal bleeding, kidney or bowel injury, epidural spread

## Flashcards
- Q: Which sympathetic block is the gold standard for pancreatic cancer pain, and at what level?
  - A: Coeliac plexus block, at L1.
- Q: Two common side effects of coeliac plexus block?
  - A: Postural hypotension and diarrhoea (unopposed parasympathetic).
- Q: Level and indication of superior hypogastric plexus block?
  - A: L5–S1; pelvic pain from descending colon to rectum, bladder and urogenital organs.
