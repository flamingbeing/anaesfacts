# Fascia Iliaca Block

Large-volume injection under the fascia iliaca that spreads to the femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves; low risk, used mainly for hip and anterior thigh analgesia.

## Distribution
- Femoral nerve blocked in all cases (100%)
  - Anterior and medial thigh down to and including the knee
  - Variable strip of skin on the medial leg and foot
  - Articular branches to hip and knee
- Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve blocked most of the time (80%-100%)
  - Skin of the anterolateral thigh
- Anterior branch of obturator nerve: unreliable

## Indications
- Anterior thigh and knee surgery
- Hip surgery
- Analgesia for hip fractures managed conservatively

## Anatomy
- Fascia iliaca compartment: a potential space
  - Anterior: posterior surface of the fascia iliaca
  - Posterior: anterior surface of iliacus and psoas major
  - Medial: vertebral column (also given as the linea terminalis or pelvic brim)
  - Lateral: inner lip of the iliac crest
  - Cranio-medial: continuous with the space between quadratus lumborum and its fascia
- Nerves in the compartment
  - Femoral nerve (L2–4): hip, anterior (extensor) compartment of the thigh, skin of anterior thigh, medial leg and medial foot to the first MTP joint
  - Lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh (L2–3): skin of anterolateral thigh
- Principle: both nerves run under the fascia iliaca, so a large volume placed beneath it, even away from the nerves, can spread to reach them
- Fascia iliaca splits the space under the inguinal ligament into two compartments
  - Superomedial: femoral vessels (femoral sheath)
  - Inferolateral: psoas major, iliacus and femoral nerve
- Spread proximally to the lumbar plexus was traditionally assumed but has not been shown consistently

## Safety
- Very low risk profile
- Injection site far from vessels and nerves, so intravascular injection (LAST) and mechanical nerve injury are very unlikely

## Equipment
- 30-40 mL of dilute local anaesthetic (e.g. 0.2% ropivacaine)
- 80- to 100-mm needle; short bevel helps feel the fascial pops
- Linear transducer

## Infrainguinal ultrasound technique
- Position: supine, bed flat to expose the groin
  - Femoral pulse helpful but not needed: the artery is found quickly by scanning across the crease
- Probe: linear, transverse, at and parallel to the femoral crease, lateral to the femoral artery
- Sonoanatomy
  - Femoral artery first, as for a femoral block
  - Hyperechoic femoral nerve lateral to the artery, deep to the fascia iliaca
  - Iliopsoas: large hypoechoic muscle lateral and deep to the femoral vessels
  - Fascia iliaca: thin layer on the surface of iliacus, separating it from the tissue above
    - Bounded superolaterally by the iliac crest; medially merges with the psoas fascia
    - Femoral nerve and lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh lie under it in their pelvic course
  - Fascia lata: more superficial (subcutaneous layer), may have more than one layer; superficial to the femoral artery but can lie close to the fascia iliaca over iliopsoas
  - Slide laterally several centimetres to find sartorius (covered by its own fascia and the fascia iliaca); further lateral is the anterior superior iliac spine
- Target: under the fascia iliaca at about the lateral third of the line from ASIS to pubic tubercle, several centimetres lateral to the artery
- Needle
  - Skin local, then in-plane, lateral to medial
  - Fascia is seen to indent, then a pop as it is pierced, and the fascia snaps back on screen
  - Inject 1 to 2 mL to confirm the plane between fascia and iliopsoas; reposition if spread is above the fascia or inside the muscle
  - Adults usually need a large volume, between 30 and 40 mL
  - Correct spread separates the fascia from the muscle medially and laterally
  - Best predictor of success: spread medially to the femoral nerve and laterally under sartorius
  - If spread is poor, add injections medial or lateral to the first
- Out-of-plane approach may suit obese patients

## Suprainguinal approach
- More targeted; needs less volume and makes catheter placement easier
- Probe: high-frequency linear, parasagittal, just inferior and medial to the ASIS
  - An angle more perpendicular to the inguinal ligament may be better than strictly parasagittal
- Sonoanatomy
  - Iliacus superficial to the ilium and AIIS; hyperechoic fascia iliaca on its superficial border; tilt to optimise
  - Bowtie sign: internal oblique and sartorius
  - Deep circumflex iliac artery between internal oblique and fascia iliaca: landmark and structure to avoid
  - Confirm by scanning along the inguinal ligament to the femoral artery, then back superolaterally to the AIIS bump on the ilium
- Needle
  - 100 mm needle, 1 cm cephalad to the inguinal ligament, in-plane, directed cranially
  - Tip under the fascia iliaca; hydrodissect fascia off iliacus, advance further cranially and slightly dorsally
  - LA pools below the artery, above iliacus, and spreads cephalad

## Continuous catheter
- Advance the catheter 5–10 cm beyond the needle tip
- Intermittent large boluses may give better spread than a fixed infusion

## Landmark technique
- Success is inconsistent because false pops occur
- Insertion point: 1cm below the junction of the lateral 1/3rd and medial 2/3rd of the line from ASIS to pubic tubercle
- Feel for a double pop (fascia lata then fascia iliaca)
- Optionally angle about 60 degrees with the tip cranial; then flatten to about 30 degrees and advance 1-2 mm more
- Inject about 20mls slowly

## Flashcards
- Q: Which nerves does a fascia iliaca block reliably cover?
  - A: Femoral (100%) and lateral femoral cutaneous (80%-100%); obturator unreliable
- Q: Volume and drug for an adult fascia iliaca block?
  - A: 30-40 mL of dilute LA, e.g. 0.2% ropivacaine
- Q: Landmark insertion point?
  - A: 1 cm below the junction of the lateral third and medial two-thirds of the ASIS to pubic tubercle line
- Q: Suprainguinal approach needle entry?
  - A: 1 cm cephalad to the inguinal ligament, in-plane, directed cranially
