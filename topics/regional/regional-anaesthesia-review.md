# Regional anaesthesia review

Core anatomy, technique and complications for common neuraxial and peripheral blocks, plus subclavian vein anatomy, ultrasound physics and LAST.

## Subclavian vein (infraclavicular cannulation)
- Origin: continues on from the axillary vein at the lateral border of the 1st rib
- Course: behind the clavicle, separated from the subclavian artery by anterior scalene; grooves the upper surface of the 1st rib
- Termination: behind the sternoclavicular joint, joining the IJV to form the brachiocephalic vein, which drains to the SVC
- Relations
  - Anterior: back of the clavicle, subclavius
  - Posterior: anterior scalene, subclavian artery, phrenic nerve, internal mammary artery (medially)
  - Superior: skin, platysma, superficial aponeurosis
  - Inferior: 1st rib, lung apex (medially), 1st intercostal space (laterally)
  - Medial: thoracic duct, brachiocephalic trunk, trachea, vagal trunk
  - Lateral: inferior trunk of the brachial plexus
- Landmarks: clavicle, both heads of sternocleidomastoid (SCM), suprasternal notch, deltopectoral groove, manubriosternal junction
- Infraclavicular approach: enter 2-3cm below the deltopectoral groove, aim at the suprasternal notch
- Supraclavicular approach
  - Enter at the angle between clavicle and SCM, just above and lateral to the clavicular head of SCM
  - Aim at the opposite nipple, just under the clavicle
  - Expect the jugulosubclavian venous bulb at 1-4cm

## Spread of intrathecal local anaesthetic
- Solution factors
  - Baricity: hyperbaric solutions spread more predictably, in the direction of gravity
  - Plain bupivacaine: hyperbaric at 24 deg C but hypobaric at 37 deg C, so spread is more variable
  - Volume, dose and concentration: small volumes (1.5-2ml) may limit spread; a 50% dose (and volume) increase adds roughly one dermatome on average
  - Temperature: density falls as temperature rises
  - Viscosity: more viscous solutions (e.g. glucose 10%) spread further
- Technique factors
  - Position: hyperbaric sinks, hypobaric floats; little effect on truly isobaric solutions
  - Injection level: with plain bupivacaine, a higher level gives more cephalad spread; less consistent with hyperbaric
  - Needle: paramedian approach with marked cephalad angle spreads further
  - Intrathecal catheters: tip position and direction make distribution very variable
  - Barbotage (repeated aspiration and reinjection of CSF): little evidence it increases spread
  - Faster injection spreads plain solutions further (evidence conflicting)
  - Epidural injection compresses the thecal sac and pushes LA-containing CSF cephalad
- Patient factors
  - Age: higher blocks in younger patients
  - Sex: generally less cephalad spread in males than non-pregnant females
  - Height: longer spinal column gives less cephalad spread
  - Weight: no clear effect
  - Spinal curvature alters gravitational spread
  - Raised intra-abdominal pressure: more cephalad spread (tissue pushed through foramina reduces CSF volume)
  - Pregnancy: more cephalad spread (lordosis, CSF volume and density change)

## Epidural space
- Mid-thoracic epidural: T7-T9; needle between mid-thoracic vertebrae, drug via needle and/or catheter
- Extent: foramen magnum to sacral hiatus, between dural sac and bony canal
- Borders: anterior posterior longitudinal ligament; posterior ligamentum flavum; lateral pedicles and intervertebral foramina
- Surface landmarks
  - C7 (vertebra prominens): most prominent cervical spinous process
  - Lower tip of scapula: T7
  - Intercristal line: crosses L4 body in most patients
- Ligaments, superficial to deep: supraspinous (joins spinous process tips), interspinous (between processes), ligamentum flavum (tough; posterior wall of the space)
- Contents: nerves, vessels and fat in a discontinuous space (posterior, lateral and anterior compartments); LA is usually placed posteriorly and must spread to work

### Thoracic versus lumbar
- Thoracic
  - 12 vertebrae in a kyphosis; flexion helps only a little
  - Long, caudally angled spinous processes: midline needs a steep cephalad angle (harder in short patients); paramedian is easier and more reliable mid-thoracic
  - Smaller intervertebral foramina: lateral angulation may give a paravertebral catheter and one-sided block
  - Shallow skin-to-space depth (as little as 3cm) and a large cord: dural puncture can happen early and may injure the cord
- Lumbar
  - Lordosis is reduced by flexion, which greatly improves success
  - Broad, near-perpendicular spinous processes: slight cephalad angle; perpendicular or slightly caudad at low levels
  - Midline is standard; paramedian needs less angle than thoracic
  - Ligamentum flavum rarely less than 4cm deep
  - Cord ends at L1/2 (90%) or L2/3 (10%): no needle risk to the cord at or below L3/4

## Thoracic paravertebral block
### Anatomy
- Boundaries
  - Medial: vertebral body, intervertebral disc, intervertebral foramen
  - Posterior: transverse process, rib head, superior costotransverse ligament (CTL)
  - Anterolateral: parietal pleura, posterior intercostal membrane (remnant of innermost intercostal), endothoracic and subserous fascia
- Continuous up and down; communicates with the epidural space, intercostal spaces and the opposite paravertebral space (beneath the anterior longitudinal ligament and endothoracic fascia)
- Contents: intercostal nerves, rami communicantes, sympathetic chain, endothoracic and subserous fascia
- Depth to transverse process depends on habitus, shallowest mid-thoracic: mean 2.5cm at T4
- Transverse process to pleura (the space): mean 4.5cm, 3.5cm from midline

### Indications
- Anaesthesia or postoperative analgesia: thoracic, breast, cholecystectomy, appendicectomy, inguinal hernia repair
- Rib fractures, liver capsule pain, neuralgia, pancreatic pain

### Advantages (e.g. radical mastectomy)
- One injection blocks more than 1 dermatome (spread along the space, intercostal spaces and nerves)
- Dense somatic and sympathetic block, often enough for surgery alone
- Prolonged analgesia equal to or better than epidural, with less bilateral sympathetic block (hypotension) and less nerve damage
- Reduces chronic pain after breast and thoracic surgery (dense block prevents central sensitisation and NMDA wind-up)
- May reduce tumour recurrence after breast surgery (unproven)

### Complications
- Partial or complete failure about 10%, even with experts
- Significant pneumothorax; haemothorax (rare)
- Hypotension (rare): usually one-sided, responds quickly to fluids and/or vasopressors
- Epidural spread (at least 10%)
- Total spinal with cardiovascular collapse (extremely rare)
- LAST (rapid absorption from the space)
- Direct nerve injury (needle in spinal nerve, medial path into the neuraxis, neurotoxic agents)
- Horner's syndrome (a sign of an effective block)
- Pain (so often done under GA)
- No infections reported

### Landmark technique
- Position: sitting, neck and back flexed (awake or sedated); lateral, operative side up (under GA)
- Mark spinous process tips; injection point 2.5cm lateral to midline at the same level
- Asepsis: 0.5% chlorhexidine spray, air-dried
- 18-22G Tuohy needle on extension tubing with LA
- Insert straight anteroposterior with a finger guard at the 3.5cm mark
- No bone: withdraw, redirect slightly caudad then cranial; if still none, move the guard to 4cm, 4.5cm then 5cm and repeat
- On bone: note depth, move guard 1cm deeper, withdraw and re-insert caudad and slightly lateral to the guard
- The CTL is passed and the space reached within 1-1.5cm
- Confirm by change in resistance (air or saline syringe); complete loss of resistance suggests pleural puncture
- Nerve stimulator can confirm (intercostal or abdominal muscle twitch)
- Negative aspiration, then adrenaline test dose (watch for heart rate rise)
- Single shot: 15-20mls 0.5% ropivacaine or levobupivacaine (with clonidine 1mcg/ml) gives 4-12hrs of dense analgesia
- Catheter: advance only 1-2cm (deeper risks epidural or anterior placement); infuse 0.1ml/kg/hr of 0.1-0.2% ropivacaine

### Ultrasound technique
- Use to map depth to the transverse process and space, to assist landmarks, or for real-time guidance
- Parasagittal out-of-plane
  - Probe parasagittal about 5cm from midline: see rib, posterior intercostal membrane and pleura
  - Slide medially to the step from rib to the more superficial transverse process; identify CTL and space beneath
  - Needle out-of-plane, tip past the CTL; watch the gap between CTL and pleura widen
  - Follow spread up and down to judge need for more injections; check a catheter by injecting air
- Transverse in-plane: needle from lateral, tip between internal intercostal membrane and pleura, watch LA spread

## Caudal block (child)
### Indications
- Children: sub-umbilical surgery (inguinal hernia, urology, anal atresia repair, lower limb)
- Adults: caudal epidural steroid for chronic low back pain after failed conservative treatment

### Contraindications
- Absolute: patient or parent refusal, LA allergy, local infection, severe coagulopathy, raised ICP
- Relative: systemic sepsis, hypovolaemia; spinal stenosis, spinal dysraphism (tethered cord), sacral anomalies (previous myelomeningocele), pilonidal cyst

### Anatomy
- Sacral hiatus (unfused laminae of S4-S5) between the sacral cornua, above the gluteal cleft, at the apex of a triangle with both posterior superior iliac spines
- Needle or cannula at 45 degrees through skin, subcutaneous tissue and sacrococcygeal membrane; a pop marks the epidural space
- Canal contents: epidural fat, epidural venous plexus, filum terminale, sacral roots
- Cord ends L1-2 in adults, L3-4 in infants
- Dural sac ends L3-4 in adults, S3-4 in infants
- Sacral epidural volume rises with age and height: about 10ml in infants to 30mls in adults

### Technique
- History, examination, contraindications, consent; usually under GA in children
- Prone or lateral with hips and knees flexed; full asepsis
- 0.125-0.25% bupivacaine or ropivacaine (less motor block)
- Volume: 0.5mls/kg sacral, 1ml/kg lumbar, 1.25ml/kg low thoracic dermatomes

### Complications
- Intravascular injection (LAST); subdural injection (high or total spinal); intraosseous injection
- Anorectal perforation, nerve injury, haematoma
- Infection including sacral osteomyelitis
- Hypotension

## Scalp nerves (awake craniotomy)
- Supraorbital: frontal nerve (V1); forehead back to the lambdoid suture; supraorbital notch above the medial iris looking forward; risks nerve injection, eyelid injury, orbital injection
- Supratrochlear: frontal nerve (V1); lower forehead; medial orbital corner, a few mm lateral to the nasal apex
- Zygomaticotemporal: zygomatic nerve (V2); small forehead and temporal area; midway between supraorbital and auriculotemporal
- Auriculotemporal: V3; back of temple, tragus, front half of ear; 1.5cm in front of the tragus near the superficial temporal artery, above the zygoma; risks facial nerve, artery, intra-articular injection
- Greater occipital: C2/3 (C1-4); back of scalp to vertex; end of medial third of line from occipital protuberance to mastoid tip; risk arterial injection
- Lesser occipital: C2; lateral posterior scalp; start of lateral third of the same line

## Shoulder surgery and interscalene block
### Nerves to block
- Main motor and sensory: suprascapular nerve (upper trunk) and axillary nerve (posterior cord)
- Minor sensory: lateral pectoral, musculocutaneous, subscapular
- Skin over cape of shoulder, upper chest, ACJ and SCJ: supraclavicular nerves (superficial cervical plexus, C3, 4)
- Skin below the glenohumeral joint: superior lateral cutaneous nerve of arm (axillary), medial cutaneous nerve of arm (medial cord), lateral cutaneous branch of the 2nd intercostal nerve

### Neurological complications
- Phrenic nerve block
  - Depends on LA type and volume: 100% with classic landmark technique, 25-50% with reduced dose
  - FEV1 40%
  - Most healthy patients compensate with intercostal and accessory muscles
  - High risk (OSA, lung disease): troublesome dyspnoea, may need ventilatory support
  - Reduce: modify technique, diagnose with diaphragm ultrasound, intraoperative CPAP or high-flow nasal oxygen, HDU after
- Nerve dysfunction more common than with other peripheral blocks: temporary in 14% at 10 days
  - Peripheral nerve or cervical cord injury
  - Use ultrasound; block only awake or lightly sedated patients
- Transient Horner's syndrome: ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis, nasal congestion; can distress patients
- Recurrent laryngeal nerve block: hoarseness; contraindicated if opposite RLN palsy

### Other complications
- LAST: vertebral artery injection may cause seizures
  - Keep a superficial path relative to the C7 root, aspirate, inject in increments, watch spread
- Hypotension and bradycardia
  - Up to 20% in shoulder surgery, typically beach chair, about 30 mins after the block
  - Causes: vasovagal; Bezold-Jarisch reflex (high catecholamines plus an underfilled, hypercontractile ventricle from venous pooling when sitting trigger cardiac mechanoreceptors, so vagal tone rises suddenly and sympathetic tone falls)
  - Treat promptly with an antimuscarinic (atropine, fast onset) with or without a sympathomimetic

### Preventing phrenic nerve palsy
- Aim: less LA reaching the phrenic nerve, by changing dose, site, injection method or block
- Lower volume (larger volumes spread further)
  - 20mls around C5-6 roots: about 100% palsy
  - Ultrasound-guided 10mls: 60%
  - 5mls: 25-50%
  - Analgesia not compromised up to 24hrs after surgery
- Lower concentration (independent of volume): less palsy and better lung function, but weaker analgesia and more opioid use
- Change site
  - Extrafascial (periplexus) injection 4mm lateral to the plexus sheath: similar analgesia, palsy falls from 90% to 21%
  - Intrafascial injection below C6, at the C7 root: similar analgesia, palsy 13% (from 93%)
  - Posterior versus anterior to C5-6 root: no difference
- Slow, low-pressure, small titrated aliquots may limit spread (no published support yet)

### Alternatives to interscalene block
- Superior trunk block
  - Trunk formed by C5-6; phrenic nerve already well away
  - Find and avoid suprascapular and transverse cervical arteries
  - 10-15mls LA covers the main shoulder supply; done before the suprascapular nerve leaves (it runs laterally deep to omohyoid)
  - Phrenic palsy 25-50%
  - Reports of weaker sensory block and more analgesia needed; no prospective randomised data yet
- Supraclavicular block
  - Similar shoulder analgesia, less Horner's
  - Phrenic block risk similar; may miss an early-branching suprascapular nerve
- Suprascapular plus axillary nerve block
  - Suprascapular gives about 70% of capsular sensation; block in the supraspinous fossa (posterior) or as it leaves the upper trunk deep to omohyoid (anterior)
  - Axillary nerve: anterior chest (from posterior cord), proximal axilla, or posterior humerus at the quadrangular space
  - Analgesia not anaesthesia; less effective than interscalene for open or extensive surgery (capsule only)
- GA with subacromial or intra-articular LA at the end of surgery: 20-40mls with or without a catheter

## Femoral nerve block (at the inguinal ligament)
### Anatomy
- Posterior divisions of anterior rami L2-L4, formed within psoas
- Leaves psoas laterally, runs in the groove between psoas and iliacus (supplies iliacus)
- Passes under the midpoint of the inguinal ligament into the femoral triangle, lateral to the femoral sheath (artery, vein, lymphatics)
- Lies deep to fascia iliaca on psoas; the iliopectineal band separates it from the sheath
- Divides early into
  - Superficial: musculocutaneous branch to sartorius continuing as intermediate cutaneous nerve (medial and lateral front of thigh); medial anterior cutaneous nerve; nerves to pectineus and rectus femoris
  - Deep: nerves to vastus lateralis, intermedius and medialis (each with a knee capsule branch; vastus medialis with the SFA into the adductor canal); saphenous nerve (with SFA in the adductor canal; medial knee and leg to base of 1st metatarsal)
- Femoral triangle: roof fascia lata; floor pectineus, iliopsoas, adductor longus; superior inguinal ligament; lateral medial border of sartorius; medial medial border of adductor longus

### For total knee arthroplasty
- Advantages: excellent analgesia (faster recovery, fewer complications, better satisfaction); opioid sparing (less PONV, giddiness, respiratory depression, urinary retention, pruritus)
- Disadvantages
  - Quadriceps weakness delays mobilisation and risks falls
  - Single shot lasts only 12-24hrs
  - Vascular puncture or injection (especially with nerve stimulator); nerve damage less likely as branches separate
  - Day-case TKR and ERAS need motor-sparing analgesia
  - Alternatives exist, e.g. adductor canal block (almost purely sensory)

## Hip fracture and fascia iliaca block
### Preoperative pain
- Assess on arrival, 30 mins after analgesia, hourly until settled on the ward, then with routine observations
- Give analgesia on arrival, even if cognitively impaired; enough to allow investigations
- Stepwise multimodal: oral paracetamol 6hourly unless contraindicated, opioids, nerve block (e.g. fascia iliaca) by trained staff; NSAIDs not recommended

### Fascia iliaca compartment
- Anterior: back of fascia iliaca
- Posterior: front of iliacus and psoas major
- Medial: psoas origin and vertebral column
- Lateral: iliacus origin and inner iliac crest
- Targets: lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh, femoral nerve, obturator nerve

### Ultrasound technique
- Supine; full asepsis (hat, gown, gloves, mask, 0.5% chlorhexidine spray air-dried, probe cover, sterile gel)
- 50mm, 22G block needle unless habitus needs longer
- High-frequency linear probe transverse below the inguinal ligament, lateral to the femoral artery; find fascia iliaca, fascia lata and iliacus
- Skin wheal over sartorius; needle in-plane from lateral, tip under fascia iliaca
- Aspirate, inject 1-2mls: fascia should lift off muscle and spread medially towards the femoral nerve
- Then 30-40mls dilute LA (e.g. 40mls 0.25% bupivacaine, max 2mg/ml) with repeated aspiration

## Popliteal sciatic block
### Popliteal fossa
- Upper medial: semitendinosus; upper lateral: biceps femoris
- Lower medial and lateral: medial and lateral heads of gastrocnemius
- Contents: popliteal artery and vein; sciatic nerve, whole or already split into common peroneal and tibial nerves
- Nerve lies on adductor magnus higher up, on the femur lower down
- It can divide anywhere, so the block may be sciatic or separate tibial and common peroneal blocks

### Sciatic nerve
- Sacral plexus L4-S3: common peroneal (lateral) and tibial (medial) parts in one sheath
- Leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below piriformis, deep to gluteus maximus, on the back of the ischium
- Crosses obturator internus and gemelli; between quadratus femoris (in front) and gluteus maximus (behind), about midway between ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter
- Below the buttock: on adductor magnus, crossed superficially by biceps femoris from medial
- Runs down the middle of the back of the thigh and splits at the apex of the popliteal fossa

### Ultrasound technique
- Contraindications, consent, full monitoring, IV access, resuscitation drugs, block trolley, skilled assistant
- Position: prone, lateral (block side up), or supine with knee flexed at 90 degrees and heel supported or leg on a pillow, leaving room for the probe
- Full asepsis; 50-100mm block needle; high-frequency linear probe transverse at the popliteal crease
- Find popliteal artery and vein (colour Doppler); nerve sits between semimembranosus and biceps femoris
- Foot dorsiflexion and plantarflexion rocks the nerves (seesaw sign); nerves are anisotropic, so tilt the probe distally
- Tibial nerve: larger hyperechoic oval, superficial and medial to the artery; common peroneal: smaller, superficial and lateral
- Scan up as the tibial nerve moves lateral and joins the common peroneal to form the sciatic
- Skin wheal, needle in-plane from lateral, tip inside the sheath
- Single injection on the sciatic, or two injections after the split (may miss the sural communicating nerve)

### Wrong-side block
- Contributing factors
  - Organisational: emergency, after hours, block done outside theatres (remote, little help), culture
  - Surgical: no site mark, or mark hidden after positioning
  - Anaesthetic: fatigue, distraction, time-out skipped, poor communication between anaesthetist, patient and nurse, repositioning for a 2nd block without a repeat time-out, little supervision, junior anaesthetist
  - Patient: too unwell to point out the wrong side
- Management
  - Open disclosure and agree the anaesthetic: repeat block on the correct side (if within LA dose limit), spinal (if suitable) or GA
  - Watch for LAST
  - Afterwards: formal meeting with patient and family, explain and apologise; follow up for block complications (nerve injury, infection); offer outpatient review; document; discuss at departmental critical incident review

## Compartment syndrome (lower leg)
- Pressure in a fascial compartment rises (swelling, bleeding) above capillary pressure, causing ischaemia of muscle and nerve
- Normal tissue pressure 0-10mmHg
- Capillary flow at risk above 20mmHg
- Muscle and nerve ischaemia risk above 30mmHg, though may be tolerated (delta pressure = DBP minus compartment pressure)
- Injury after 4hrs of complete ischaemia, irreversible over the next 4 hrs: local rhabdomyolysis and neurapraxia, progressing to axonotmesis
- Complications: gangrene and amputation, ischaemic contracture, rhabdomyolysis with renal failure
- Diagnosis
  - High-risk injury; pain out of proportion; pain on passive stretch
  - Paraesthesia, hypoaesthesia; paralysis (late); pulselessness; pallor; perishingly cold
  - Tense, woody compartment
  - X-ray for underlying fracture; compartment pressure measurement (e.g. Stryker STIC)

## Ankle block
| Nerve | Sensory area | Landmark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tibial | Heel and sole | Midway between medial malleolus and calcaneum tip; inject behind the posterior tibial artery |
| Superficial peroneal | Dorsum of foot except the 1st/2nd toe web | From the tibial ridge towards the lateral malleolus, subcutaneous wheal |
| Deep peroneal | 1st/2nd dorsal toe web | 2-3cm below the intermalleolar line; dorsalis pedis artery lies lateral to extensor hallucis longus tendon; inject either side |
| Sural | Lateral foot, 5th toe, plantar 4th/5th toe web | Subcutaneous between lateral malleolus and Achilles tendon |
| Saphenous | Medial ankle and foot | From the saphenous vein in front of the medial malleolus, subcutaneous back to the Achilles tendon |

- Advantages: good analgesia; may avoid GA in high-risk patients; simple with low LAST risk; minimal motor block, so suits bilateral surgery
- Disadvantages: uncomfortable if unsedated; does not cover tourniquet pain; vascular puncture and haematoma (saphenous vein especially)

## Anterior abdominal wall and TAP block
### Innervation
- Anterior rami T7-T12 and L1 run between internal oblique and transversus abdominis; cutaneous branches pierce the rectus sheath
- T7-9 above the umbilicus; T10 around it; T11-12 with iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal below it
- Iliohypogastric (L1): inguinal area
- Ilioinguinal (L1): hernia sac, scrotum or labia, medial thigh

### Uses
- Covers the abdominal wall (not visceral pain) T7-L1, but spread above the umbilicus is unreliable: best for incisions below the umbilicus
- Abdominal hysterectomy, open myomectomy, caesarean section, retropubic prostatectomy, open appendicectomy, lower colorectal surgery, laparoscopic surgery
- Benefits: part of multimodal analgesia; fewer opioids (useful in OSA, lung disease); alternative when neuraxial is contraindicated, e.g. coagulopathy (less effective); emergency caesarean without time for neuraxial

### Ultrasound technique
- Contraindications, consent, monitoring, IV access, resuscitation drugs, block trolley, assistant
- Supine; full asepsis; 50-100mm block needle
- High-frequency linear probe (curvilinear if obese) transverse in the mid-axillary line between ribs and iliac crest
- Skin wheal if awake; needle in-plane from lateral, tip between internal oblique and transversus abdominis
- Aspirate, 1-2mls to see the layers separate and spread; then 20mls dilute LA (e.g. 0.25% bupivacaine, max 2mg/ml) with repeated aspiration
- Bilateral blocks for midline or crossing incisions

### Landmark technique
- Triangle of Petit: iliac crest below, posterior edge of external oblique in front, anterior edge of latissimus dorsi behind
- 50mm block needle usually enough
- Perpendicular to skin just above the iliac crest in the posterior axillary line
- Two pops (external then internal oblique fascia); aspirate and inject

### Complications
- Failure (variable spread)
- LAST (large volumes, especially bilateral)
- Wrong placement (much less with ultrasound): intraperitoneal, liver, bowel perforation, haematoma, kidney
- Infection and intravascular injection (rare)
- Transient femoral nerve block

## Coeliac plexus block
- Anatomy
  - Bilateral; preganglionic sympathetic fibres from greater (T5-T9), lesser (T10-T11) and least (T12) splanchnic nerves
  - Converge in front of T12 and enter the abdomen behind the crura
  - Retroperitoneal; anterolateral to L1; in front of aorta and crura; either side of the coeliac artery origin; medial to the IVC; behind stomach and pancreas
- Supplies liver, gallbladder, stomach, small bowel, large bowel to the splenic flexure, pancreas, spleen, kidneys and adrenals
- Indications: intractable pain from these organs, e.g. pancreatic cancer, chronic pancreatitis, gastric cancer
- Approaches: posterior, anterior, retrocrural or transcrural, transaortic, transdiscal, paramedian
- Complications
  - Retroperitoneal haematoma (aorta or IVC injury)
  - Intravascular injection (check with contrast)
  - Paraplegia from phenol in spinal cord arteries; direct cord or nerve injury
  - Retrocrural phenol spread injuring nerve roots; intrathecal or epidural injection
  - Visceral puncture, abscess or cyst (stomach, kidney); psoas injection with cyst or abscess
  - Pneumothorax, chylothorax
  - Sexual dysfunction (phenol along sympathetic chain)
  - Hypotension (upper abdominal vasodilation); warm legs

## Ultrasound for nerve blocks
### Image generation
- Piezoelectric crystal expands and contracts as voltage polarity alternates, emitting 2.5-15MHz waves
- Waves reflect at interfaces of differing acoustic impedance
- Same crystal receives: returning waves deform it, producing a voltage that builds the image

### Image quality
- Attenuation: energy lost through tissue, worse at higher frequency; caused by
  - Divergence of the beam
  - Refraction: beam hitting an interface at other than 90 degrees bends, causing artefact
  - Reflection: at impedance interfaces (impedance = density x speed of sound); makes the image, but strong superficial reflection leaves less for deep structures
    - Acoustic shadowing: no image beyond a strong reflector such as bone
    - Post-cystic enhancement: brighter signal deep to fluid, which transmits easily
  - Scattering: objects the size of the wavelength or smaller
  - Absorption: becomes heat
- Resolution (axial, lateral, temporal): higher frequency improves axial; focusing improves lateral
- Anisotropy: appearance depends on beam angle; image fades as the probe-to-skin angle becomes more acute

### Needling
| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In-plane | Whole needle seen; tip seen near the nerve | Hard to keep the whole needle in view; longer path, more pain and possible damage |
| Out-of-plane | Familiar landmark entry points; shorter path; less painful (no muscle) | Needle seen only as a bright dot; tip-to-nerve distance harder to judge |

## Collapse during a block and LAST
- Causes of sudden collapse
  - Patient: MI, malignant arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism
  - Anaesthetic: LAST, high or total spinal from intrathecal or epidural injection, tension pneumothorax, vascular injury with haemorrhagic shock
- Features of LAST
  - CNS: tinnitus, perioral numbness, heavy tongue, metallic taste, agitation, restlessness, confusion, motor excitation, seizures, coma
  - CVS: early hyperdynamic (tachycardia, hypertension, arrhythmias); conduction problems (long PR, T wave changes, bradycardia, asystole); worsening hypotension; collapse
  - Airway: obstruction, loss of reflexes, respiratory distress
- Mechanism
  - Intravascular injection, or too much LA absorbed too fast
  - Sodium channels held in the inactive state
  - Heart: direct depression, re-entrant arrhythmias, VF
  - Brain: inhibitory interneurons blocked first (excitation), then all neurons depressed (coma, apnoea)
- Immediate treatment
  - Stop injecting; declare LAST, call senior help, alert theatre team, get the LAST kit; ABCDE
  - Airway and breathing: check SpO2; 100% O2, bag-mask with oral airway if needed; intubate if apnoea, respiratory distress or GCS less than 8; avoid hyperventilation (lowers seizure threshold); hypoxaemia and acidosis worsen toxicity
  - Circulation: large-bore IV access, ECG, NIBP; CPR if arrest
  - Lipid: Intralipid 20% 1.5ml/kg over 1 min, then 15ml/kg/hr infusion
  - If still unstable: repeat bolus after 5 mins (up to 3 doses total), raise infusion to 30ml/kg/hr; upper limit 12ml/kg
  - Arrhythmias per BCLS/ACLS, but avoid beta blockers, calcium channel blockers and lidocaine
  - Consider cardiopulmonary bypass for refractory arrest
  - Seizures: midazolam 0.5-1mg IV boluses, lorazepam 0.1mg/kg IV (max 4mg, repeat once), or propofol 10-20mg boluses (avoid larger doses, which depress the heart)
  - If intubated, give a muscle relaxant to cut O2 use and acidosis during seizures; EEG to detect ongoing seizures
  - ICU monitoring for at least 12hrs (LAST may persist or recur)

## Flashcards
- Q: Where does the dural sac end in infants, and where does the cord end?
  - A: Dural sac S3-4; cord L3-4
- Q: Caudal volumes of 0.125-0.25% bupivacaine for sacral, lumbar and low thoracic levels?
  - A: 0.5mls/kg, 1ml/kg and 1.25ml/kg
- Q: Phrenic palsy rate with 20mls versus 5mls interscalene LA?
  - A: About 100% versus 25-50%
