# Interscalene Block

Brachial plexus block at the roots/trunks between the scalene muscles, for shoulder, upper arm and lateral clavicle surgery; the superior trunk block is a more distal, potentially phrenic-sparing variant.

## Distribution of block
- Covers the shoulder, upper arm and lateral two thirds of the clavicle
- Proximal and superficial spread may also block the supraclavicular branches of the cervical plexus (skin over the acromion and clavicle)
- Inferior trunk (C8-T1) usually spared unless the injection is more distal in the plexus
  - Not suitable for lower arm and hand surgery
  - Ulnar (C8-T1) territory can be reached with a larger volume (e.g. 15-20 ml) and a low interscalene injection, between the interscalene and supraclavicular levels

## Indications
- Shoulder and upper arm surgery
- Clavicle surgery (combined with a cervical plexus block)

## Equipment
- Linear transducer (8–14 MHz)
- 50mm needle
- Local anaesthetic: 7–15 mL
- Nerve stimulator optional: 0.5 mA, 0.1 msec

## Positioning
- Supine, beach chair or semilateral decubitus; head turned away from the side to be blocked
- Slight head-up tilt: more comfortable, better venous drainage, less prominent neck veins
- Pillow under the neck and contralateral shoulder (ipsilateral shoulder left free) lifts the site and gives more room posteriorly
- Ask the patient to reach for the ipsilateral knee to drop the shoulder

## Ultrasound anatomy
- Linear probe transverse at the cricoid (C6), near the sternocleidomastoid
  - About 3–4 cm above the clavicle, over the external jugular vein
- Find the carotid artery, then scan laterally to the anterior and middle scalene muscles
- Plexus lies lateral to the carotid and internal jugular vein, between anterior and middle scalenes
  - Looks like a "traffic light"
  - Depth typically 1–2 cm
- Sternocleidomastoid and the superficial cervical plexus lie superficial to it
- Vessels to avoid: vertebral artery, transverse cervical artery (plus suprascapular and inferior thyroid arteries from the thyrocervical trunk), dorsal scapular artery
  - Vertebral artery is medial to the roots at C7; it leaves the subclavian and enters the transverse foramen at C6 (not visible above C6)
- Phrenic nerve runs on the anterior scalene
- Chassaignac tubercle: the prominent anterior tubercle of the C6 transverse process; C7 has a deficient anterior tubercle
- Traceback alternative: find the subclavian artery at the base of the neck (plexus lateral to it), then follow the plexus upward until it sits between the scalenes
- If the interscalene view is hard, start at the supraclavicular fossa (plexus superficial and posterior to the subclavian artery) and slide cephalad

## Technique
- Skin local anaesthetic, then in-plane, usually lateral to medial
  - Lateral approach protects the phrenic nerve (anterior to the anterior scalene)
  - But the dorsal scapular and long thoracic nerves leave the roots and pass through the middle scalene, so can be hit on the way in
- A "pop" is often felt through the prevertebral fascia
- Aim for the space between anterior and middle scalenes, and between roots rather than at them
- 7–15 mL usually gives a fast, reliable block
- Avoid high injection resistance: intraneural injection can track proximally into the spinal canal
  - Needle touching the plexus always gives high opening pressure (> 15 psi): withdraw slightly
- Motor response (shoulder, arm or forearm) confirms placement but is not required
  - If seen below 0.5 mA, withdraw slightly before injecting (possible intraneural tip)
- Check spread: after 5–7 mL, follow the plexus down to the supraclavicular fossa with the needle held still; correct-compartment injection shows clear spread there (e.g. after 10 mL the nerves lateral to the subclavian artery are surrounded and look deeper)

## Anatomical variants
- C6 and C7 often split proximally: do not inject between fascicles of one root (intraneural risk); inject between C5 and C6, superficial to C5 or deep to C6
- C5 may run partly through the anterior scalene: follow it distally until it enters the groove

## Landmark technique
- Draw a line laterally from the cricoid (C6 transverse process level)
- Palpate the interscalene groove
- Plexus is superficial: needle usually only 1–2 cm deep

## Superior trunk block
- More distal than the interscalene, so further from the phrenic nerve: potentially phrenic-sparing
- Suprascapular nerve supplies 70% of shoulder joint innervation and arises from the superior trunk
- Positioning and starting probe position as for interscalene
- Slide distally until the suprascapular nerve is seen leaving the superior trunk
  - Or follow C5 and C6 caudally until they join as the superior trunk, ideally above the suprascapular branch
- Target is just before the suprascapular nerve branches off
- In-plane, lateral to medial
  - Tip posterior/inferior to the trunk: 10 ml
  - Redirect anterior/superior (still lateral to the trunk): 5 ml

## Complications
- Respiratory: hemidiaphragmatic paralysis from ipsilateral phrenic nerve block
  - Caution in severe respiratory disease (e.g. uncontrolled COPD)
  - Level 1a evidence: almost universal with traditional high volumes (20 ml or more), including continuous infusions
  - Not a problem with normal lung function, but limits use in those who would gain most (poor respiratory reserve, elderly, obese)
  - Lower dose and volume cuts the incidence of phrenic block and hemidiaphragmatic paresis to between 13 and 45%
  - Bilateral interscalene blocks are contraindicated
- Horner's syndrome from sympathetic block (ptosis, anhidrosis, miosis, enophthalmos, nasal congestion)
- Hoarseness (laryngeal hyperaemia or recurrent laryngeal nerve block)
- Epidural spread or total spinal from injection into a root's dural sleeve
- Vertebral artery injection: seizures
- Direct needle injury to the cervical spinal cord
- Bezold-Jarisch reflex in shoulder surgery: sitting position reduces venous return, leading to bradycardia, hypotension and even cardiac arrest
- Bleeding
- Pneumothorax

## Phrenic nerve blockade
- Phrenic nerve lies just superficial to the interscalene groove at the cricoid level, running caudad and anteriorly over the anterior scalene
- Common after interscalene block; can compromise breathing in pre-existing lung disease
- Check with M-mode ultrasound: low-frequency curvilinear probe under the rib cage in the anterior axillary line, watching hemidiaphragm movement
- Options when phrenic block is a concern
  - Lower volume (e.g. 5 mL): less phrenic block but possibly shorter analgesia and lower success; 10 mL at the cricoid level still blocks the phrenic
  - Lower interscalene injection, around C7
  - Superior trunk block
  - Combined suprascapular and axillary nerve block (CSANB)
  - Supraclavicular block (spread to suprascapular and axillary nerves may be insufficient)
- Persistent phrenic nerve palsy
  - Cause unclear; probably partly inflammation and nerve entrapment rather than direct needle trauma
  - Cervical spine disease may contribute
  - Most reported patients were male, overweight or obese and middle-aged

## Continuous interscalene block
- Steps: (1) place needle; (2) inject local anaesthetic to confirm tip position and open the space; (3) advance catheter; (4) inject via catheter under ultrasound to confirm position; (5) secure catheter
- Ultrasound guides the first two steps
- Needle usually in-plane lateral to medial, under the prevertebral fascia; out-of-plane or caudad-aimed approaches also possible
- Motor response of deltoid, arm or forearm (0.5 mA, 0.1 msec) can confirm placement; then give 4–5 mL to open the space and ease catheter insertion
- Advance catheter 2–3 cm into the interscalene space (one operator or with an assistant)
- Confirm tip by watching the catheter or injecting through it; if unclear, inject 1 mL of air
- Tunnelling: consider in older or obese patients, loose neck skin, or longer planned infusions
  - Downsides: dislodgement while tunnelling, scarring
- Securing devices help stabilise the catheter
- Stimulating and non-stimulating catheters both work; stimulating ones can prompt needless manipulation because a motor response may be absent even when well placed
- Catheter-over-needle technique has been reintroduced as an option

## Flashcards
- Q: Which trunk does an interscalene block usually spare?
  - A: The inferior trunk (C8-T1), so it is unsuitable for lower arm and hand surgery
- Q: Usual local anaesthetic volume for a single-shot interscalene block?
  - A: 7–15 mL
- Q: Incidence of hemidiaphragmatic paresis with reduced-volume interscalene blocks?
  - A: Between 13 and 45% (almost universal with 20 ml or more)
- Q: What share of shoulder joint innervation comes from the suprascapular nerve?
  - A: 70%
- Q: Volumes for a superior trunk block?
  - A: 10 ml posterior/inferior to the trunk, then 5 ml anterior/superior
