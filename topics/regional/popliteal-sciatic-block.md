# Popliteal Sciatic Block

Sciatic nerve block above the knee, ideally inside Vloka's sheath where the tibial and common peroneal nerves are just separating; covers the leg below the knee except the medial (saphenous) strip.

## Distribution of anaesthesia
- Motor and sensory block of the **leg below the knee, except the medial leg and foot** (saphenous nerve, a branch of the femoral nerve)
  - Combine with a **saphenous or femoral nerve block** for full cover
- Hamstring motor fibres are spared; fibres to the back of the knee joint are blocked

## Indications
- **Foot and ankle** surgery, Achilles tendon surgery

## Goal
- Probe **transverse over the popliteal fossa**, then moved **upwards and slightly lateral**
- Lateral and posterior approaches are most common; they differ in position and needle path but are otherwise alike
- Aim to inject inside the **common sciatic (Vloka's) sheath**, which wraps the **tibial nerve (TN) and common peroneal nerve (CPN) where they meet**
  - Alternatively, block TN and CPN **separately**
- With nerve stimulation (0.5 mA, 0.1 msec), touching either branch usually gives a calf or foot twitch

## Equipment
- Linear probe
- 50-100mm needle (e.g. **80mm Pajunk**)
- Local anaesthetic 15–20 mL

## Lateral approach: positioning
- **Lateral** or supine
  - Lateral: patient **faces the operator**, **knee flexed**, **pillow between the knees**
  - Supine: foot on a raised footrest
- With nerve stimulation, expose the calf and foot to see twitches

## Lateral approach: ultrasound anatomy
- **Linear probe transverse at the popliteal crease**, then moved **up and slightly lateral**
- Find the **popliteal artery** (colour Doppler if needed), about 3–4 cm deep
  - **Popliteal vein** lies just **superficial** (posterior) to the artery
  - **Biceps femoris** lateral; semimembranosus and semitendinosus medial
- **Tibial nerve: superficial and lateral** to the vessels
  - Bright, round or oval, honeycomb appearance
  - Tilting the probe towards the feet usually helps it stand out from fat
  - Ankle dorsiflexion and plantar flexion make the two branches twist and move against each other
- **CPN sits slightly more superficial and lateral** than the tibial nerve
- **Slide proximally** until TN and **CPN join** as the sciatic nerve; see the nerve **both before and after the split**
  - Block where TN and CPN **are starting to separate** but are **still inside Vloka's sheath**
  - The junction is usually **5–10 cm above the popliteal crease**, but can be right at the crease or, less often, higher in the thigh
  - Sciatic nerve usually 2–4 cm deep
  - Moving proximally, the vessels get deeper and harder to see

## Lateral approach: needle insertion
- **In plane** from the **lateral thigh**, entering **2–3 cm anterior (deeper) to the lateral edge** of the probe (**estimate nerve depth on ultrasound first**), and **advance horizontally**
- Place the tip **inside Vloka's sheath**, between the **two components**, ideally where they are **slightly separated by fat**, giving a **safe gap for the needle**
  - Injecting around each branch separately also works but is less common
- Inject 1–2 mL first to confirm: local anaesthetic should **spread within the sheath and push TN and CPN apart**; then give the rest
  - Correct spread: local anaesthetic **tracks proximally and distally around both divisions**, visible inside the sheath above the injection point
  - One injection is usually enough

## Tips
- Anatomy varies; TN and CPN **divide at an inconsistent distance above the crease**
- In obese patients, **press with the probe to push the nerve laterally** and shorten the needle path
- **Moving the ankle makes the nerves "dance"**, helping identification
- At about 5 cm above the crease the TN and CPN have typically just started to diverge, lying lateral and superficial to the popliteal vessels

## Posterior approach
- In plane (from lateral or medial) or out of plane
- Needle enters from the **back of the knee**
  - Prone: a medial in-plane approach is an option if the branches lie the other way, to place the needle between them
- Out of plane advantage: needle passes through **skin and fat rather than muscle, so it hurts less**
- Can be done lateral or prone

## Nerve stimulator techniques
- Larger volumes (e.g. > 40 mL) were used to improve success
- Ultrasound lowers the volume needed, as injection can stop once spread is adequate

## CAPS block (crosswise approach to popliteal sciatic)
- Ultrasound-guided alternative for the **supine** patient
- Curvilinear probe
- In plane or out of plane

## Continuous popliteal sciatic block
- Aim: catheter inside the sciatic sheath in the popliteal fossa
- Thread the catheter 4-5 cm past the needle tip; confirm by seeing local anaesthetic spread in the sheath
- Secure with tape or tunnel it
- Lateral approach suits catheters better than prone:
  - Biceps femoris holds the catheter and reduces dislodgement (prone sites sit in mobile subcutaneous tissue)
  - The side of the thigh moves less than the back of the knee with knee movement
  - Easier access to the catheter site
- Common starting infusion: ropivacaine 0.2% at **5 mL/h** plus a patient bolus of 5 mL every 60 minutes

## Flashcards
- Q: Which area is not covered by a popliteal sciatic block?
  - A: Medial leg and foot (saphenous nerve); add a saphenous or femoral block
- Q: Where is the ideal injection point?
  - A: Inside Vloka's sheath where TN and CPN are starting to separate, usually 5–10 cm above the popliteal crease
- Q: Typical volume for a single-shot popliteal block?
  - A: 15–20 mL
- Q: Where does the tibial nerve lie relative to the popliteal vessels?
  - A: Superficial and lateral; CPN is slightly more superficial and lateral still
- Q: Common starting catheter infusion?
  - A: Ropivacaine 0.2% at 5 mL/h with a 5 mL patient bolus every 60 minutes
