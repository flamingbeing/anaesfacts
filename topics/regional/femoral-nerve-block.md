# Femoral Nerve Block

Block of the femoral nerve at the femoral crease, deep to the fascia iliaca and lateral to the femoral artery, for knee, anterior thigh and hip fracture analgesia.

## Distribution
- Anterior and medial thigh down to and including the knee
- Variable strip of skin on the medial leg and foot
- Joint supply: hip, knee and ankle

## Indications
- Surgery on the knee, anterior thigh and medial lower leg
  - e.g. femur, patella, quadriceps tendon and knee surgery
- Analgesia for hip fracture

## Anatomy
- Femoral triangle boundaries
  - Superior: inguinal ligament
  - Lateral: medial border of sartorius
  - Medial: medial border of adductor longus
  - Roof: fascia lata (cribriform fascia at the saphenous opening), skin and subcutaneous tissue
  - Floor: iliacus, psoas major, pectineus and adductor longus, with their fascia
- Femoral triangle contents
  - Femoral nerve, under the fascia iliaca
  - Femoral sheath: femoral artery and branches, femoral vein and tributaries (including the long saphenous vein), femoral canal (lymphatics and deep inguinal nodes, including the node of Cloquet)
- The femoral sheath is a downward extension of abdominal fascia behind the inguinal ligament: transversalis fascia in front of the vessels, iliac fascia behind
  - So the femoral nerve lies outside the sheath, deep to the fascia iliaca

## Equipment
- High-frequency linear probe, 8–18 MHz (the nerve is superficial)
- 10–15 mL of local anaesthetic is enough
- Nerve stimulator (if used): 0.5 mA, 0.1 msec; quadriceps twitch as the needle passes the fascia iliaca and reaches the nerve

## Ultrasound-guided technique
- Position
  - Supine, ipsilateral leg slightly abducted
  - Bed flat, groin exposed for good access; in obesity, tape the abdominal fat back to expose the femoral crease
- Scanning
  - Probe transverse on the femoral crease over the femoral pulse
  - Slide lateral to medial to find the femoral artery
  - If the deep artery of the thigh is also seen, move proximally until only the femoral artery is visible
  - Nerve lies lateral to the artery: hyperechoic, triangular or oval, at a depth of 2–4 cm
  - Trace the nerve to where it divides (sometimes above the inguinal ligament) and block proximal to this so both anterior and posterior divisions are covered
  - Trace it up and down to confirm it is not a lymph node
  - Identify iliacus, fascia iliaca and fascia lata: injecting under the wrong fascia causes failure
  - Fascia lata is most superficial; fascia iliaca lies directly over the nerve, wraps it and separates it from the artery
- Needle
  - In-plane, lateral to medial preferred
    - Pros: whole needle and tip visible (out-of-plane from directly above risks nerve puncture); nerve is the most lateral structure, so vessels are avoided
    - Cons: longer path, may need a longer needle; less convenient for a catheter
  - Target: needle tip just lateral to the nerve, in the wedge-shaped space lateral to the artery, below the fascia iliaca
  - A "pop" may be felt as the needle passes the fascia iliaca
- Injection
  - Correct spread: the nerve is pushed away by injectate, or local anaesthetic spreads above or below the nerve, separating it from the fascia iliaca
  - Aspirate carefully (artery is close); ultrasound may also reduce arterial puncture
  - Never inject against high resistance: may mean intrafascicular tip or wrong fascial plane
  - Full circumferential spread is not needed; a pool next to the posterolateral or anterior surface is enough

## Tips
- If the nerve is hard to see, tilt the probe proximally or distally to separate it from iliacus and fat
- Find the femoral vein with light probe pressure and colour Doppler if needed
  - Usually medial to the artery, but can be deep or lateral
  - Often compressed by the probe: release pressure and recheck vessels before injecting
- Heavy probe pressure compresses tissue, makes injection harder and may limit spread between fascial layers
- After hip arthroscopy, fluid extravasation can push the artery and nerve much deeper

## Landmark technique
- Supine
- Inguinal ligament: line from the anterior superior iliac spine to the pubic tubercle
- Palpate and mark the femoral artery on this line
- Insert the needle 1–2 cm lateral to the pulse
- Aim for a quadriceps twitch

## Continuous femoral nerve block
- Goal: catheter near the nerve, just deep to the fascia iliaca
- Five steps
  - (1) needle placement
  - (2) injection through the needle to confirm the plane
  - (3) catheter advancement, 2–4 cm past the needle tip
  - (4) injection through the catheter to confirm position
  - (5) securing the catheter
- Ultrasound helps with the first two steps in most patients
- Mobile groin and shallow nerve make dislodgement likely
  - A more lateral entry point leaves more catheter in iliacus; muscle holds a catheter better than fat
- Typical adult regimen: ropivacaine 0.2% at 5 mL/h with 5 mL/h patient-controlled bolus
- With ultrasound, use non-stimulating catheters
  - Stimulating catheters take longer, do not improve analgesia, and prompt needless manipulation when a well-placed catheter gives no twitch
  - Confirm position by local anaesthetic spread in the right space, not by motor response

## Complications and aftercare
- Marked quadriceps weakness: no walking until sensation has fully returned
- Intravascular injection or femoral artery puncture
- Nerve injury (intrafascicular injection)

## 3 in 1 block
- Same site as femoral block, with a larger volume and pressure applied distally to push spread cranially to the lateral femoral cutaneous and obturator nerves
- Rarely reaches the obturator nerve; better called a 2 in 1 block
- Outdated term

## Flashcards
- Q: Where does the femoral nerve lie at the femoral crease?
  - A: Lateral to the femoral artery, deep to fascia lata and fascia iliaca, at 2–4 cm depth
- Q: Volume of local anaesthetic for a single-shot femoral block?
  - A: 10–15 mL
- Q: Landmark needle entry point?
  - A: 1–2 cm lateral to the femoral pulse on the ASIS to pubic tubercle line
- Q: Typical continuous femoral infusion in adults?
  - A: Ropivacaine 0.2% at 5 mL/h plus 5 mL/h patient-controlled bolus
- Q: Why is the 3 in 1 block better called 2 in 1?
  - A: It rarely blocks the obturator nerve
