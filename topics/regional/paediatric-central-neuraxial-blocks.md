# Paediatric Central Neuraxial Blocks

Principles, local anaesthetic pharmacology and toxicity management for spinal, epidural and caudal blocks in children.

## Options
- Caudal epidural
- Lumbar or thoracic epidural
- Spinal (subarachnoid) block

## Why use them
- Increasingly popular in the UK because of:
  - Better understanding of neonatal and child pain and its long-term effects
  - Wish to avoid opioid side effects in vulnerable children
  - Better postoperative infusion devices
  - Growing evidence of safety

## Asleep vs awake
- Blocks are normally done under general anaesthesia, unlike in adults
  - Early signs of neurotoxicity are masked under anaesthesia, but children under 8 years old do not reliably report toxicity symptoms even when awake
  - No evidence of more complications
  - Relies on monitoring, which must be on before the block
  - Block extent can only be tested once awake, though the response to surgical stimulus gives a clue
- Awake blocks still used for:
  - Ex-premature neonates
  - Muscle diseases with poor tolerance of general anaesthesia
  - Others, e.g. malignant hyperthermia susceptibility

## Contraindications
- Patient or parent refusal
- Systemic sepsis
- Local sepsis at the insertion site
- Coagulopathy
- Some neurological diseases (relative)
- Local anaesthetic allergy

## Technique
- Young infants may have a higher threshold for LA neurotoxicity but a lower one for cardiotoxicity
- Resuscitation drugs and equipment immediately available wherever blocks are done, awake or asleep
- Consent for every block, covering common and serious risks; verbal consent acceptable if documented in detail

## Local anaesthetic pharmacology in children
- Account for age-related differences in pharmacology and physiology
- Central blocks may need relatively large LA volumes
- Nerve sheaths are thin and myelination is incomplete until about 2 years
  - Immature nerves are more sensitive; weaker solutions than in adults give a dense block
- Neonates: amide elimination half-life at least double the adult value (larger volume of distribution, maybe lower clearance)
- Protein binding:
  - α-1 acid glycoprotein binds LA with high affinity; albumin binds more drug in total, but weakly
  - Acidosis lowers binding and raises the free (toxic) fraction
- For low-dose blocks, choice of LA is personal preference
- Use conservative doses or a less toxic single enantiomer when there is hepatic impairment, risk of intravascular injection or rapid absorption, or a long infusion planned
- Under 6 months:
  - Immature hepatic amide metabolism and low α-1 acid glycoprotein raise free drug levels
  - Neonatal dose is half the adult maximum, for boluses and infusions; somewhat arbitrary but keeps the same safety margin on current pharmacokinetic data
- Over 6 months: same mg/kg dose as adults

## Individual local anaesthetics
- Bupivacaine
  - Potent amide
  - Hepatic metabolites are slightly active but far less toxic than the parent drug
  - Longer half-life than in adults; may accumulate with infusions
  - Infusion of 0.1% or 0.125% usually gives good postoperative analgesia
- Levobupivacaine
  - S(-) enantiomer of bupivacaine
  - Equipotent but less toxic than racemic bupivacaine
  - Not fully studied; treated as behaving like bupivacaine
- Ropivacaine
  - Single-isomer amide
  - Similar potency to bupivacaine, less toxic
  - Peak levels 30-115 min after injection, perhaps from its local vasoconstriction
    - Vasoconstriction makes it unsuitable for blocks in areas with end-arterial supply
  - Maximum dose 2.5 mg/kg
- Prilocaine
  - Avoid under three months: higher risk of methaemoglobinaemia
  - Maximum dose 5 mg/kg

## Local anaesthetic toxicity
- In children often presents as cardiovascular collapse
- Initial treatment per APLS guidelines
- ECG: T-wave or ST-segment changes
- Bretylium is no longer available for ventricular arrhythmias
- Drugs that can be used:
  - Magnesium: 50 mg/kg over 15 min, then 25 mg/kg/hour
  - Phenytoin: 5-10 mg/kg IV
  - Amiodarone: 5 mg/kg IV over 3 min, then 5-15 mcg/kg/min
  - Intralipid 20%: 1 mL/kg over 1 min, repeated every 3-5 min, then 0.25 mL/kg/min
- Follow the local departmental protocol for doses
