# Peripheral Nerve Block Technique

General steps for any peripheral nerve block, by landmark or ultrasound, plus troubleshooting.

## Exam outline: describing a block
- Consent
- Stop Before You Block: confirm side and site
- Preparation (SEA-MIR-GA):
  - Sterile procedure: hand wash, sterile gloves, sterile dressing pack
  - Equipment: ultrasound, needle, local anaesthetic (LA)
  - Assistant able to help with regional or general anaesthesia
  - Monitoring to AAGBI minimum standard
  - IV access
  - Resuscitation drugs and equipment at hand
  - General anaesthesia: drugs and equipment ready to convert if needed
- Position the patient and expose the area
- Clean and drape
- State probe type and transducer position
- Name the structures to identify on ultrasound
- LA to skin
- Needle length, insertion site, in-plane or out-of-plane and direction
- After negative aspiration for blood, inject 1-2 ml, watching for high resistance, paraesthesia and correct spread
- Continue slowly in 5 ml aliquots, aspirating between each
- Reposition the needle as needed and inject further volume with the same checks
- Remove the needle and dress the site (e.g. spray film dressing)

## General technique (steps)
- Step 0: monitoring
  - Explain risks and benefits, as for neuraxial blocks
  - Standard monitors; sedation with IV opioid and/or benzodiazepine if wanted
  - In a separate block room, keep monitors, equipment and drugs close in case of complications (e.g. LA toxicity seizure)
- Step 1: prepare equipment
- Step 2: position the patient
- Step 3: asepsis; sterile skin preparation
- Step 4: landmark the transducer position or needle insertion point
- Step 5: identify the correct needle position by one of three methods
  - Paraesthesia: shock-like radiating sensation when the needle touches or nears a nerve; withdraw slightly before injecting, as injecting into the paraesthesia can cause pain and permanent nerve injury
  - Nerve stimulation: motor response appears as the stimulating needle nears the nerve
  - Ultrasound: shows nerves and surrounding structures
- Step 6: aspirate; no blood
- Step 7: inject; no paraesthesia
- Watch for immediate complications:
  - Bleeding or haematoma
  - Injury to nearby structures (e.g. pneumothorax, bowel perforation)
  - LA toxicity

## Ultrasound probes
- Frequency trade-off:
  - High frequency for shallow structures
  - Low frequency reaches deeper structures but with poorer resolution
- Frequency by target depth:
  - <2cm: 12-15MHz (e.g. wrist, ankle blocks)
  - 2-4cm: 10-12MHz (e.g. interscalene, supraclavicular, axillary brachial plexus, femoral, TAP blocks)
  - 4-10cm: 5-10MHz (e.g. infraclavicular, popliteal, subgluteal sciatic, pudendal, gluteal sciatic, lumbar plexus blocks)
  - >10cm: 3-5MHz (e.g. anterior approach to sciatic, coeliac ganglion block)
- Probe shapes:
  - Linear
  - Curvilinear (convex): wider field of view, used for abdominal scanning
  - Phased array: small footprint for narrow windows such as the temporal bone (transcranial) or between ribs (cardiac)
- Other controls: time-gain compensation, focus
- Probes used for nerve blocks:
  - Linear, high frequency (5–13 MHz): superficial; suits most blocks
  - Hockey stick, high frequency (7-15MHz): superficial, small footprint, narrower field (ankle, elbow blocks)
  - Curvilinear, low frequency (2-5MHz): deep, wide field (sciatic block)

## Needles
- Echogenic needles have an etched, cross-hatched shaft that scatters more of the beam back to the probe

## Imaging the nerve
- Short-axis or long-axis view
- Nerves look round, oval or triangular; hyperechoic or hypoechoic
  - Above the diaphragm: tend to be hypoechoic
  - Below the diaphragm: tend to be hyperechoic
  - Described as honeycomb (multifascicular), cluster of grapes or snowman
- Slide the probe along the nerve (cephalad-caudad) to confirm what it is
- Optimise the image:
  - Tilt or fan the probe until its face is perpendicular to the target, for maximum returning echoes
  - Rotate
- Pre-block scan: plan a needle path clear of vessels, pleura and periosteum
  - Colour Doppler separates vessels from other structures

## Needle insertion
- In-plane or out-of-plane
- The closer the needle runs parallel to the probe face, the brighter it appears
  - Entering further from the probe gives a shallower angle and a better image, at the cost of a longer tissue path
  - Heeling: press the probe edge opposite the needle side to align the probe face with the needle
- Keep the shaft and tip in view at all times while advancing
  - In-plane tip: double echo (step appearance) from the bevel, unlike the single echo of the shaft
  - Out-of-plane: needle shows only as a bright dot at any point along it, so tip and shaft are hard to tell apart; tilt the probe and the last dot seen is the tip
- Ways to find the needle:
  - Tissue movement: watch tissues move as it advances; jiggle in small in-out movements
  - Hydrolocation: inject <1ml of dextrose or LA and watch where it spreads
- Cross fascia slowly and wait for a pop or give
  - Approach fascia lying against a nerve tangentially so the needle does not spear the nerve when the fascia gives
- Hydrodissection: inject fluid on purpose to separate tissue planes

## Injecting local anaesthetic
- Aim to surround the nerve while avoiding intravascular and intraneural injection
- Aspirate, then inject 1–2 mL
  - Watch for pain or high injection pressure
  - Watch for spread at the needle tip; no visible spread suggests the tip is out of plane or inside a vessel
  - Confirm adequate distribution
- Aim for circumferential spread ("doughnut" or "halo"): move the needle and give two to three smaller aliquots if needed
  - Also speeds block onset
  - Not always needed: where fascial planes hold the LA against the nerve (interscalene groove, femoral triangle), one position on one side is enough
- Immobile needle technique for large-volume plexus and major nerve blocks
  - One operator holds the needle; a second aspirates and injects through the extension tubing
  - Less risk of needle displacement and intravascular injection

## Catheters for continuous blocks
- Hard to see on ultrasound as they coil within tissue sheaths
  - Tip visible if only a short length is passed beyond the needle
  - Once 3–5 cm past the tip, needle, nerve and catheter never lie in one plane
- Best check of tip position: watch the spread of injectate
  - Inject 1mL through the catheter
  - Tilt or slide the probe to find a bright dot (catheter in cross-section)

## Troubleshooting
- No distinct pops: withdraw, recheck landmarks, change angle or entry point
- Bone contact: too deep
- Blood on aspiration: remove the needle and press on the site for 2 minutes; be alert for LA toxicity
- Resistance to injection: do not inject
  - Withdraw slightly; the tip may be in muscle or, worse, inside the nerve
- Pain on injection:
  - Mild local burning is normal; inject more slowly
  - Severe pain or paraesthesia is not normal: stop injecting
- Signs of LA toxicity (circumoral numbness, tinnitus, dizziness, seizure):
  - Stop injecting, call for help, high-flow oxygen, life support as needed
- No pain relief after 30 minutes:
  - Give a further 20 ml of low-concentration LA, or consider other analgesia

## Workflow
- Day before:
  - Book the ultrasound machine
  - Syringe pump may be needed for sedation
- Before the block:
  - Bring the patient early
  - Assess and examine as for general anaesthesia
  - Start the anaesthetic record and monitoring as for general anaesthesia
  - Sedation if needed
- Block:
  - Switch on the ultrasound and prepare the trolley
  - Position the patient
  - Put on sterile gloves while the nurse opens the block set
  - Skin preparation
  - Lidocaine (lignocaine) skin wheal; sterile cover (e.g. Tegaderm) over the probe
  - Landmark, then scan for the nerve
  - Insert the needle while an assistant injects
  - Clear away all equipment

## Flashcards
- Q: What probe frequency suits a target 2-4cm deep, and which blocks?
  - A: 10-12MHz; e.g. interscalene, supraclavicular, axillary, femoral, TAP.
- Q: How does the in-plane needle tip look different from the shaft?
  - A: A double echo (step) from the bevel; the shaft gives a single echo.
- Q: Injecting LA but no spread is seen at the tip. What does that suggest?
  - A: The tip is out of plane or inside a vessel.
- Q: What is hydrolocation?
  - A: Injecting <1ml of dextrose or LA to show where the needle tip is.
- Q: Blood on aspiration: what do you do?
  - A: Remove the needle and press on the site for 2 minutes; watch for LA toxicity.
