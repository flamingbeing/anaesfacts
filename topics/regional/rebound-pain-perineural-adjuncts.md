# Rebound Pain and Perineural Adjuncts

Pain that surges as a dense block wears off, and the drugs added to local anaesthetic to make single-shot blocks last longer.

## Rebound pain
- Acute postoperative pain once effective regional anaesthesia wears off, as the surgical pain is unmasked
- Seen in up to 40-50% of patients
- Most common after techniques that give complete analgesia and surgical anaesthesia
- Risk factors: bony surgery, preoperative pain or opioid use, young women
- Little evidence it is caused by hyperalgesia; patients are generally still satisfied with regional anaesthesia

## Prevention
- Warn patients before surgery what to expect as the block wears off and surgical pain starts
- Prescribe regular multimodal analgesia; counsel day-case patients on taking it
- Non-drug measures such as elevating the limb
- In selected patients: catheter techniques or perineural adjuncts

## Perineural adjuncts: general
- An alternative to continuous catheters for prolonging single-shot blocks
- Possible harms: drug errors from mixing, longer motor block, side effects, neurotoxicity

## Dexamethasone
- Can be given perineurally or IV
- Mechanism (proposed):
  - Glucocorticoid receptor stimulation increases inhibitory K+ channel expression and reduces nerve excitability and conduction
  - Systemic anti-inflammatory effect
- Perineural use:
  - Use a preservative-free preparation
  - May be incompatible with ropivacaine because dexamethasone is alkaline
  - Can disturb blood glucose
  - Ceiling effect at about 4mg

## Dexmedetomidine (alpha-2 agonist)
- Blocks hyperpolarisation-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN) channels, which normally restore the neurone's resting potential
- Effect does not depend on alpha-2 receptors, as peripheral nerve axons lack them

## Adrenaline
- One of the oldest adjuncts
- Speeds onset, prolongs the block and slows systemic absorption, lowering the risk of local anaesthetic systemic toxicity
- Can be neurotoxic at higher concentrations

## Magnesium
- Can be neurotoxic
