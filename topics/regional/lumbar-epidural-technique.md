# Lumbar Epidural Technique

Step-by-step lumbar epidural insertion: preparation, midline and paramedian approaches, loss of resistance, catheter checks and ultrasound guidance.

## Preparation
- Stop oxytocin augmentation first
  - Pain and distress make insertion harder
  - Adds to uterine hyperstimulation from intrathecal fentanyl
- Check
  - Allergies; medications such as antiplatelets and anticoagulants
  - FBC: platelet trend
  - BMI: may be difficult; may need ultrasound or long needles
  - Obstetric: pre-eclampsia
  - Cardiovascular disease, e.g. aortic stenosis
  - Brain or spine lesions, e.g. CNS lesion, scoliosis, lumbar nerve root compression (ask about existing numbness)
- Consent
  - If too distressed to consent, call a senior
    - Likely technically hard (moving target)
    - Consider: epidural in lateral position; single-shot spinal with 27G pencil-point needle; non-neuraxial analgesia

## Equipment
- Tuohy needle and stylet, with or without a CSE set
- Multi-orifice closed-tip catheter with clip and filter
- Low-resistance syringe for loss of resistance (LOR)

## Positioning
- Sitting: easier to find the midline, more flexion
- Lateral: more comfortable
- In labour, sitting makes external fetal monitoring difficult
- Scoliosis
  - Direct the needle into the curve
  - Use ultrasound
  - If possible, insert below the curve

## Landmarks
- Tuffier's (intercristal) line: L4/5 or body of L4
  - In pregnancy it crosses higher (L3-4) because the pelvis rotates forward, so the chosen level is often higher than expected
- Mark the L3/4, L4/5 or L2/3 interspaces
- Unlike a spinal, an epidural can be sited at any interspace
- Spinous processes hard to feel or curved spine (obesity, previous back surgery)
  - Optimise position
  - Consider ultrasound
  - Morbid obesity: consider a long needle

## Midline approach
- Scrub; skin prep with antiseptic
- Infiltrate skin with local anaesthetic
  - The LA needle can act as a finder needle for bony landmarks, especially in obesity
  - Do not go too deep: risk of accidental dural puncture (ADP), especially in thin patients
- Insert the epidural needle, midline or paramedian
- Find the space by loss of resistance or hanging drop

### Loss of resistance (preferred: continuous LOR with saline)
- Syringe of saline (preferred) or air on the needle; constant pressure on the plunger while advancing
- Supraspinous, interspinous ligaments and ligamentum flavum resist the plunger; entering the epidural space gives sudden loss of resistance (like a good IV cannula)
- Glass syringe with 2-3ml saline; may add 0.1-0.3ml air (in ligamentum flavum the bubble compresses without the saline injecting)
- Saline versus air
  - Saline: fewer dural punctures (perhaps pushes the dura away)
  - Air: case reports of pneumocephalus, cord or nerve root compression, patchy block (air pockets)
- Continuous versus intermittent
  - Air is compressible, so continuous LOR is hard
  - Continuous LOR stops the needle the moment LOR occurs: less overshoot and dural puncture

### Hanging drop
- Drop of saline on the needle hub stays put through the ligaments
- Sucked into the needle on entering the negative-pressure epidural space

### Advancing the needle
- Advance mm by mm, styletted, bevel cephalad
  - The stylet prevents coring and keeps debris or tissue plugs out (which would hide CSF after an ADP)
- Feel of the tissues
  - Supraspinous ligament grips the needle; a wobbly or laterally drifting needle is off midline: withdraw and redirect
  - Interspinous ligament feels gritty
  - Ligamentum flavum: more resistance again (air bubble compresses) before the LOR
  - Absolute resistance: likely bone (vertebral arch); forcing hurts (periosteum)
  - Once in ligamentum flavum with incompressible saline, consider advancing by plunger pressure alone
- Labour
  - Harder because of pain and increased lordosis
  - Raised intra-abdominal pressure (contractions) is transmitted to the epidural space; advancing during a contraction risks dural puncture
- Depth to ligamentum flavum is usually 4 to 6 cm in normal-sized adults; varies widely; may increase with the saline injected
- Needle markings are not numbered: count marks visible between skin and hub and subtract from the needle length
  - e.g. 4 marks visible on a 9cm needle (common length) gives a depth of 5 cm

### Problems during needle insertion
- Floppy needle or lateral angle: off midline, supraspinous ligament missed
  - Reassess midline, redirect
- Bone at < 2 cm: spinous process, or poor flexion
  - Find the interspace again; aim for its caudal part
- Bone at ≥ 4 cm: entry too lateral, lamina
  - Redirect towards midline
- Bony resistance throughout: ossified ligaments, arthritic spine
  - Consider paramedian approach
- Warm clear fluid from the needle: possible CSF, accidental dural puncture
  - Tell CSF from saline or LA
  - If CSF, replace the stylet at once
  - Consider intrathecal catheter or resiting at another interspace

## Catheter insertion
- Thread about 3-5 cm past the needle tip
  - <3 cm: may migrate out of the space
  - >7 cm: more unilateral block, venous puncture and knotting
- Once threaded past the Tuohy tip (past 12cm), never pull the catheter back through the needle (shearing); remove needle and catheter together
- Cannot advance catheter: narrow space, needle near dura, blocked orifice, false LOR
  - Predistend with fluid
  - Rotate the bevel
  - Advance the needle slightly
  - Resite at another interspace

## Checking position
- Aspirate the catheter: no blood or CSF
- Consider a test dose of lidocaine with or without adrenaline 1:200,000
- Safe labour epidural practice
  - Initial aspiration for blood and CSF
  - Test dose
  - Incremental injections, especially top-ups
  - Continuous watch for LA toxicity and high block
  - A comfortable patient on a dilute infusion with no motor block almost certainly has an epidural catheter (intravascular gives poor analgesia; intrathecal gives dense motor block)

### Test dose
- Aims to detect intrathecal or intravascular placement
- Some argue it is unnecessary because labour solutions are very dilute and aspiration is usually diagnostic
- But aspiration is not always predictive (especially single-orifice catheters), and concentrated LA for operative delivery is dangerous
- Typical: 40-60mg lidocaine (2-3ml 2%) (Chestnut); no motor block in 3-5 min (and no high sensory level or hypotension) means probably not intrathecal
- Practice varies: 3-20 mg bupivacaine or 15-90 mg lidocaine
- Adrenaline 1 in 200,000 as an intravascular marker (heart rate rises); the trend is to avoid it (ATOTW)
  - Aspiration of a multi-orifice catheter is up to 98% sensitive for intravascular placement
  - Adrenaline is less reliable in labour: confused with contraction tachycardia
- Treat every epidural dose (initial or top-up) as a test dose: catheters can migrate intrathecally or intravascularly later

### Problems after threading
- Blood in catheter: epidural vein cannulation (lateral entry, engorged veins)
  - Withdraw 1–2 cm and flush with saline
  - Resite if blood persists
  - Consider siting in the lateral position
- Pain or paraesthesia on threading: catheter near a nerve root
  - Withdraw to < 6 cm in the space (2–3 cm for short surgical cases)
  - Resite if pain persists

## Finishing
- Give the initial bolus of epidural drugs
- Haemostasis and secure the catheter
  - Opsite spray
  - Clear occlusive dressing so the site can be inspected
  - Gauze for bleeding if needed
  - Loop the catheter to reduce dislodgement
  - Commercial locking devices exist

## Paramedian approach
- Larger opening into the epidural space than midline
- Useful when the patient cannot be positioned or flex, with calcified ligaments, or with spinal deformity (kyphoscoliosis, prior lumbar surgery)
- Technique
  - Find the interspace by landmarks, palpation or ultrasound
  - Skin wheal about 1 cm lateral and 1 cm caudal to the lower edge of the upper spinous process
  - Needle 15° off the sagittal plane, angled to midline, tilted cephalad
  - Feels different: misses supra- and interspinous ligaments; passes through paraspinous tissue with little resistance before ligamentum flavum
  - Bone (likely lamina): redirect cephalad and medially

## Ultrasound-guided epidural
- Advantages
  - Finds midline and anatomy when landmarks are abnormal or impalpable (obesity, scoliosis, previous spinal surgery)
  - Measures depth to epidural or intrathecal space
  - Accurate level identification
- Technique
  - Find the sacrum and count to the target interspace in the parasagittal oblique (interlaminar) view
  - Rotate to the transverse interlaminar or interspinous view; optimise the posterior complex
- Ultrasound-assisted midline approach
  - Mark the midpoint of the probe's long edge (midline) and short edge (interspace); where the lines cross is the insertion point
  - Then proceed as usual

## Flashcards
- Q: How far should an epidural catheter be threaded into the space?
  - A: About 3-5 cm; <3 cm risks migration out, >7 cm risks unilateral block, venous puncture and knotting
- Q: What is a typical epidural test dose?
  - A: 40-60mg lidocaine (2-3ml 2%); no motor block in 3-5 min suggests not intrathecal
- Q: Usual depth from skin to ligamentum flavum in an adult?
  - A: 4 to 6 cm
- Q: Where is the skin entry for a paramedian epidural?
  - A: About 1 cm lateral and 1 cm caudal to the lower edge of the upper spinous process, needle 15° off sagittal
- Q: How sensitive is aspiration of a multi-orifice catheter for intravascular placement?
  - A: Up to 98%
