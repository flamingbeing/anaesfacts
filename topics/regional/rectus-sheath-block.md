# Rectus sheath block

Local anaesthetic on the posterior rectus sheath blocks the terminal intercostal branches that supply the skin around the umbilicus; done on both sides of the midline.

## Anatomy
- Targets the ends of the 9th 10th, and 11th intercostal nerves
- They run between internal oblique and transversus abdominis, pierce the back of rectus abdominis and end as anterior cutaneous branches to the periumbilical skin

## Indications
- Analgesia after umbilical hernia repair and other umbilical surgery
- Umbilical port of laparoscopic surgery

## Landmark technique
- Blind technique is the most described: through the anterior sheath and rectus muscle, injecting on the posterior sheath
- Supine; entry 2–3 cm from the midline, just above the umbilicus, at the top of the rectus bulge
- Short-bevel 5 cm needle at right angles to the skin
  - Feel the anterior sheath resistance and a clear pop through it
  - Advance to the firm resistance of the posterior sheath
- Depth of the posterior sheath correlates poorly with age, weight or height, so depth is hard to predict
- Inject 15–20 ml in 5 ml aliquots
- Repeat on the other side

## Ultrasound-guided technique
- Probe transverse, just lateral to the umbilicus or at the linea semilunaris, where the posterior sheath is best seen
- Aim: spread between rectus muscle and posterior rectus sheath
- In-plane or out-of-plane
- Volume: 10-30 mL per side in adults; 0.1 mL/kg per side in children

## Complications
- Peritoneum lies just deep to the posterior sheath
  - Intraperitoneal injection: block fails, risk of bowel perforation
  - Vessel puncture, usually inferior epigastric vessels
- Incomplete block from anatomy: in up to 30% the anterior cutaneous branches form before the sheath and never pierce its posterior wall
