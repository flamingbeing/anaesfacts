# Labour Analgesia

Neuraxial analgesia is the gold standard; aim for good analgesia with minimal motor block using dilute local anaesthetic plus opioid, with non-neuraxial options when it is unavailable or contraindicated.

## Stages of labour
- First stage: from onset of contractions to full cervical dilatation
  - Latent phase: up to 3-4cm
  - Active phase: 3-4cm to 10cm
- Second stage: from full dilatation to delivery of the baby
- Third stage: from delivery of the baby to delivery of the placenta

## Mechanism of labour pain
- Pain is mainly in the first and second stages
- First stage: visceral pain from cervical dilatation and uterine contractions
  - Nerve roots T10-L1 (visceral afferents)
- Second stage: increasing somatic pain from pelvic floor, vagina and perineum
  - Nerve roots S2-4 (pudendal nerve), possibly L1-2 (genitofemoral nerve)
- Aim for a T10-L1 band early in labour to cover contractions and dilatation; give larger volumes near delivery to cover the perineum

## Aims of labour analgesia
- Good analgesia with no or minimal motor block
  - Bupivacaine below 0.125%, with or without opioid, reduces motor block
- Avoid instrumental or operative delivery
  - Higher local anaesthetic concentrations may increase instrumental delivery; use low concentrations
  - Adding opioid lowers the local anaesthetic concentration needed

## Non-pharmacological options
- Uses: maternal preference, contraindication to or unavailability of other methods
- Advantages: easy to use, minimal side effects
- Disadvantages: weak evidence for many; some are costly or time consuming
- Options
  - TENS
  - Relaxation and breathing techniques (e.g. Lamaze); may work by supraspinal pain modulation
  - Heat or cold packs, water immersion
  - Hypnosis, massage, acupuncture, aromatherapy

### TENS
- First stage: electrodes over T10-L1 dermatomes, about 2cm either side of the spinous processes
- Second stage: second set over S2 – S4 dermatomes
- Woman controls the current
- Proposed mechanisms: A-fibre stimulation blocking pain transmission, local beta-endorphin release
- No better than placebo in trials, but very few side effects; reasonable when other methods are contraindicated or unavailable

## Inhalational analgesia
### Nitrous oxide (Entonox)
- Entonox: 50 % nitrous oxide in oxygen
- Onset within 20-30 seconds of inhaling; peak effect after about 45 seconds
- Advantages
  - Useful, especially where other options are limited
  - Easy to use, no doctor supervision needed
  - Little accumulation with intermittent use
  - Self-administered, giving some control
- Disadvantages
  - Drowsiness, disorientation; brief loss of consciousness (0.4% after prolonged use)
  - Nausea
  - Incomplete analgesia for many women, though helps most significantly

### Volatile agents
- Several have been inhaled intermittently
- Limited by difficulty with safe delivery and scavenging

## Systemic opioids
- Used: morphine, fentanyl, pethidine (meperidine), hydromorphone, remifentanil; mixed agonist-antagonists (butorphanol, nalbuphine)
- Pethidine: usually IM 1mg /kg
- Advantages
  - Useful first-line option, or when other methods are unavailable or contraindicated
  - Easy to give
- Disadvantages (side effects)
  - Crosses the placenta: neonatal respiratory depression; tell the paediatricians if given in advanced labour
    - Fetal plasma level peaks 2-3 h after maternal IM dose
  - Sedation
  - Dose-dependent respiratory depression
  - Delays gastric emptying; increases gastric volume in labour
- IV fentanyl helps very distressed women who cannot keep still for a neuraxial block
- Benzodiazepines (midazolam) have been used for anxiolysis

### Opioid PCA
- Useful when neuraxial analgesia is unavailable or contraindicated
- Remifentanil PCA: faster onset, no accumulation; bolus 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg with 2 minute lockout used successfully
- Advantages: gives the woman control, which improves satisfaction
- Disadvantages
  - Needs PCA equipment and nurse training
  - Neonatal respiratory depression; maternal respiratory depression and other side effects
    - Monitor mother and baby during labour and after delivery; adjust settings
    - Mother may need supplemental oxygen
  - Hard to match peak opioid level to contractions, so analgesia can be suboptimal

## Regional techniques
- Gold standard for labour analgesia
- Neuraxial (epidural, CSE, spinal): best analgesia; works in both first and second stages
  - Caudal: rarely used now that epidural catheters exist
- Pudendal block: safe, excellent for second-stage somatic pain, but rarely done
- Paracervical block: first stage only; risks fetal bradycardia and death, so largely abandoned

## Indications for labour epidural
- Maternal request
  - Most effective analgesia, minimal toxicity risk
- Preparation for surgery, to reduce need for GA (catheter can be topped up quickly for caesarean section)
  - Expected operative delivery: malpresentation or breech, multiple pregnancy, VBAC, fetal heart rate changes during attempted vaginal delivery
  - High risk for GA: high BMI, difficult airway, history of MH
  - Expected anaesthetic for postpartum haemorrhage
- Maternal disease where sympathetic stimulation could harm mother or fetus (epidural gives sympatholysis)
  - Pre-eclampsia (without severe thrombocytopenia or coagulopathy) and other hypertensive disorders of pregnancy
  - Some cardiac disease, e.g. regurgitant valves
  - Some neurological disease, e.g. AV malformations, space-occupying lesions

## Choosing epidural, CSE or single-shot spinal (ATOTW)
- GA risk factors (high BMI, difficult airway): early plain epidural so it is working if caesarean section is needed
  - Reassess often; low threshold to replace a poorly working catheter
- Expected difficult insertion (high BMI, spinal disorders): early plain epidural to allow time
- Early labour, little pain: plain epidural sitting (CSE also possible)
- Early labour, distressed, drowsy from Entonox: CSE in lateral position, with or without rapid single-shot spinal or IV opioid first
- Fully dilated multiparous woman with previous rapid uneventful labour: consider single-shot spinal in lateral position
  - CSE possible, though the epidural part is unlikely to be needed
  - Sitting if she can keep still

## Contraindications
- Coagulopathy
- Thrombocytopenia: platelet cutoff 80; look at the trend too
- Existing neurological deficit: warn that numbness may worsen

## Complications of neuraxial labour analgesia
### Common (1 in 20)
- Inadequate analgesia
  - About 70% effective
  - 1 in 10 need adjustment (e.g. pulling catheter back)
  - 1 in 20 need resiting
  - Consistently better analgesia than non-epidural methods, though not always higher satisfaction
- Nausea, shivering, pruritus: ~ 1:10
  - Nausea 20-40%, vomiting 5-15% (hypotension, opioids)
  - Shivering 10 – 50%
  - Pruritus 40% intrathecal, 2% epidural (opioids)
- Fever (epidural-related hyperthermia): 1 deg C rise over 7 – 10 hours
  - Intrapartum hyperthermia 20% with epidural, 5% without
- Delayed gastric emptying (opioid)
- Urinary retention (opioid and local anaesthetic)

### Uncommon (1 in 100)
- Large needle dural puncture leading to PDPH (1 in 100)
  - PDPH after spinal 1.5-2%
  - Accidental dural puncture with Tuohy 1.5%, of which 50% get PDPH (specific to epidurals)
- Hypotension responsive to treatment (1 in 50)
- Temporary high block that may need intervention
- Fetal heart rate changes on starting the epidural, especially bradycardia
- Respiratory depression (opioid) 1:5000

### Rare to very rare (1 in 10,000)
- Neurological injury (traumatic, infective, ischaemic, chemical)
  - Mild, temporary (numb patch, weak leg): 1 in 20000
  - Mild, permanent: 1 in 80000
  - Epidural abscess: 1 in 50,000
  - Epidural haematoma: 1:200,000
  - Severe injury including paralysis: 1:250,000
  - Obstetric trauma often injures the lumbosacral trunk: a peripheral nerve distribution suggests obstetric cause; a dermatomal pattern suggests the block
  - Multiple attempts and needle manipulations raise the risk of infection, haematoma and nerve damage
- Severe local anaesthetic complications: 1:10,000
  - Accidental IV injection and LAST
  - Accidental intrathecal epidural dose: total spinal
  - Local anaesthetic allergy and anaphylaxis

### Other
- Temporary back pain at the insertion site, but not long-term back pain
- May prolong labour and increase instrumental delivery
- May mask uterine rupture

## Controversies
### Effects on labour
- Slightly prolongs the second stage; may increase oxytocin use
  - Avoiding epidurals in early labour for fear of slowing it has been largely abandoned
- More instrumental deliveries in meta-analysis
  - COMET trial (2001): 25% more operative vaginal deliveries with traditional bupivacaine 0.25% top-ups than with low-dose local anaesthetic plus opioid
- No increase in caesarean section rate in large RCTs
- Use low-dose techniques for the best chance of spontaneous vaginal delivery

### Effects on mother and fetus
- Fetal heart rate changes, especially bradycardia, at initiation
  - Possible causes: uterine hyperstimulation (rapid analgesia drops maternal adrenaline, leaving noradrenaline unopposed, which increases uterine tone) and placental hypoperfusion (from hyperstimulation or maternal hypotension)
  - No consistent difference in neonatal arterial pH or APGAR scores
- Epidural-related hyperthermia
  - Not caused by infection; probably multifactorial
    - Altered thermoregulation: more shivering, less sweating and hyperventilation due to sympathetic block; warm labour rooms worsen it
    - Non-infectious inflammation with raised interleukin-6 (IL-6); the epidural may itself trigger it
  - Confounders
    - Women with long labours or subclinical chorioamnionitis are more likely to ask for epidurals
    - Systemic opioids in non-epidural groups are antipyretic
  - Size of rise relates to labour duration (1 deg C rise over 7 – 10 hours); early insertion may increase risk
  - No treatment
  - Main problem: fever raises concern for intrapartum infection, leading to unnecessary tests and antibiotics for mother and baby
- Back pain
  - Short-term local soreness only; well-powered RCTs show no link with long-term back pain
  - Almost 50% of women report back pain 6 months after delivery; 28% have it for the first time postpartum

## Consent in labour
- Elements: autonomy (legal basis), full disclosure, capacity, no duress
- Pain does not remove the need for full consent
- Full disclosure is hard with a woman in severe pain and distress
- Capacity may be questioned with pain, fatigue, anxiety and stress
  - Severe pain may not impair intellectual function; recall afterwards may be affected
- She may be sedated from earlier drugs (IM pethidine, Entonox)
- Best to discuss antenatally (e.g. information leaflet)
  - She can revoke earlier advance decisions about labour analgesia
- For a severely distressed woman requesting analgesia, proceeding without further discussion may be justified on beneficence, but beware paternalism
  - She may also decline further discussion

## Plain epidural
### Drugs
- Ropivacaine 1%: usually 20ml ampoules, sometimes 10ml
- Bupivacaine 0.5%
- Use 2% lidocaine (preservative free) for neuraxial use, not 1%

### Initiation
- Test dose: lidocaine 40mg (with or without adrenaline)
  - Lidocaine 40 mg: 2% 2ml + saline 2ml, giving 1% 4ml
  - Check for motor block (intrathecal) and perioral numbness or tingling (intravenous)
- Loading (activation) dose once the test dose confirms correct placement, for faster onset
  - Ropivacaine 12 mg diluted to 8-10ml (0.15-0.12%)
  - With or without fentanyl 50 mcg
  - With or without morphine 3 mg
- Epidural initiation concentrations
  - Ropivacaine 0.1-0.2%
  - Bupivacaine 0.0625-0.125%
  - Lidocaine 0.75-1%
  - Fentanyl 50 mcg

### Maintenance solutions
- Dilute ropivacaine with low-dose fentanyl
  - Ropivacaine preferred to bupivacaine (less cardiotoxic)
  - Low-dose mixtures give the best chance of spontaneous vaginal delivery
  - Epidural opioid is synergistic with local anaesthetic and reduces the dose needed
  - Fentanyl preferred: poorly lipid-soluble opioids (e.g. morphine) cause delayed respiratory depression
- Recipes
  - Ropivacaine 0.2% + fentanyl 2 mcg/ml (100ml): 76ml saline + 20ml 1% ropivacaine + 4ml fentanyl
  - Ropivacaine 0.2% + fentanyl 3 mcg/ml (100ml)
  - Ropivacaine 0.16% + fentanyl 1.6 mcg/ml (124ml): 100ml saline + 20ml 1% ropivacaine + 4ml fentanyl; most popular as easiest to make
  - Ropivacaine 0.13% + fentanyl 2 mcg/ml (150ml): 124ml saline + 20ml 1% ropivacaine + 6ml fentanyl
- 0.16% is a good balance; 0.13 is rather dilute and 0.2 rather strong; some units go as low as 0.1

### Maintenance options
- Single shot through the Tuohy: risk of a large volume without a test dose
- Intermittent top-ups: labour intensive, no pump needed
- Continuous epidural infusion (CEI), with or without patient-controlled boluses
  - Usually 8-12ml/h (up to 15ml/h), titrated to block height
  - Start at 10ml/hr unless block is high
  - Needs only an infusion pump
- Programmed intermittent bolus (PIB)
  - Wider spread with smaller doses than CEI
  - Needs a dedicated device
- Patient-controlled epidural analgesia (PCEA)
  - Better analgesia and fewer clinician interventions
    - Dose matches pain as labour progresses
    - Gives the woman control; may improve satisfaction
    - Fewer clinician top-ups
    - Less total local anaesthetic and opioid
  - Needs a dedicated device, may be costly
  - Women need teaching to use it well

### Fluids
- Hartmann's bolus 1L, then infusion 3L/day, to reduce hypotension
  - Reduce infusion to 100ml/hr (2.4L a day) in gestational diabetes

## Problems during epidural placement
### Large needle dural puncture
- Rapid outflow or spurting of clear CSF from the Tuohy hub (saline from loss of resistance only drips slowly)
- Replace the stylet and call a senior
- Option 1: re-attempt at another level
  - Harder epidural
  - Epidural drugs may pass through the hole into the intrathecal space
- Option 2: intrathecal catheter
  - May trigger fibrosis
- Option 3: non-neuraxial analgesia

### Paraesthesia on spinal needle insertion (CSE)
- Spinal needle next to or in a nerve root
- Stop advancing at once; it should resolve
- Remove the stylet; if CSF flows and paraesthesia has gone, consider giving the intrathecal dose
- If it persists or recurs:
  - Remove spinal needle and do a plain epidural (if the epidural space was hard to find), or
  - Remove the Tuohy (if the space was easy to find) and re-attempt angled away from the side of paraesthesia

### No CSF on spinal needle (CSE)
- Tuohy not midline, or level too low
- Adjust spinal needle depth
- Reconfirm loss of resistance; do not advance the Tuohy
- If confident in the epidural space, or it was hard to find: thread the catheter without the spinal dose (plain epidural)
- If it was easy and she is not distressed: repeat the Tuohy insertion (adjust to midline or use another interspace)

### Cannot thread catheter
- Keep firm pressure while threading
- Reconfirm loss of resistance
- Injecting saline first to open the space is controversial and may spread the block higher
- If still impossible, remove the Tuohy and re-attempt
- Do not rotate the Tuohy
- Do not pull the catheter back alone if ≥ 12 cm mark is at the hub stabiliser: risk of shearing; withdraw needle and catheter together

### Paraesthesia on catheter insertion
- Stop advancing; it should resolve
- If transient, continue threading
- If persistent or recurrent, remove the Tuohy and re-attempt angled away from that side
- Inject nothing until it has fully resolved

### Pain on injection (intrathecal or via catheter)
- Possible intraneural injection
- Stop injecting at once, remove the spinal needle, consider resiting the epidural if a catheter is in

### Blood in the catheter
- Catheter tip has entered an epidural vein
- Withdraw slowly; if blood still aspirates at 3 cm in the space:
  - Withdraw to no less than 2 cm in the space (if the space was hard to find), or
  - Re-attempt (if it was easy) at another site or angle; flush the catheter clear of blood before reinserting
- Recheck for blood after securing
- Reducing the risk of a bloody tap
  - Midline approach
  - Leave no more than 4cm of catheter in the space
  - Thread between contractions
  - Pre-injecting saline (controversial)
- Detecting blood
  - Aspirate gently with a 3ml syringe
  - Consider aspirating at the connector with the filter removed (never inject without the filter)
  - Lower the catheter end below the insertion site and watch for free flow

## Breakthrough pain and epidural failure
- Incidence 9-12% (ATOTW)
- Causes (ATOTW)
  - Altered anatomy (e.g. previous spinal surgery)
  - Inadequate maintenance dose
  - Catheter migration
  - Catheter lateral in the epidural space: unilateral or patchy block
  - Sacral sparing in the second stage
  - Patient expectations

### Assessment
- Labour progress; occiput posterior position; exclude dangerous causes (e.g. uterine rupture)
- Severity of pain
- Site: contraction pain versus back or perineal pain
- Sensory level (T10-S4), Bromage score
- Skin marking of the catheter (especially if unilateral or no improvement after the first top-up)

### Management
- Insufficient height
  - Causes: intrathecal dose worn off, inadequate dose, catheter migration
  - Bolus local anaesthetic and increase the infusion rate
  - Check catheter marking if level does not change after the first top-up
- Patchy block (adequate height, missed segments)
  - Causes: catheter lateral, in a foramen, or subdural (tell a senior early if haemodynamically unstable)
  - Withdraw catheter by 1cm first
  - Bolus concentrated local anaesthetic, with or without fentanyl
  - Consider PIB
- Unilateral block
  - Causes: catheter lateral or in a foramen
  - Bolus with the unblocked side down
  - Withdraw catheter by 1cm
- Perineal pain
  - Cause: labour progressing with sacral sparing
  - Warn it is hard to treat
  - Sit up
  - Epidural fentanyl 25-50mcg
- Other options
  - Entonox
  - Resite: if analgesia is still inadequate despite the above, and if more than two clinician boluses are needed (ATOTW)
- If pain score is >4 after 30 mins (typically after 1-2 boluses), inform the senior

### Top-up doses
- Lidocaine 1-2%: <5ml per bolus, <10ml in 30min
  - Or ropivacaine 5ml bolus from the pump, or bupivacaine 0.125%
- Fentanyl 25-50mcg
- Aspirate for blood first; look for signs of intrathecal migration and systemic toxicity
- Reassess 10-15 mins after each top-up

### Specific patients
- Obese women need less epidural analgesic
  - Smaller epidural space from raised intra-abdominal pressure, so more cephalad spread
  - More epidural fat increases local absorption
- Occiput posterior position causes more back pain

## Spinal analgesia in labour
- Advantages over epidural
  - Faster onset
  - More reliable, less sacral sparing
  - Technically easier and quicker
  - No large needle dural puncture
  - Less risk of epidural haematoma
- Disadvantages
  - Limited duration; a single-shot spinal may be enough when labour will be short (e.g. grand multipara, advanced dilatation)
  - More transient hypotension (rapid sympathectomy)
  - More fetal bradycardia (hypotension or uterine hyperstimulation)
- Continuous spinal with opioid alone suits women who cannot tolerate sympathectomy
  - Standard catheters need large needles: unacceptable PDPH rate
  - Microcatheters via small needles cause less PDPH but had unacceptable neurological complications, probably from local anaesthetic neurotoxicity rather than the catheters themselves
- Technique
  - Aim for T10
  - A true saddle block misses the uterus; use larger doses and keep her sitting for no more than 30 seconds to reach about T10

## Combined spinal-epidural (CSE)
- Find the epidural space by loss of resistance low in the lumbar spine and hold the Tuohy steady
- Pass a long, fine, pencil-point spinal needle through the Tuohy until a "pop" is felt
- Confirm CSF and give the intrathecal drugs
- Remove the spinal needle and thread the epidural catheter

### Intrathecal dose
- Ropivacaine 3 mg plain
  - e.g. 0.3ml of 1% diluted to 1.5-2ml
- With or without fentanyl 15 mcg
- With or without morphine 0.1 mg
- Spinal initiation doses
  - Ropivacaine 2-3.5 mg
  - Bupivacaine 1.25-2.5 mg
  - Lidocaine: not used
  - Fentanyl 15-25 mcg

### Epidural component
- Test dose: lidocaine 40 mg (2% 2ml + saline 2ml, giving 1% 4ml), as above
  - The test dose concept is flawed in CSE
- Then maintenance as for epidural

## Single-shot spinal then epidural
- Fast, effective analgesia, and easier positioning for the epidural

## Dural puncture epidural (DPE)
- Deliberate dural puncture with a spinal needle as in CSE, but no intrathecal drugs
- Better analgesia than epidural alone: epidural drug passes through the dural hole into the intrathecal space
- Avoids the sudden haemodynamic changes of intrathecal local anaesthetic
