# Complications of Neuraxial Blocks

Complications of spinal and epidural blocks, grouped by cause: needle or catheter, the block itself, drug toxicity and neurological injury.

## Overview
- Immediate complications of spinal to watch for
  - Hypotension from sympathetic block
  - High or total spinal
  - Intravascular injection and LA toxicity
- Complications to discuss when consenting
  - Nausea, shivering, itch
  - Post-dural puncture headache (PDPH)
  - Hypotension, myocardial infarction, stroke (sympathetic block, high or total spinal)
  - Bleeding or haematoma at the insertion site
  - Local infection or abscess
  - Nerve injury
  - Block failure and conversion to GA
- Risk of major complications with permanent harm after any neuraxial block: 1 in 25000 to 50000 (NYSORA, from NAP3)

## Needle or catheter complications
- Post-dural puncture headache: see PDPH topic
- Back pain
  - Temporary, from needle insertion: mild, low back, rarely radiates to the legs, lasts only a few days
  - No evidence that neuraxial anaesthesia permanently worsens existing back pain; incidence in adults same as after GA
  - Backache is common in pregnancy and often persists afterwards
- Sheared epidural catheter
  - Can happen when a catheter is pulled back through a Tuohy needle; avoid doing this
- Misplaced epidural needle or catheter
  - Subcutaneous: no block height or analgesia, local tissue swelling
  - Subdural (see below)
  - Intrathecal: total spinal
  - Intravenous: LAST

## High or total spinal
- Can lead to respiratory and cardiac arrest
- E.g. after unrecognised intrathecal placement

## Hypotension
- Sympathetic block causes vasodilation: lower preload and lower SVR
- Fall in BP thought to be slower and smaller with epidural than with spinal at similar sensory levels
- Risk factors
  - Elderly
  - Hypovolaemia or other preload-dependent states (existing, or ongoing blood loss)
  - High thoracic sensory level (e.g. larger dose)
  - Combined GA and neuraxial
- Awake presentation: light-headedness, altered mental state, nausea and vomiting (often the first sign), signs of high spinal (dyspnoea, voice change, bradycardia)
- Prevention
  - Cycle BP every 1 min (q1min)
  - Coloading (rapid fluid immediately after the spinal) beats preloading; if preloading, colloid beats crystalloid
  - Prophylactic phenylephrine; but it lowers cardiac output, can cause bradycardia and hypertensive crisis, and IV phenylephrine may lower block height
  - Lower LA dose, slower titration; aim for block height below T10 for lower limb surgery
- Management
  - Run fluids fast: restore venous return and cardiac output
  - Vasopressors: phenylephrine, ephedrine
  - Obstetrics: left uterine displacement
  - Stop any epidural infusion
  - Raise the legs
    - Trendelenburg with hyperbaric LA can cause a high spinal; reduce this by raising the upper body on a pillow under the shoulders while keeping the legs above heart level
    - With hyperbaric LA, avoid reverse Trendelenburg to limit spread: it worsens venous pooling
- Empirical fluid has not been convincingly shown to reduce hypotension after neuraxial block

## Bradycardia
- Causes
  - Block of cardioaccelerator fibres (T1–T4) as the block approaches this level
  - Bezold-Jarisch reflex (Miller, NYSORA): left ventricular receptors sense vigorous contraction of an underfilled heart, triggering parasympathetic outflow and bradycardia
    - More common in young (age less than 50), healthy (ASA class 1), awake patients
    - More likely with ephedrine than phenylephrine
  - Reverse Bainbridge reflex (NYSORA): less venous return reduces right atrial filling, sensed by atrial stretch receptors, leading to a marked rise in vagal tone
  - Can rarely progress to asystole and cardiac arrest
- Risk factors
  - Sensory level above T6
  - Young (age less than 50), healthy (ASA class 1), awake patients
  - Baseline heart rate less than 60bpm, prolonged PR interval
  - Beta blockers
- Management: atropine, ephedrine or adrenaline as needed

## Nausea and vomiting
- Mechanisms
  - Hypotension lowers cerebral blood flow, causing brainstem ischaemia (vomiting centre and chemoreceptor trigger zone)
    - Think of hypotension first when nausea starts, especially right after onset of the spinal, and especially in obstetrics
  - Unopposed vagal activity increases gut peristalsis
  - Intrathecal additives: morphine, diamorphine, clonidine and neostigmine all increase nausea and vomiting
    - Intrathecal fentanyl may reduce it (NYSORA), by improving block quality
  - Inadequate block: nausea from surgical stimulation
  - High spinal: relative parasympathetic excess
- Risk factors
  - Sensory level T6 and above
  - History of motion sickness
- Prevention
  - Avoid hypotension
  - Lower LA dose

## Shivering
- Mechanism unclear: fall in core temperature, or thermoregulatory response to heat redistribution
- Usually worse with epidurals than spinals

## Pruritus
- Caused by neuraxial opioids
- Commoner by the spinal route (46%) than epidural (8.5%) or systemic routes

## Urinary retention
- Block of S2–S4 roots reduces bladder tone and inhibits the voiding reflex; neuraxial opioids add to it
- Risk factor: elderly men with prostatic hypertrophy
- Prevention
  - Urinary catheter for moderate to long procedures
  - Careful postoperative assessment

## Inadvertent subdural block (instead of epidural)
- Subdural space is potential; it opens only when the needle and injected fluid tear neurothelial cells
- Features
  - High sensory block (can reach cervical levels, with Horner syndrome), patchy and asymmetric
  - Motor and sympathetic sparing, probably because the small ventral capacity of the space spares anterior fibres
  - Onset slower than subarachnoid block (2-5mins) but faster than epidural (5-15mins); usually resolves after 2 hours
  - Can also cause complete block failure

## Other block complications
- Failed block and conversion to GA: see failed regional anaesthesia topic
- Hearing loss
- Maternal fever with labour epidural analgesia

## Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity
- Usually from intravascular injection through the needle or a catheter threaded into a vessel
- Mostly an epidural problem, since spinal doses are small
- Intravascular placement in up to 3-7% of epidurals
- Preventing intravascular injection and LAST
  - Aspirate frequently for blood (high false-negative rate)
  - Test dose with lidocaine or adrenaline
  - Slow incremental doses: lower peak plasma levels and earlier detection
  - Keep talking to the patient to catch perioral tingling or tinnitus
- Preventing intravascular placement
  - Lateral position for insertion
  - Do not advance needle or catheter during a contraction
  - Insert catheter no more than 5 cm (≤ 5 cm) into the space
  - Soft-tipped flexible catheter
  - Gravid uterus compresses the vena cava and distends epidural veins, raising the risk of venous cannulation
- If the catheter is intravascular
  - Withdraw 1 cm at a time, flush with saline and aspirate until no blood returns, or remove and resite

## Allergy and anaphylaxis
- Anaphylaxis to LA is very uncommon; more likely with esters than amides

## Neurological injury
- See neurological injury after neuraxial blocks, spinal epidural haematoma and spinal epidural abscess

## Flashcards
- Q: Risk of permanent harm after any neuraxial block (NAP3)?
  - A: 1 in 25000 to 50000
- Q: Risk factors for bradycardia during spinal?
  - A: Level above T6, age less than 50, ASA 1, awake, baseline HR less than 60 bpm, prolonged PR, beta blockers
- Q: Features of a subdural block?
  - A: High patchy asymmetric sensory block (may include Horner syndrome) with motor and sympathetic sparing; resolves after about 2 hours
- Q: Maximum epidural catheter depth in the space to limit intravascular placement?
  - A: 5 cm or less
- Q: Incidence of pruritus with spinal versus epidural opioid?
  - A: 46% spinal, 8.5% epidural
