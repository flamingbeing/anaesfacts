# Sciatic Nerve Block and Parasacral Sacral Plexus Block

Proximal sciatic block (anterior, transgluteal, subgluteal or landmark approaches) and parasacral sacral plexus block for surgery and analgesia below the knee, posterior knee, thigh and hip.

## Distribution
- Whole lower limb below the knee, motor and sensory, except medial leg and foot skin (saphenous nerve)
- Back of the knee and the hamstrings

## Indications
- Surgery below the knee when combined with a femoral block: foot and ankle surgery, below-knee amputation
- Analgesia after knee surgery involving the posterior compartment

## Approaches overview
- Ultrasound-guided
  - Anterior: transverse on the proximal medial thigh
  - Transgluteal: transverse on the buttock between ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter
  - Subgluteal: transverse on the gluteal crease
  - Others described, e.g. parasacral and lateral
- Landmark: transgluteal (classic), anterior, lithotomy, parasacral
- Aim: local anaesthetic spread inside the sciatic nerve sheath
- Nerve stimulator (if used): 1.0 mA, 0.1 msec; calf or foot twitch on contact

## Equipment
- Local anaesthetic 10–20 mL
- 100- or 150-mm needle (deep block)
- Curved (phased array) probe, 2–8 MHz

## Anterior approach (ultrasound)
- Also called the lateral approach
- Advantages
  - Supine, no need to flex the limb; sciatic and femoral blocks done in one position
  - Useful when lateral positioning is not possible: pain, trauma, external fixators
- Limitation: misses posterior thigh skin
  - The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve leaves the sciatic nerve above this level
  - Usually unimportant unless the incision is on the posterior thigh; thigh tourniquet pain is mainly from muscle ischaemia, not skin
- Position
  - Supine; thigh slightly abducted and externally rotated where possible, hip and knee slightly flexed
  - Scan and insert on the anteromedial (not anterior) proximal thigh
  - Expose calf and foot to watch for twitches if using a stimulator (recommended)
- Scanning
  - Curved probe transverse on the anteromedial proximal thigh, about the level of the lesser trochanter
  - Show all 3 fascial compartments: anterior, medial and posterior
  - Femoral artery under sartorius; deep artery of the thigh deep and medial to it (colour Doppler helps)
  - Medial to the vessels: pectineus, adductor longus, adductor brevis
  - Femur: bright rim with shadow, under vastus intermedius
  - Medial to the femur: adductor magnus; anterior to it the hamstrings (biceps femoris, then semimembranosus and semitendinosus)
  - Sciatic nerve: bright oval between adductor magnus and biceps femoris, at 6–8 cm depth
  - If unclear, slide and tilt the probe; ankle dorsiflexion or plantarflexion moves the nerve in its plane
- Needle: after skin local anaesthetic, in-plane from medial to lateral towards the nerve

## Transgluteal approach (ultrasound)
- Also called the posterior approach
- Lateral position, operative side up, hips and knees flexed
- Curved probe transverse on the buttock between ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter
- Layers from superficial to deep: fat, gluteus maximus, quadratus femoris (between the two bony landmarks)
- Nerve: bright oval or triangle in the subgluteal plane between gluteus maximus and quadratus femoris, between the 2 bony landmarks, at 5-7 cm depth
- Needle in-plane, usually from the lateral end of the probe

## Subgluteal approach (ultrasound)
- Lateral position, operative side up, hips and knees flexed; Raj approach is a supine variant with the hip flexed
- Curved probe transverse on the gluteal crease, a few centimetres distal to the transgluteal level
- Alternative: trace the tibial nerve up from the popliteal fossa
- Note the inferior gluteal artery
- Needle in-plane, usually from the lateral end of the probe
- Posterior femoral cutaneous nerve coverage is unreliable
  - One study found only 68% coverage
  - A deep investing fascia at this level can block spread between the sciatic and posterior femoral cutaneous nerves, so two separate injections may be needed
  - A targeted ultrasound block of the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve has been described

## Parasacral approach (ultrasound)
- Blocks the sacral plexus (SIPPS)
  - Superior gluteal nerve
  - Inferior gluteal nerve
  - Pudendal nerve
  - Posterior femoral cutaneous nerve
  - Sciatic nerve
- Indications
  - Hip surgery with a lumbar plexus block (covers the superior and inferior gluteal nerves and nerve to quadratus femoris, which supply the hip)
  - Thigh surgery
- Curvilinear probe
- Gadsden technique: probe angled 45 degrees, halfway between the gluteal cleft and the lateral hip
- Parasacral parallel shift (PSPS, Bendtsen)
  - Line from PSIS to greater trochanter; probe along the line just medial to the midpoint
  - Slide parallel inferomedially until the iliac bone breaks off at the greater sciatic notch
  - Further sliding shows the posterior border of the ilium and piriformis
- Parasacral ischial plane (PIP)
  - Like PSPS, but the end point is the posterior border of the ilium; a fascial plane block
  - Needle makes bony contact with the ischium
  - Local anaesthetic should spread medially under piriformis

## Landmark techniques
- Transgluteal (Labat)
  - The original, long-favoured approach because the nerve position is fairly constant
  - Ultrasound has shifted practice towards subgluteal, anterior and popliteal approaches; ultrasound may reduce femoral artery puncture compared with landmarks
  - Lateral (Sims) position, operative side up, operative knee flexed, other leg straight
  - Line 1: greater trochanter to posterior superior iliac spine
  - Line 2: greater trochanter to sacral hiatus
  - From the midpoint of line 1, drop a perpendicular to line 2; the crossing point is the insertion site
  - Needle perpendicular to all planes
- Anterior
  - Supine, leg slightly internally rotated
  - Inguinal line from ASIS to pubic tubercle; parallel line through the greater trochanter
  - From the junction of the middle and medial thirds of the inguinal line, drop a perpendicular to the trochanter line; the crossing point is the insertion site
  - Needle should pass just medial to the femur onto the nerve; if it hits femur, walk off the bone
- Lithotomy (Raj)
  - Assistant flexes hip and knee to 90 degrees
  - Line from greater trochanter to ischial tuberosity; inject at the midpoint
  - May miss the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh

## Flashcards
- Q: Which skin area is spared by a sciatic block below the knee?
  - A: Medial leg and foot (saphenous nerve)
- Q: Depth of the sciatic nerve in the anterior and transgluteal approaches?
  - A: Anterior 6–8 cm; transgluteal 5-7 cm
- Q: Between which muscles is the sciatic nerve in the transgluteal approach?
  - A: Gluteus maximus and quadratus femoris
- Q: Which nerve does the anterior approach miss?
  - A: Posterior femoral cutaneous nerve (posterior thigh skin)
- Q: Volume and needle for a proximal sciatic block?
  - A: 10–20 mL; 100- or 150-mm needle
