# Axillary Brachial Plexus Block

Block of the terminal branches of the brachial plexus around the axillary artery, for surgery distal to the elbow.

## Distribution
- Targets the musculocutaneous, median, ulnar and radial nerves
- Medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve is not aimed for but is nearly always blocked (runs with the median nerve)
- Often missed: axillary nerve, medial brachial cutaneous nerve, intercostobrachial nerve (T2)
- Gives full cover distal to the elbow
  - Spared: medial upper arm down to the medial epicondyle (medial brachial cutaneous and intercostobrachial nerves)
  - Spared: lateral skin over deltoid (axillary nerve)
- Medial upper arm (intercostobrachial, T2) can be added with a subcutaneous injection just distal to the axilla

## Indications
- Surgery distal to the elbow, such as forearm and hand
- When higher plexus sites are unusable: local infection, burns, indwelling venous catheters

## Advantages and limitations
- Relatively simple
- Far from lung and neuraxis, so fewer serious complications than interscalene (spinal cord or vertebral artery puncture) or supraclavicular (pneumothorax) blocks
- Main risk is intravascular injection into the axillary artery or vein; usually managed with local pressure, no pleura or cord nearby
- Blocks terminal branches: onset slows the more distal the block, and axillary is the slowest of the upper limb blocks

## Equipment
- Linear transducer
- 50 mm needle
- Local anaesthetic 20 mL in adults (smaller volumes have also worked)

## Ultrasound-guided technique
- Position
  - Supine, arm abducted to 90 degrees and externally rotated, elbow flexed for comfort, head turned away
  - Operator's right hand lateral to the patient: face the patient's head for a left block, the feet for a right block
  - Working space: head of bed up, bump under the scapula
  - Avoid over-abduction: uncomfortable and stretches the plexus, possibly making needle or injection injury more likely
- Transducer and sonoanatomy
  - Linear probe short axis to the arm, in the axilla just distal to the pectoralis major insertion on the humerus
  - Start over both biceps and triceps (medial arm); slide proximally if the artery, conjoint tendon and nerves are not seen
  - Structures are superficial, 1–3 cm deep; the axillary artery lies within about a centimetre of the skin
  - One or more axillary veins, often medial to the artery; heavy probe pressure collapses them and they get punctured
  - Consider colour Doppler
  - Three of the four main branches lie around the artery in a neurovascular bundle:
    - Median: superficial and lateral
    - Ulnar: superficial and medial
    - Radial: posterior and medial; hardest to see, reliably between 4 and 6 o'clock; a small bolus there helps show it, or inject at 5–6 o'clock next to the artery just above the conjoint tendon over teres major
  - Surrounding muscles: biceps (anterior, superficial), wedge-shaped coracobrachialis (anterior, deep), conjoint tendon of teres major and latissimus dorsi (medial, posterior)
  - Nerves look round and hyperechoic
  - Musculocutaneous nerve: in the fascia between biceps and coracobrachialis (sometimes inside either), a few cm from the artery; flat hypoechoic oval with a bright rim; appears to move towards or away from the bundle when scanning up and down the arm
  - Nerve positions vary a lot; tracing nerves up from distal helps confirm them
  - Medial antebrachial and medial brachial cutaneous nerves leave the medial cord at mid-axilla, first run in the sheath next to the artery, then may become subcutaneous along the vessels (covered by the subcutaneous intercostobrachial injection)
- Needle insertion
  - Skin local, then in-plane from anterior, aiming at the posterior side of the artery (lateral to medial)
  - Nerves and vessels are packed close: hydrodissect with 0.5–2 mL and advance a few millimetres at a time
  - Inject posterior to the artery first (radial nerve), 5–7 mL; doing median or ulnar first pushes structures deeper and hides them
  - Withdraw almost to skin, redirect to median and ulnar nerves, 7–10 mL
  - Finally redirect to the musculocutaneous nerve, 5–7 mL; stimulation gives elbow flexion
  - All redirections through one skin puncture
  - If the musculocutaneous nerve lies beside the median nerve, a separate injection may be unnecessary
- Perivascular alternative
  - Single injection deep to the artery at 6 o'clock instead of targeting each nerve
  - Quicker to do but slower onset, so total time to surgical block is the same

## Tips
- Aspirate often and inject slowly: systemic toxicity has followed seemingly easy ultrasound-guided axillary blocks
- No visible spread on injection suggests the tip is in a vein: stop, withdraw slightly, ease probe pressure and look again for vessels
- In 16% of people the musculocutaneous nerve leaves the median nerve below the axilla, so median injection covers it

## Continuous catheter technique
- For analgesia and sympathetic block; aim to place the catheter in the plexus sheath near the branches
- Needle in-plane, anterior to posterior, as for single shot
- Confirm tip position posterior to the artery with local anaesthetic, then thread the catheter 3–5 cm past the tip
- Inject through the catheter to confirm spread wrapping the artery
- Alternative: long-axis view of the artery and catheter alongside it; harder, and neither approach shown to be better

## Landmark (transarterial) technique
- Supine, elbow flexed 90°
- Palpate the axillary artery pulse as high in the axilla as possible
- Deliberately enter the artery; once blood returns, advance until it can no longer be aspirated
- Inject behind the artery, then in front of it
- About 40 mL local anaesthetic in total

## Flashcards
- Q: Which nerves are usually spared by an axillary block?
  - A: Axillary, medial brachial cutaneous and intercostobrachial (T2) nerves
- Q: Typical adult local anaesthetic volume for ultrasound-guided axillary block?
  - A: 20 mL
- Q: Which nerve is injected first, and why?
  - A: Radial (posterior to the artery), so earlier volume does not push the other nerves deeper and hide them
- Q: Where is the musculocutaneous nerve found?
  - A: In the fascial plane between biceps and coracobrachialis, away from the artery
- Q: How often does the musculocutaneous nerve branch from the median nerve below the axilla?
  - A: 16% of cases
