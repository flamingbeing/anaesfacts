# Cervical Plexus Block

Block of the C2–C4 cervical plexus at superficial, intermediate or deep (paravertebral) level; superficial or intermediate is simpler, safer and usually just as good as deep.

## Overview
- Deep block is advanced, with serious risks such as intrathecal or vertebral artery injection
- Superficial block is simpler and safer, and suits most indications as well as the deep block

## Anatomy
- Formed by ventral rami of C1–C4, arranged in 3 loops
  - Loop 1 (C1–C2): mainly with the hypoglossal nerve (CN XII)
  - Loop 2 (C2–C3): ansa cervicalis, greater auricular and transverse cervical nerves
  - Loop 3 (C3–C4): lower plexus, phrenic and supraclavicular nerves
- Superficial plexus: from ventral rami of C2 to C4, four cutaneous branches (all C2–4)
  - Lesser occipital (C2)
  - Greater auricular (C2–C3)
  - Transverse cervical (C3–C4)
  - Supraclavicular (C3–C4)
- These four branches emerge from behind the posterior border of SCM; SCM forms a "roof" over them

## Distribution
- Superficial plexus (sensory): skin of anterolateral neck, from jawline to clavicle, back to the mastoid
- Deep plexus (motor): anterior vertebral muscles; contributes to SCM, trapezius, scalenus anterior and medius
- Not covered: mental, infraorbital and supraorbital nerves (trigeminal branches)

## Layers of injection
- Superficial (sensory): superficial to the deep cervical fascia
- Intermediate: between the investing layer of deep cervical fascia (which wraps SCM) and the prevertebral fascia
- Deep (motor): deep to the prevertebral fascia, effectively a paravertebral block of the C2–4 roots as they leave their foramina

## Superficial block: landmark technique
- Subcutaneous infiltration along the posterior border of SCM at its midpoint (Erb's point)
- Fan technique: redirect up and down along the posterior border, 2–3 cm below and above the insertion point, aiming for all four branches
  - 3 ml at the midpoint
  - Then 7 ml subcutaneously caudad and cephalad along the posterior border
- Stay subcutaneous and behind SCM; avoid going deep (more than 1–2 cm)
- Total 10–15 mL (3–5 mL per redirection)
- Motor block not needed, so use a lower concentration of long-acting drug: ropivacaine 0.2–0.5% or bupivacaine 0.25%

## Superficial block: ultrasound
- True superficial (skin only): subcutaneous infiltration at the lateral edge of SCM

## Intermediate block (ultrasound)
- Targets the plexus between investing and prevertebral fascia
- Indications: carotid endarterectomy, superficial neck surgery
- Advantages over landmark: see the spread in the right plane (higher success), avoid going too deep and hitting nearby structures
- Aim: local anaesthetic near the sensory branches of C2, C3 and C4
- Equipment
  - High-frequency linear transducer
  - 50mm needle
  - 5–15 mL local anaesthetic (for example a 10-mL syringe)
  - Purely sensory nerves, so low concentrations suffice: ropivacaine 0.25–0.5%, bupivacaine 0.25% or lidocaine 1%
- Position
  - Supine or semi-sitting, head turned slightly away
  - Expose neck and upper chest to judge SCM length and position
  - Posterior border of SCM hard to feel in obesity: ask the patient to lift the head off the bed
  - Head on pillow; slide the pillow out of the way of the needling hand, as for interscalene
- Clean and drape
- Sonoanatomy
  - Linear probe transverse on lateral neck over the midpoint of SCM (about cricoid level)
  - Find SCM, slide posteriorly until its tapering posterior edge is mid-screen
  - Look for the brachial plexus or interscalene groove between anterior and middle scalenes
  - Prevertebral fascia: hyperechoic line separating SCM from brachial plexus and scalenes
  - Cervical plexus: small cluster of hypoechoic nodules (honeycomb) just deep or lateral to the posterior edge of SCM, just superficial to the prevertebral fascia over the interscalene groove
  - Greater auricular nerve sometimes seen on the superficial surface of SCM as a small round hypoechoic dot
- Needle insertion
  - In-plane, lateral/posterior to medial
  - Through the investing fascia to the plexus; inject to surround it
- Alternative if the plexus is not seen: sub-sternocleidomastoid approach
  - Pass the needle behind SCM; tip in the space between SCM and prevertebral fascia near the posterior border
  - Inject 5–15 mL and watch it layer out between SCM and prevertebral fascia; reposition if spread is poor
- Tip: look for carotid, jugular and vertebral vessels; use Doppler if needed

## Deep block
- Local anaesthetic deep to the prevertebral fascia: paravertebral block of C2–C4 at the intervertebral foramina
- Landmark technique (NYSORA)
  - Draw a line from the mastoid tip to Chassaignac's tubercle (C6 transverse process, felt behind the clavicular head of SCM just below the cricoid)
  - Insertion points along it caudal to the mastoid: C2 at 2cm, C3 at 4cm, C4 at 6cm
  - Single injection at C4 or triple injection at C2/C3/C4; a single C3 injection is also considered safe and effective
  - Needle perpendicular to skin, advance slowly to contact the transverse process (posterior tubercle; spinal nerves lie just in front)
  - Withdraw 1–2 mm, aspirate, inject 3-5 mL per level
- Landmark technique (FRCA UK)
  - Skin infiltration at the midpoint of the posterior border of SCM
  - Through this, infiltrate lateral to the transverse processes of the second, third and fourth cervical vertebrae, reached about 2 cm deep

## Complications
- Superficial and intermediate: similar to interscalene, though serious complications are reported as close to zero
  - Intravascular injection
  - Brachial plexus block, especially with large volumes or high concentrations
  - Phrenic nerve block (breathlessness)
  - Horner's syndrome (blurred vision, ptosis)
  - Recurrent laryngeal nerve block (hoarseness)
- Deep: higher complication rate than superficial
  - Carotid, internal jugular or vertebral artery puncture and intravascular injection, leading to seizures (LAST)
  - Epidural or intrathecal injection, leading to total spinal anaesthesia
  - Phrenic nerve palsy, especially relevant in COPD
  - Recurrent laryngeal, hypoglossal, vagus nerves, brachial plexus, sympathetic chain (Horner's)
  - Conversion to GA (2.5%)

## Flashcards
- Q: Which roots form the superficial cervical plexus and what are its four branches?
  - A: C2–C4; lesser occipital, greater auricular, transverse cervical, supraclavicular
- Q: Where is local anaesthetic placed for an intermediate cervical plexus block?
  - A: Between the investing layer of deep cervical fascia and the prevertebral fascia
- Q: Landmark superficial cervical plexus block volume?
  - A: 10–15 mL (3–5 mL per redirection)
- Q: Deep cervical plexus landmark insertion distances from the mastoid?
  - A: C2 2cm, C3 4cm, C4 6cm along the line to Chassaignac's tubercle (C6)
- Q: Conversion to GA rate quoted for cervical plexus block?
  - A: 2.5%
