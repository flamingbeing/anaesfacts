# Paediatric Epidural Blockade

Epidural analgesia in children: why it is used, anatomy, technique, doses and complications.

## Why it has become popular

- More focus on good analgesia in children
- Allows light anaesthesia, minimal opioid, early extubation and no postoperative ventilation
- Better outcomes in some high-risk cases: open fundoplication, severe respiratory disease having abdominal surgery, neonatal oesophageal atresia repair
- Good equipment in suitable sizes
- Paediatric catheters often have one end hole and no side holes; depth markings may differ from adult catheters and a 20 cm mark may be missing

## Indications

- Local anaesthetic with or without opioid for intraoperative anaesthesia (usually with general anaesthesia) and postoperative analgesia in thoracic, abdominal or lower body surgery
- Most useful after major surgery with prolonged severe pain
- Evidence that it beats IV opioid analgesia in children is limited

## Contraindications

- As for other central blocks
- Ventriculoperitoneal shunt is not a contraindication; strict asepsis and consider prophylactic antibiotics

## Anatomy

- Depth to epidural space varies widely
- Rough guide (lumbar, over 1 year old): depth = 1 mm/kg
- Cord ends lower in young children; lumbar approach at L4/L5 or L5/S1

## Technique

- Much as in adults, except the child is anaesthetised
- Loss of resistance to saline preferred; air has caused air embolism and central neurological complications
  - Usually a saline-filled syringe
  - Microdrip method: loss of resistance to an open saline drip, leaving both hands free for the Tuohy needle
- Approaches: caudal, sacral intervertebral, lumbar, thoracic
- Cervical epidurals: no place in children
- Lumbar Tuohy angle: best at 74° pointing cephalad; steep adult thoracic angles are not needed in young children
- Paramedian: slightly off midline, contact the lamina, walk off its upper edge to the ligamentum flavum
- Catheter tip check: X-ray, ultrasound (young children), or Tsui methods (ECG morphology or electrical stimulation)
- Sacral intervertebral block
  - Possible because the sacrum is not fully fused in children
  - Easiest at S2/3, just below the line joining the posterior superior iliac spines
  - No needle angulation needed; standard epidural technique
  - Space is shallower than lumbar as there is no sacral lordosis

## Dosage

- No perfect formula
- Lumbar initial bolus: 0.7 mL/kg of 0.25% bupivacaine
- Infusions: usually 0.1% or 0.125%; weaker solutions work if adjuncts are added
- Maximum bupivacaine infusion: 0.25 mg/kg/hour under 6 months; 0.4 mg/kg/hour in older children
- Ropivacaine: 0.2 mL/kg/hour of 0.2% (0.4 mg/kg/hour)
- Low-dose opioid adjuncts (good safety record)
  - Morphine 1-5 mcg/kg/hour
  - Fentanyl 0.1-0.5 mcg/kg/hour
  - Diamorphine 5-25 mcg/kg/hour (where still available in the UK)
- Continuous oxygen saturation monitoring required
- Opioids cause some sedation: monitor it, though it can help with active children who have catheters
- Often nursed in HDU; many centres use surgical wards with an agreed protocol

## Complications

- Fewer cardiovascular effects in small children (immature sympathetic system, low vascular tone)
- Hypotension without bleeding: suspect subarachnoid or subdural catheter
- Hypotension more common over 8 years of age
- Bloody tap, especially with IVC obstruction or excessive spinal flexion during insertion
- Leak around the catheter: expected in neonates; ignore it if the block works; dressings may need changing more often

## Flashcards

- Q: Rough depth to the lumbar epidural space in a child over 1 year?
  - A: 1 mm/kg
- Q: Initial lumbar epidural bolus in children?
  - A: 0.7 mL/kg of 0.25% bupivacaine
- Q: Maximum bupivacaine epidural infusion rate by age?
  - A: 0.25 mg/kg/hour under 6 months; 0.4 mg/kg/hour in older children
- Q: Standard paediatric ropivacaine epidural infusion?
  - A: 0.2 mL/kg/hour of 0.2% (0.4 mg/kg/hour)
