# Bier Block (Intravenous Regional Anaesthesia)

Local anaesthetic injected into the veins of an exsanguinated limb isolated by a tourniquet; simple, fast and dense, but limited by tourniquet pain and toxicity risk on release.

## Mechanism
- Local anaesthetic fills the venous system of a limb emptied by gravity or compression and cut off from the circulation by a tourniquet
- Two kinds of anaesthesia
  - "Direct": almost immediate, between the two cuffs; local anaesthetic bathes bare nerve endings, like infiltration
  - "Indirect": after 5 to 7 minutes, distal to the distal cuff; drug reaches nerve trunks via the vasa nervorum and causes conduction block

## Indications
- Short surgery or manipulation of the upper or lower limb
- Most accepted for the upper limb; tourniquet and safety problems are more frequent in the leg
- Typical: short hand or forearm surgery, e.g. radial fracture reduction, carpal tunnel release
- Lower limb: mass excision, digital nerve repair, phalangeal fracture or dislocation, accessory navicular excision; foot, ankle or distal leg orthopaedic cases of about 45 minutes or less
- Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS, formerly reflex sympathetic dystrophy), as an alternative to repeated sympathetic ganglion blocks
  - IVRA chemical sympathectomy with guanethidine or bretylium can last up to 5 days; local anaesthetic alone lasts only several hours
  - Quantitative sudomotor axon reflex testing (QSART) before and after can predict who will get lasting relief from guanethidine IVRA after injury or surgery

## Contraindications
- Crush injury of the limb
- Local skin infection or cellulitis
- Compound fractures
- Convincing local anaesthetic allergy
- Severe vascular injury of the limb
- Existing arteriovenous shunt; any patient unsuited to a tourniquet (e.g. severe peripheral vascular disease)
- Sickle cell disease
- Surgery expected to last over 1 hour (tourniquet pain)

## Technique: upper limb
- Cannula in a peripheral vein as distal as possible
- Double pneumatic tourniquet high on the upper arm
- Elevate the arm (passive exsanguination), then wrap an Esmarch bandage from fingertips to the distal cuff (active exsanguination)
- Compress the axillary artery by hand, inflate the proximal cuff to 50–100 mm Hg above systolic pressure, then remove the Esmarch
- Inject local anaesthetic, then remove the cannula
  - 30–50 mL of 0.5% lidocaine, depending on arm size
  - Concentration no higher than 0.5% w/v, e.g. 25–50 mL of 0.5% lidocaine
- Onset with 0.5% lidocaine is quick (about 4.5 ± 0.3 minutes); offset after deflation is also quick (5.8 ± 0.5 minutes)
- After about 25–30 minutes of anaesthesia, or with tourniquet pain: inflate the distal cuff, then deflate the proximal cuff
- Even for very short procedures, keep the cuff up for at least 20 min to avoid a large systemic bolus

## Choice of local anaesthetic
- Lidocaine is the standard in the United States; prilocaine is preferred in Europe
- Alkalinising lidocaine adds little or nothing
- Avoid long-acting agents (risk of early cuff release)
  - Never use bupivacaine (toxicity)
- Mepivacaine is less attractive because it is a strong vasoconstrictor

## Technique: lower limb
- Same as the upper limb but needs a larger volume to fill the bigger vascular space from cannula to cuff: 100 mL versus 50 mL

## Cuff deflation
- Always deflate in cycles
- After at least 30 minutes, deflate the cuff and reinflate at once
- Watch and ask for toxicity: tinnitus, light-headedness, metallic taste, signs of CNS excitation
- If none after about 1 minute, deflate again and reinflate for about 1 to 2 minutes, again watching for toxicity
- If still none, the cuff can be released and removed
- Each brief deflation lets only a small part of the unbound drug into the circulation, avoiding a sudden sustained rise in blood level

## Complications
- Systemic local anaesthetic toxicity, e.g. from tourniquet failure
  - Leak under the cuff in almost 100% of lower limb blocks, about 25% of upper limb blocks
- Tourniquet pain
- Nerve injury (median, ulnar, musculocutaneous): very rare, from direct cuff pressure, with crush-like changes on histology
  - Keep tourniquet time under 2 hours to limit capillary and muscle damage from tissue acidosis
- Compartment syndrome: rare; mainly with reduction of lower limb long-bone fractures, from large volumes and incomplete exsanguination

## Flashcards
- Q: Upper limb IVRA dose of lidocaine?
  - A: 30–50 mL of 0.5% lidocaine (concentration no more than 0.5%)
- Q: Minimum cuff time even for a very short procedure?
  - A: At least 20 min (cyclical deflation after at least 30 minutes)
- Q: Which local anaesthetic must never be used for IVRA?
  - A: Bupivacaine
- Q: Proximal cuff pressure for a Bier block?
  - A: 50–100 mm Hg above systolic pressure
- Q: Incidence of leak under the tourniquet, lower versus upper limb?
  - A: Almost 100% versus about 25%
