# Local Infiltration and Field Block

Infiltration numbs nerve endings in the tissue around the wound; a field block lays a wall of LA across the nerves supplying the area, away from the surgical site.

## Principle
- Infiltration targets nerve endings close to the injection, within a limited area
- Peripheral nerve blocks target nerve axons, often far from the surgical site

## Indications
- Any surgery where a specific nerve or plexus block is not possible

## Contraindications
- Infection over the injection site: risks seeding deeper tissues, and LA works less well

## Technique
- Inject subcutaneously or intradermally
- Infiltration: LA into the wound edges, subcutaneously
- Field block: LA into the skin around the surgical site
- Aspirate before injecting to reduce intravascular injection
- Timing
  - Before surgery: allows lighter GA, but can distort the wound (e.g. vermilion border of the lip) or tissue planes (e.g. herniotomy)
  - Before closure: inject or instil into the incision; a gradual emergence lets it take effect
- Reducing pain of injection when awake: distraction, topical LA first, warmed LA, 27 G needle, slow injection

## Local anaesthetic and duration
- Much shorter than after a nerve block
- Ropivacaine and bupivacaine: 4 to 8 hours
- Lidocaine: 1 to 2 hours

## Field block
- Anaesthetises a fairly small area by injecting a wall of LA across the path of its nerves
- Injection is into soft tissue some distance from the operative site, where the nerves run
- Advantages: longer duration and no distortion of the operative field

## Complications
- Intravascular injection
- LA toxicity
- Haematoma
