# Infraclavicular Block

Brachial plexus block at the cords around the axillary artery below the clavicle, for surgery below the shoulder; deepest brachial plexus block, with paracoracoid, retroclavicular and costoclavicular approaches.

## Distribution of block
- Upper limb below the shoulder
- Medial upper arm skin (intercostobrachial nerve, T2) needs an extra subcutaneous injection on the medial arm just below the axilla

## Indications
- Arm, elbow, forearm and hand surgery; a good block from the lower arm to the hand

## Considerations
- Previously avoided: complex, variable landmarks, frequent vascular puncture and limited success
- Ultrasound shows the axillary artery and surrounding cords, so it is now popular
- Needle is further from pleura and neuraxis: low risk of pneumothorax or neuraxial spread
- Phrenic block very unlikely with the lateral parasagittal approach
- Downsides: plexus is deeper and the needle angle steeper, so needle and anatomy are harder to see together
- Cords need not be identified: a U-shaped deposit around the artery is enough

## Equipment
- Linear transducer (8–14 MHz)
- 80-100mm needle
- Local anaesthetic: 20–30 mL
- Nerve stimulator optional (0.5–0.8 mA, 0.1 msec)
  - First response usually lateral cord (elbow or finger flexion)
  - Deeper, below the artery: posterior cord (finger and wrist extension)

## Paracoracoid (traditional) technique
### Positioning
- Supine, head turned away
- Arm abducted to 90 degrees, elbow flexed: lifts the clavicle, makes the plexus shallower, and improves views of the pectoral muscles, cords and needle

### Ultrasound anatomy
- Linear probe parasagittal, below the clavicle, just medial to the coracoid process
  - Find the coracoid by palpating just medial to the shoulder while raising and lowering the arm; it meets the fingers as the arm comes down
  - Cephalad on screen left, caudad on screen right; needle travels left (cephalad) to right (caudad)
  - May need rotating so the probe runs cephalo-medial to caudo-lateral
- Axillary artery in cross-section, deep to pectoralis major and minor and their fasciae
  - Axillary vein: compressible, hypoechoic, caudal to the artery; smaller vessels (e.g. cephalic vein) often seen
  - Adjust depth for chest wall thickness; artery typically 3-5 cm deep
- Three cords around the artery at 3–5 cm (the deepest brachial plexus block), seen as round hyperechoic structures; position varies a lot
  - Lateral cord: cephalad, 9 o'clock
  - Medial cord: caudal, 3 o'clock
  - Posterior cord: deep, 6 o'clock
  - Cords may not be visible; not needed for success
- Scanning medially and laterally in the parasagittal plane locates chest wall and pleura medially (deep caudal part of the image); block lateral to the pleura to reduce pneumothorax risk
- Subscapularis may be seen deep in the cephalad part of the image
- Consider colour Doppler for the thoracoacromial artery and cephalic vein in the needle path

### Needle insertion
- Skin local anaesthetic; in-plane from the cephalad end of the probe, just below the clavicle, aimed caudad
- Through pectoralis major and minor, towards the deep side of the artery first (6 o'clock)
- Aspirate, give 1–2 mL to confirm; then small aliquots, repositioning as needed to cover every plane holding the plexus
- Endpoint: U-shaped spread around the artery (cephalad, caudad and posterior) on ultrasound; individual cords need not be targeted
- Septa may limit spread around the artery: reposition to complete the U

### Tips
- An artefact behind the artery is often mistaken for the posterior cord
- "Heel-up" (rock the probe towards the head, pressing tissue caudal to it) makes it easier to change needle angle

## Retroclavicular approach (RAPTIR)
- Probe as for the traditional approach
- Needle in-plane, entering just cephalad and posterior to the clavicle, running parallel to the probe
  - Letting the upper edge of the probe slightly overlap the clavicle helps orientation
- Needle seen passing under the clavicle; aim for 6 o'clock, deep to the axillary artery

## Costoclavicular block
- Supine, head turned away, ipsilateral arm abducted 90º if possible (brings structures more superficial)
- Probe transverse in the medial infraclavicular fossa, parallel and next to the clavicle
- Find the axillary artery with the cords lateral to it
  - Cords: hyperechoic ovals in a triangle
  - Tilt cephalad so the beam is perpendicular to nerves and vessels
- Needle in-plane, lateral to medial, into the middle of the three cords (ideally between lateral and posterior cords)
- 15-20 mL of local anaesthetic
  - A septum often separates the lateral cord from the medial and posterior cords: two injections, 5 mL for the lateral cord and 10-15 mL for the rest
- Alternative: in-plane medial to lateral, aiming between the axillary artery and the lateral cord

## Landmark technique
- Below the clavicle the plexus forms three cords around the axillary artery
- Several approaches; most common: from the midpoint of the clavicle, mark a point 2–3 cm caudad
- Needle directed at a 45 degree angle towards the axilla
- Seek a hand motor response at a current <0.5 mA

## Continuous infraclavicular block
- Well suited to catheters: chest wall muscle holds the catheter and resists dislodgement better than the shallower interscalene and supraclavicular sites
- Aim: catheter near the cords under the pectoral muscles
- Three phases: (1) needle placement; (2) catheter advancement; (3) securing the catheter; ultrasound guides the first two in most patients
- Needle in-plane, cephalad to caudad, as for single shot
- Tip posterior to the axillary artery before injecting and threading
  - A posterior cord response (finger or wrist extension) also confirms; then inject 1–2 mL
- Advance and secure as for other continuous blocks
- Typical start: 5 mL/h, with 8-mL patient-controlled boluses every hour
  - Larger boluses are needed to spread around the artery and reach all cords

## Flashcards
- Q: Where are the three cords relative to the axillary artery on a parasagittal infraclavicular view?
  - A: Lateral cord cephalad (9 o'clock), medial cord caudal (3 o'clock), posterior cord deep (6 o'clock)
- Q: Spread pattern needed for a successful paracoracoid infraclavicular block?
  - A: U-shaped spread around the axillary artery (cephalad, caudad, posterior)
- Q: Volumes for a costoclavicular block?
  - A: 15-20 mL total; 5 mL for the lateral cord and 10-15 mL for the rest if a septum divides them
- Q: Typical infraclavicular catheter regimen?
  - A: 5 mL/h with 8-mL patient-controlled boluses every hour
- Q: Depth of the cords at the infraclavicular level?
  - A: 3–5 cm, the deepest brachial plexus block
