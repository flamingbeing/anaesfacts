# Spinal Anaesthesia

Local anaesthetic injected into the CSF in the spinal canal; fast, dense, fixed-duration block mainly for surgery below the umbilicus.

## Definition
- Injection of LA into the CSF within the spinal canal

## Mechanism of action
- LA disrupts nerve conduction in the spinal nerve roots and spinal cord

## Indications
- Mainly surgery below the umbilicus (NYSORA)
  - Urology, gynaecology, obstetrics, lower abdominal and perineal general surgery
  - Lower limb vascular and orthopaedic surgery
- Increasingly used for some surgery above the umbilicus
- Obstetrics: caesarean section; with or without labour analgesia
- Gynaecology (T10): D&C, hysteroscopy
- Urology (T10)
  - TURP: spinal preferred for better sacral coverage, denser sensory block and shorter duration
  - Procedures involving the ureters need a sensory level up to T8
- Lower limb orthopaedics: hip fracture

## Specific situations
- Well suited to:
  - COPD and other respiratory disease
  - Autonomic dysreflexia: spinal better than epidural (more complete block)
  - Malignant hyperthermia
- Can be used for ambulatory surgery

## Advantages over GA
- Better early postoperative analgesia
  - Less analgesia needed in PACU, but no difference after 6h (wears off quickly)
  - Shorter PACU stay from better pain control
- No airway instrumentation and its risks, including aspiration
- Fewer postoperative respiratory complications
- Avoids risks of GA drugs
  - Still has significant haemodynamic effects, unlike caudal or slowly titrated epidural
  - Less postoperative cognitive dysfunction in the elderly
  - No risk of awareness
- Possible benefits
  - Less bleeding in orthopaedic surgery and LSCS
  - Lower DVT risk in lower limb surgery, especially hip
  - Attenuated surgical stress response (neuraxial opioids in cardiac surgery)
  - Fewer surgical site infections
  - Vasodilation lowers risk of arterial thrombosis in angioplasty
- PONV: may be reduced, but not reliably
  - Hypotension, intrathecal opioids and vagal GI overactivity after sympathetic block can all still cause it
- Obstetrics: minimal drug transfer to the fetus; mother awake and partner present
- Usually no mortality benefit (except hip fracture); clear benefits are hard to prove

## Disadvantages compared with GA
- Takes longer to set up
- Can fail, needing conversion to GA
- Fixed, limited duration; cannot be extended
- Needs positioning (lateral or sitting), not always possible (e.g. on traction)
- Patient may move during surgery if oversedated or confused
- Rapid sympathectomy: sudden fall in SVR and preload leads to sudden hypotension
  - Contraindicated in hypovolaemia and fixed cardiac output states
- Other complications: PDPH, LNDP, vertebral haematoma or abscess; in obstetrics, maternal fever

## Physiological effects
- Due to block of sympathetic and somatic (sensory and motor) fibres
- Onset is rapid: within 60 s
- Neurological: differential block
  - Small fibres (e.g. C fibres) are blocked more easily than large ones
  - Order of block: sympathetic, then temperature, pain, touch, and motor last
  - In neuraxial block this shows as spatial separation, as LA concentration falls with distance from the injection site
  - Sympathetic block extends about 2-6 dermatomes beyond the motor block (Miller)
  - Pain and touch block extends 2-3 dermatomes beyond the motor block
  - Dilute LA in epidurals may give minimal motor block
- CNS: no cognitive effect unless hypotension reduces cerebral blood flow
- Cardiovascular: from sympathetic block; resembles combined alpha-1 and beta blockade
  - Peripheral sympathetic fibres (T1-L2)
    - Arterial and venous dilation; venous effect larger as most blood volume is venous
    - Preload falls from venodilation
    - HR may rise (baroreflex) or fall (block of cardiac accelerator fibres T1–T4, reverse Bainbridge reflex or Bezold-Jarisch reflex)
    - SVR falls from arteriolar dilation
    - Stroke volume and cardiac output fall, as the fall in preload outweighs the fall in afterload
    - Some patients show a brief early rise in cardiac output when SVR falls more than venous return, especially elderly hypertensives with high baseline SVR
    - Hypotension follows from falls in both stroke volume and SVR
    - Size of the effect depends on baseline sympathetic tone (Miller)
  - Cardiac sympathetic fibres (T1-T4), with a high spinal
    - Bradycardia
    - Usually well tolerated, but asystole and second- and third-degree heart block can occur
  - Bradycardia and hypotension may also come from the Bezold-Jarisch reflex (Miller, NYSORA) or reverse Bainbridge reflex (NYSORA)
- Respiratory: usually little effect
  - High block paralyses abdominal and intercostal muscles, impairing active expiration
  - Phrenic nerve and diaphragm usually spared, so inspiration is largely preserved
  - Dyspnoea in a high block is often from not feeling the chest wall move; if the patient speaks clearly in a normal voice, ventilation is usually adequate and reassurance helps (NYSORA)
- Hepatic and renal: blood flow falls with MAP
  - Block of sacral parasympathetic outflow (S2-4) to the bladder can cause urinary retention
- GI: block from T6 to L1 removes splanchnic sympathetic supply, so peristalsis increases
- Endocrine: reduced stress response

## Contraindications
- Absolute (as for other blocks)
  - Patient refusal
  - Allergy to the drugs used
  - Infection at the insertion site
- Relative
  - Haematological: significant bleeding tendency (risk of spinal haematoma)
    - Deranged PT/PTT, anticoagulants, antiplatelets, low platelets
    - Often avoided if platelets <100k; definitely avoided if <50k
    - SOAP 2021 consensus: proceed if platelets >70k, avoid if <50k
  - Cardiovascular: where a sudden fall in SVR is unwanted (fixed cardiac output, preload-dependent states); the speed and size of the fall are unpredictable
    - Severe hypovolaemia, absolute or relative (e.g. sepsis): exaggerated hypotension
    - Severe stenotic valve disease; many anaesthetists are wary even in mild AS
    - A catheter technique (epidural or intrathecal catheter) allows small repeated doses and better haemodynamic control
  - Neurological: intracranial space-occupying lesion or raised ICP
  - Neurological: pre-existing or suspected neurological deficit
    - Theoretical worsening of injury (double-crush phenomenon)
    - May confuse later neurological assessment
    - Multiple sclerosis: demyelinated fibres may be more sensitive to LA toxicity
  - Previous spinal surgery or instrumentation
    - Does not itself raise the risk of neurological complications
    - Scar, adhesions, hardware or bone graft can make access to the CSF or epidural space hard or impossible, and spread unpredictable or patchy
    - Chronic low back pain without a deficit is not a contraindication; no proven link to worse back pain
  - Infection: sepsis or bacteraemia (theoretical seeding of the neuraxis); some avoid neuraxial blocks in febrile patients

## Postoperative care
- Fall precautions
- Analgesic plan

## Flashcards
- Q: What sensory level is needed for procedures involving the ureters?
  - A: T8
- Q: Platelet thresholds for neuraxial block in the note (general and SOAP 2021)?
  - A: Often avoid if <100k, definitely if <50k; SOAP 2021: proceed if >70k, avoid if <50k
- Q: How far does the sympathetic block extend beyond the motor block?
  - A: About 2-6 dermatomes (pain and touch 2-3 dermatomes)
- Q: Which fibres are blocked by a high spinal to cause bradycardia?
  - A: Cardiac accelerator sympathetic fibres, T1-T4
- Q: Why does cardiac output fall after spinal anaesthesia?
  - A: Venodilation lowers preload, which outweighs the fall in afterload
