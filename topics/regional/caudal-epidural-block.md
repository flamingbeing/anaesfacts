# Caudal Epidural Block

Local anaesthetic injected into the epidural space through the sacral hiatus (between the sacral cornua), below the end of the spinal cord; simple, effective and safe, mainly used in children.

## Why it is easier in children
- Sacrum is flatter and the injection angle easier (smaller gluteal muscles, lower natal cleft)
- Sacral hiatus is relatively large (less ossification around the sacrococcygeal membrane)

## Indications
- Intra- and postoperative analgesia for surgery up to the umbilicus in neonates and infants
- In older children it reliably covers only sacral and lumbar roots; lower thoracic roots are inconsistent
- Common uses:
  - Circumcision
  - Hypospadias repair
  - Inguinal herniotomy
  - Orchidopexy
  - Anorectal surgery
  - Elective lower limb orthopaedic surgery
  - Lower limb plastic surgery

## Contraindications
- General contraindications to central neuraxial blocks
- Abnormal sacral anatomy
  - Pigmentation, dimples or hairy patches over the sacrum may signal spinal dysraphism or a tethered cord
  - Do not attempt without confirming normal neuroanatomy: check whether ultrasound or MRI has been done
- Mongolian blue spots are not a contraindication

## Anatomy
- Neonatal sacrum: five cartilaginous sacral vertebrae that gradually ossify and fuse into the adult sacrum
- Sacral hiatus: failed fusion of the posterior arches of S5 (sometimes S4, occasionally S3)
  - Bordered laterally by the two sacral cornua; may be felt as a dimple between them
  - Covered by the sacrococcygeal membrane (continuation of the ligamentum flavum)
  - Needle passes skin, subcutaneous tissue, then sacrococcygeal membrane
- Filum terminale exits at the hiatus and runs over the back of S5 and the sacrococcygeal joint to the coccyx; the S5 roots also exit through the hiatus, medial to each cornu
- Sacral canal contents: epidural venous plexus (usually ends at S4, may go lower) and fat
- Changes with age:
  - Dural sac ends at S3/4 at birth, rising to S2 by 3 years of age
  - Apex of the sacral hiatus is most often at S4
  - Gap between dural sac end and hiatus apex can be less than 6 mm in some people
  - Posterior laminae start to fuse at about 7 years; posterior wall mostly ligamentous until puberty; bony fusion complete only in the late 20s
  - Ossification shrinks the hiatus; canal widens side to side from neonate to toddler, and front to back with age
  - Infant sacrum sits higher relative to the iliac crests; reaches the adult position by 4 years of age
- Caudal space dimensions vary widely with age and between children of the same age
- Sacral anomalies in 3-5% of people; may shorten the hiatus-to-dural-sac distance

## Technique
- Lateral position
- Landmarks: sacral cornua and hiatus
  - Hiatus lies at the apex of an equilateral triangle with the 2 posterior superior iliac spines
  - Felt as a gap bounded by the cornua
- Equipment
  - IV cannula often used (e.g. 23G): pierce the membrane, withdraw the needle, advance the plastic cannula; blunt tip lowers the risk of venous puncture, dural puncture and nerve injury
  - Hypodermic needles from 25G (neonates) to 20G (older children); finer needles are less traumatic but linked to IV or subarachnoid injection after false-negative aspiration
  - Styletted needles or cannulae avoid carrying a skin plug (bacteria, dermal cells, risk of epidermoid tumour) into the epidural space; a small skin nick first helps for the same reason
- Insert at 45° to the sacrum through the sacrococcygeal membrane until a pop or loss of resistance
  - Needle: flatten the angle and advance slightly; no more than 2-3 mm into the epidural space (more increases venous puncture and dural tap)
  - Cannula: thread it off the needle
- Optional check: inject 3–5 mL of air or saline quickly with fingers over the needle tip; swelling or crepitus means not epidural
- Aspirate for blood or CSF; leaving the hub open for a few seconds also helps, as aspiration of small epidural veins is often negative
- Inject slowly with ECG monitoring, feeling for subcutaneous swelling and watching for arrhythmias; no test is 100% sensitive or specific

## Ultrasound guidance
- Simple, no risk of air embolism, and confirms epidural injection

## Drugs and dosage
- Choice of local anaesthetic
  - Bupivacaine traditionally; increasingly replaced by levobupivacaine and ropivacaine (less cardiotoxic, better differential block, since motor block is poorly tolerated in awake children)
- Concentration
  - 0.5%: dense sacral block lasting well into the postoperative period (e.g. hypospadias repair)
  - 0.125% often used under 6 months of age
  - 0.2% bupivacaine: 1ml/kg reaches the toxic dose limit; alternatively 0.25% ropivacaine
  - Always calculate the maximum safe dose; lower the concentration if more volume is needed for the block height
- Volume determines spread
  - Armitage formula:
    - 0.5 mL/kg: sacral roots (perineum, soles of feet)
    - 1.0 mL/kg: lower thoracic and upper lumbar roots (thoracolumbar)
    - 1.25 mL/kg: mid-thoracic (T6) roots
  - Problem: 0.5 mL/kg gives a reliable sacral block, but 1.25 mL/kg can spread erratically and sometimes alarmingly high; thoracic levels are more often reached with a caudal catheter or an intervertebral epidural
  - Simplified Armitage:
    - Over 6 months: 1 mL/kg of 0.25% bupivacaine covers upper lumbar roots (the groin) in children under 20 kg; inconsistent above this weight
    - Any age: 0.3-0.5 mL/kg reliably blocks sacral roots
  - Takasaki formula: 0.06 ml/segment/kg
  - If a higher level is needed, consider a lumbar epidural

## Duration
- Single-shot plain caudal block lasts 2-3 hr
- Analgesia usually 4-6hours

## Adjuncts
- Clonidine 1-2mcg/kg
  - Better analgesia; prolongs action to 4-6 hrs
  - Side effects: hypotension (if over 2 mcg/kg), bradycardia, postoperative sedation, blunted ventilatory response to CO2
  - Avoid under 3 months (apnoea risk)
- Dexmedetomidine 1mcg/kg
- Opioids
  - Fentanyl 1-2mcg/kg every 4 h (q4h)
  - Morphine 25-30 mcg/kg every 12 h (q12h)
  - Side effects: nausea and vomiting, pruritus, urinary retention, respiratory depression
  - Close monitoring for 24hrs after surgery; unsuitable for day surgery
  - No further opioids until at least 6h have passed
- Ketamine (preservative-free) 0.5-1mg/kg
  - Prolongs analgesia to 8-12 hours
  - Growing evidence of histopathological (neurotoxic) changes; avoid if possible, especially under 1 yr
  - If needed, S-ketamine is preferred (less neurotoxic than racemic)
  - Psychomimetic effects, especially above 1mg/kg
- Adrenaline
  - Adds little to plain caudal bupivacaine; possibly linked to spinal cord ischaemia if given intrathecally
  - With lidocaine, use 5mcg/ml
- Caudals with additives need acute pain service review the next day

## Complications
- Failure: commoner under 10 kg, where the hiatus is hard to find in about 10%
- Motor block (poorly tolerated awake; e.g. cannot walk after day surgery)
- Urinary retention (almost always settles without treatment)
- Inadvertent dural puncture and spinal anaesthesia; under GA suspect it if non-reactive dilated pupils appear
- Intravascular injection (including intraosseous) and LAST
- Hypotension uncommon: haemodynamic effects are weak or absent in children, so no fluid preload or vasoconstrictor needed
- Sacral osteomyelitis: rare (one case report)

## Caudal catheter technique
- Gives thoracic epidural analgesia in an anaesthetised infant without an intervertebral approach
- Infant epidural fat is loculated with gaps between lobules, so a catheter from the hiatus threads up to the thoracic region
  - Reaches the thoracic target in 85% of premature neonates and 95% of term infants
  - Less reliable with age: catheter coils in the lumbar region (lumbar lordosis develops, fat becomes denser and fibrous)
  - Under 2yrs the back has a single curve, so threading is predictable even to the thoracic spine
- Insertion
  - Tuohy needle (can be awkward), IV cannula or a purpose-made blunt introducer
  - Thread to the estimated length for the target roots; advances more easily in infants
- Confirming tip position
  - X-ray screening
  - ECG: saline-filled catheter with a metal hub acts as a unipolar electrode; when its trace matches a surface electrode at the target level, the tip is there
  - Motor root stimulation via a styletted catheter and nerve stimulator; watch the truncal motor response (unparalysed patient)
  - Handheld ultrasound shows the catheter as it is threaded; likely to grow in use
- Secure dressing (Steri-Strips and Tegaderm under Hypafix) to keep out faecal contamination; tunnelling is an option
- Infuse bupivacaine or levobupivacaine at standard epidural rates
- Issues
  - More colonisation (Gram-negative organisms) than thoracolumbar catheters, but infection is very rare with short use (under 72 hours)
  - Neonates: no additives, and remove the catheter within 48 hr (local anaesthetic accumulates in plasma and cannot be monitored)

## Adult caudal
- Volume 15-20ml for lower limb and perineal surgery

## Flashcards
- Q: Armitage volumes for caudal block?
  - A: 0.5 mL/kg sacral, 1.0 mL/kg lower thoracic and upper lumbar, 1.25 mL/kg mid-thoracic (T6)
- Q: Caudal clonidine dose and age limit?
  - A: 1-2 mcg/kg; avoid under 3 months (apnoea); hypotension over 2 mcg/kg
- Q: Where does the dural sac end at birth and by 3 years?
  - A: S3/4 at birth, S2 by 3 years
- Q: How far into the epidural space may a caudal needle be advanced?
  - A: No more than 2-3 mm (venous puncture and dural tap risk)
- Q: Rules for a caudal catheter in neonates?
  - A: No additives; remove within 48 hr
