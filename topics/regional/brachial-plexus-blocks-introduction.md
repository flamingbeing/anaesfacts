# Brachial Plexus Blocks Introduction

Overview of brachial plexus anatomy and how the level of block (roots and trunks down to terminal branches) sets coverage, set-up and complications.

## Anatomy
- Formed from the C5 to T1 roots
- Trunks above the clavicle: superior (C5, C6), middle (C7), inferior (C8, T1)
  - Close together as they pass under the clavicle, so easy to block here
- Each trunk splits into an anterior and a posterior division behind the mid clavicle
- Below the clavicle the divisions form cords next to the axillary artery: lateral (C5-C7), posterior (C5-T1), medial (C8, T1)
  - Lateral cord: anterior divisions of superior and middle trunks
  - Medial cord: anterior division of inferior trunk
  - Posterior cord: posterior divisions of all 3 trunks
- Terminal branches arise from the cords in the axilla
  - Musculocutaneous: from the lateral cord; pierces coracobrachialis high in the axilla
  - Median: from medial and lateral cords; usually superficial to the axillary artery
  - Ulnar: from the medial cord; usually lateral to the axillary artery
  - Radial: from the posterior cord; deep and lateral to the axillary artery
  - Axillary: from the posterior cord; runs posteriorly

## Approaches
- Interscalene: roots and trunks between anterior and middle scalene
- Supraclavicular: trunks and divisions lateral to the subclavian artery (not carotid), both between anterior and middle scalene
- Infraclavicular (including costoclavicular and retroclavicular): cords around the axillary artery, deep to both pectoral muscles
- Axillary: terminal branches around the axillary artery, plus musculocutaneous nerve

## Distribution and indications
- Interscalene: proximal upper limb, from the distal clavicle, shoulder joint and proximal humerus
  - Inferior trunk (C8-T1) usually spared
- Superior trunk block: shoulder joint and proximal humerus
- Supraclavicular: mid-humerus to fingertips, with or without the shoulder
  - Less suited to shoulder surgery: the suprascapular nerve leaves early and may be missed
  - May miss the "corner pocket" (inferior trunk and ulnar nerve)
- Infraclavicular: mid-humerus to fingertips
  - Medial cord may be spared if spread around the artery is poor
- Axillary: mid-humerus to fingertips
  - Covers median, radial and ulnar, with or without musculocutaneous; not the axillary nerve
- No brachial plexus block covers the proximal medial arm skin (intercostobrachial nerve, T2); add a subcutaneous injection just below the axilla if needed

## Equipment
- 50mm needle for interscalene, supraclavicular and axillary blocks
- 80mm needle for infraclavicular block

## Positioning
- Interscalene and supraclavicular
  - Head turned to the opposite side, ipsilateral arm adducted
  - Towel roll under the shoulder if more room is needed for the needle
- Infraclavicular and axillary
  - Head turned to the opposite side
  - Arm abducted to 90 degrees, elbow flexed

## Complications
- All blocks
  - Immediate: local anaesthetic systemic toxicity; check the total dose is below the toxic dose before starting
  - Late: nerve or plexus injury (neurapraxia, neurotmesis) from intraneural injection, local anaesthetic neurotoxicity, haematoma or needle trauma
- Interscalene
  - Ipsilateral phrenic nerve palsy (hemidiaphragm paralysis)
  - Horner syndrome
  - Hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve block)
  - Epidural spread
  - Vascular injury, including vertebral artery puncture
  - Inferior trunk often spared
- Superior trunk
  - Phrenic nerve palsy, less often than interscalene
  - Dorsal scapular nerve injury (it runs through middle scalene, where the needle goes)
- Supraclavicular
  - Main risks: pneumothorax, subclavian artery puncture and intravascular injection
  - Phrenic nerve palsy still possible, but much less than interscalene; other proximal spread risks as for interscalene
  - Inferior trunk or ulnar sparing; suprascapular sparing
- Infraclavicular
  - Pneumothorax; axillary artery or vein puncture and intravascular injection
  - Medial cord sparing
- Axillary
  - Main risk: axillary vessel puncture and intravascular injection
  - Almost no pneumothorax risk: the safest brachial plexus block
  - Musculocutaneous nerve may be missed and must be blocked separately

## Flashcards
- Q: Which trunk is usually spared by an interscalene block?
  - A: Inferior trunk (C8-T1)
- Q: Which area is never covered by any brachial plexus block?
  - A: Proximal medial arm skin (intercostobrachial nerve, T2)
- Q: Needle lengths for brachial plexus blocks?
  - A: 50mm for interscalene, supraclavicular and axillary; 80mm for infraclavicular
- Q: Safest brachial plexus block and its usual gap?
  - A: Axillary; musculocutaneous nerve must be blocked separately
- Q: Why is supraclavicular block less ideal for shoulder surgery?
  - A: The suprascapular nerve leaves proximally and may be spared
