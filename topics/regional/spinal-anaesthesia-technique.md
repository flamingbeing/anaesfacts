# Spinal Anaesthesia Technique

Step-by-step spinal technique, approaches, positioning and the factors that set block height, quality and duration.

## Preparation
- Baseline vitals; set the BP cycling interval; check the IV drip is running
- Position for both patient and operator; identify the interspace
  - Palpate up and down the spine to judge its shape (e.g. scoliosis)
  - Good positioning is essential to open the interspace
- Scrub while the nurse opens the spinal set
  - Strict asepsis: sterile gloves, gown, mask, cap
  - If antibiotic prophylaxis is needed for surgical site infection, give it before the spinal needle goes in

## Skin preparation
- Clean with antiseptic, then drape
- Method
  - Soak about 3 gauzes in chlorhexidine
  - Hold the pot out for the nurse to pour; cover the rest of the set while pouring
  - Clip the holder to the side of the tray after cleaning
  - Do not spill chlorhexidine onto the sterile tray (neurotoxic)
  - Usually prepare L3/4 and L4/5
- Chlorhexidine (e.g. Hexodane 2%: chlorhexidine 2% with methylated spirit 70%)
  - More bactericidal than iodine
  - More neurotoxic if it contaminates equipment
    - Some studies suggest chlorhexidine 0.5% to reduce this
    - Safe handling matters most: keep the pot away from the needle set; discard after use
  - Dries faster
  - Fainter tint: areas may be missed
- Povidone iodine 10%
  - Less bactericidal
  - Less neurotoxic
  - Visible tint shows all areas are covered (useful on dark skin or in poor light)
  - Does not rub off marker ink
  - Dries a little slower (2-3 mins)

## Drug preparation
- Bupivacaine: about 2.2-2.3ml (shake the ampoule first)
- Lignocaine for skin: about 4ml (nurse to help)
- Fentanyl: about 10mcg, drawn with a fine needle and insulin syringe
- Check every neuraxial drug yourself: which drug and its expiry date
- Optionally dry the skin with gauze

## Technique
- Local anaesthetic infiltration
  - Numbs the track and shows the right path for the spinal needle
  - Use a blue needle
  - Raise a small skin wheal; too much obscures palpation of the space
  - Find the ligament; once the path is right, the LA needle can be left in the skin as a guide
  - In thin patients, avoid dural puncture (and an accidental spinal) with the LA needle
- Choose midline or paramedian approach (see below)
- Introducer
  - Insert into the interspinous ligament along the planned spinal needle path
  - Steers fine needles, which are hard to direct through tissue
  - Stops skin fragments entering CSF (risk of dermoid tumours of the cord)
  - Not needed with cutting spinal needles
- Spinal needle
  - Pass through the introducer and advance
  - First "pop" at ligamentum flavum; second "pop" at dura into the subarachnoid space
  - Resistance changes at each layer; ligaments grip the needle
  - Cutting (Quincke) needle in lateral position: bevel facing up, so the bevel lies parallel to the spinal axis and the dural fibres
- Confirm free flow of CSF from the hub
  - With fine needles (26–29 gauge) this usually takes 5–10 seconds, sometimes longer
  - If no flow, rotate the needle through 90 degrees: a nerve root may block the opening
    - If rotating helps, the tip may be off to one side; redirect towards the bevel direction
  - Some rotate through 360 degrees in 90 degree steps to check flow in all four quadrants
- Injection
  - First check: no blood from the needle, no paraesthesia
  - Brace the hand on the patient's back to steady the needle
  - Let CSF fill the hub slightly, then quickly attach the syringe (no air, minimal CSF loss)
  - Twist the syringe on gently without moving the needle
  - Aspirate a little CSF before injecting: look for the CSF "swirl" in the syringe
  - Inject slowly, over 3–5 s or 0.5ml/s (NYSORA)
    - Forceful injection can disconnect a loose syringe and lose drug
    - Injection speed has been reported to affect block height
  - Consider barbotage
    - Aspirate CSF again midway and at the end to confirm the tip is still intrathecal
    - May raise block level
- Withdraw needle and introducer together
- Press with gauze for bleeding; spray dressing (Opsite)

## After the block
- Lie the patient supine at once, unless aiming for a unilateral or saddle block
  - Saddle block: keep sitting for at least 5mins
- Watch for early complications
  - Hypotension with or without compensatory tachycardia
    - Expect BP to fall from sympathetic block; measure BP every 1-3 min
    - Give fluids (caution in heart failure, end-stage renal failure, pre-eclampsia)
    - Have phenylephrine ready, with or without a prophylactic infusion
  - High spinal: widespread numbness, bradycardia, breathing difficulty from intercostal paralysis
    - Bradycardia: IV atropine, ephedrine
    - Total spinal: LA reaches the brainstem, causing unconsciousness and cardiorespiratory collapse
  - Others: bleeding, paraesthesia
- Check block level with ice (sensory testing)
  - The first 5-10 minutes are critical for cardiovascular response and block level
  - A first check at 30-60 seconds allows a change of position if needed
  - Peak height depends on the test used
    - Cold gives the highest level (sympathetic block), on average 1-2 segments above pinprick
    - Pinprick is 1-2 segments above touch
  - Upper limb sensory loss or weak forearm extension (C7/C8) warns of impending diaphragm paralysis (C3/C4/C5)
  - Can ask the surgeon to test before knife to skin

## Common problems
- Hitting bone
  - Withdraw to skin level before redirecting
  - Bending of the needle in skin or introducer can steer it off course
  - Make only small angle changes: small changes at the skin become large ones at depth
- Paraesthesia
  - Remove the stylet: if CSF flows and the paraesthesia has gone, it is safe to inject (likely a cauda equina root)
  - If no CSF: the needle may have hit a nerve root in the epidural space; withdraw and redirect away from the side of the paraesthesia

## Midline approach
- Advantage: each layer can be felt, making the intrathecal space easier to identify
- Enter in the midline between the spinous processes of the chosen interspace
  - Staying exactly midline is essential to feel the ligaments
  - Common teaching: aim towards the umbilicus (strictly only at L4/5); some advise 10-15 degrees cephalad
- Finding the space after bone contact: note the depth and redirect more cephalad, then compare the next contact depth
  - Deeper than before: go more cephalad to clear the inferior spinous process
  - Shallower than before: go more caudal to clear the superior spinous process
  - About the same depth: probably lamina; reassess the midline
  - Older patients often have calcified ligaments with high resistance; consider changing to paramedian
- If the patient feels pain, ask which side and correct towards the midline
- Layers: skin, subcutaneous tissue, supraspinous and interspinous ligaments, ligamentum flavum, epidural space, dura, subdural space, arachnoid, subarachnoid space
- L5/S1 is very hard to reach with the midline approach

## Paramedian approach
- Advantages
  - Avoids the interspinous route where it is hard to pass a needle
    - Narrow spaces: thoracic epidurals, elderly with loss of disc height, poor spinal flexion (works in any position)
    - Elderly with calcified ligaments
  - Accurate midline identification not needed (e.g. scoliosis)
- Technique
  - Insert 1 -1.5cm lateral to the midline, angled slightly medially (10-15 degrees) and slightly cephalad
  - Variants differ in the horizontal point (e.g. 1 cm lateral to the spinous process; 1 cm lateral to the interspace; 1 cm lateral and 1 cm inferior to the interspace; 1 cm lateral and 1 cm inferior to the lower edge of the superior spinous process), which changes the cephalad angle needed
  - All use a puncture site 1–1.5 cm from the midline
  - Contact lamina, then redirect cephalad (and medially if needed) to walk off into the interlaminar space
- Ligamentum flavum is the first resistance felt
- Layers: skin, subcutaneous tissue, erector spinae, ligamentum flavum, epidural space, dura, subdural space, arachnoid, subarachnoid space

## Patient position for insertion
- Sitting
  - Easier: higher first-pass success and faster
  - Midline easier to find, especially in obesity
  - Better spinal flexion, which opens the space between spinous processes
  - Avoids the spinal rotation that can occur when lateral
  - How: curve the back into a C-shape (slouch), hug a pillow, feet on a stool, flex the neck, push the back out against the operator's finger
- Lateral
  - More comfortable; patient more likely to stay still
  - Useful when sitting is hard or impossible: hip fracture, heavy sedation, uncooperative patients, advanced labour with a low fetal head
  - Sedation can be used more freely; an assistant may not be needed
  - Fewer epidural vein cannulations
  - Blunted vagal reflexes; haemodynamic changes better tolerated
  - Disadvantage: unilateral spread (sometimes useful)
  - How: fetal position (knees up); a pillow under the head may stop spinal rotation
- Prone
  - When surgery is done prone (rectal, perineal, lumbar procedures)
  - Hypobaric or isobaric LA preferred to limit cephalad spread and high block
  - Alternative: hyperbaric LA sitting, wait for the block to set (typically 15–20 minutes), then turn prone

## Level of insertion
- Typically L4/5, or L3/4, L2/3
- Landmark: Tuffier's (intercristal) line, at L4/5 or the body of L4
  - The cord usually ends at L1/L2: never insert at or above this level
  - The cord reaches L3 in 2% of adults, and the iliac crest line picks a space above L4 in 51% of cases, so avoid going above L3-4
  - Consider avoiding L2/3 with vertebral compression fractures

## Needles
- Pencil point: Whitacre, Sprotte, Pencan; 23-27G, with introducer
- Quincke (cutting): no introducer needed

## Drugs
- Bupivacaine 0.5%: heavy (hyperbaric) or plain (slightly hypobaric)
- Dose depends on block height and duration needed: 1.5-3ml
- Fentanyl 10-15mcg improves quality and prolongs analgesia

## Positioning after injection
- Heavy bupivacaine: sitting, Trendelenburg or reverse Trendelenburg as needed
- Intrathecal LA seems to stop spreading 15 to 25 minutes after injection; position matters most then, especially in the first few minutes

## Sensory level needed for surgery
- S2-S5: perianal surgery (saddle block)
- L2: foot and ankle
- L1: thigh and knee
- T10 (umbilicus): hip, TURP, vaginal delivery
- T6 (xiphoid): lower abdomen, appendicectomy
- T4 (nipple): upper abdomen, caesarean section
- Internal organs can need a higher level than their skin incision suggests: peritoneum (T4), bladder (T10), uterus (T10)

## Factors affecting block height
- Most important: baricity (density relative to CSF) and patient position just after injection; dose matters for isobaric and hypobaric solutions
- Drug factors
  - Baricity (most important for hyperbaric solutions)
    - Baricity = LA density divided by CSF density at 37°C (density falls as temperature rises)
    - Hyperbaric: baricity clearly above 1.0000 (density above 1.00059)
    - Hyperbaric solutions
      - Made by adding glucose (e.g. heavy bupivacaine)
      - Flow to dependent parts of the canal
      - Supine: run from the peak of the lumbar lordosis down to the trough of the thoracic kyphosis, giving a higher block than isobaric or hypobaric
    - Isobaric solutions
      - Made with normal saline or the patient's CSF
      - Little affected by gravity
      - Limited spread; thought to give denser motor block and longer duration
    - Hypobaric solutions
      - Made with sterile water
      - Plain bupivacaine is slightly hypobaric at 37’C (isobaric at 24’C)
      - Flow to non-dependent parts
    - Warming LA to body temperature makes it more hypobaric; a small room-temperature volume quickly warms to CSF temperature anyway
    - Consider shaking the vial to mix
  - Dose and volume (matter for isobaric and hypobaric, less for hyperbaric)
    - Dose is the most reliable predictor of height for isobaric and hypobaric solutions
    - Volume may also matter at equal doses (Barash: only dose matters)
    - With hyperbaric bupivacaine, dose and volume matter little unless under 10 mg; doses between 10 and 20 mg give similar heights (NYSORA, Barash)
  - Adding opioids raises block height (may extend the fringes where LA alone is subclinical)
- Technique factors
  - Position during and after injection (most important), together with baricity
    - Spread stops 15 to 25 minutes after injection; first few minutes matter most
    - Supine with hyperbaric: spreads from lumbar lordosis to thoracic kyphosis
    - Trendelenburg or reverse Trendelenburg moves hyperbaric and hypobaric LA by gravity; head-down for a higher hyperbaric block
    - Sitting with a small hyperbaric dose gives perineal block: stay sitting 5 to 30mins
    - Flexed hips and knees (e.g. lithotomy) flatten the lordosis and reduce sacral pooling of hyperbaric LA; with head-down this aids cephalad spread (can happen inadvertently during urinary catheterisation)
    - Prone jack-knife with hypobaric LA for rectal, perineal and lumbar surgery prevents caudal spread
    - Unilateral surgery: hypobaric with operative side up, or hyperbaric with operative side down for 6-20min before turning supine
      - Less sympathetic block and hypotension
      - Hip arthroplasty: hypobaric solution floats up to the operative side
  - Level of injection: matters for isobaric, not hyperbaric
  - Needle bevel direction: especially Whitacre; gives a more one-sided block
  - Injection speed: conflicting data; not consistently shown to change height (Miller)
  - Barbotage: not consistently shown to change height (Miller)
  - Epidural volume extension (see combined spinal-epidural)
- Patient factors
  - CSF volume and properties (composition affects CSF density)
    - Older age: higher block (less CSF, higher CSF specific gravity, more sensitive roots)
    - Height: no effect within normal adult range (adult height comes mostly from leg length, not spine); adjust dose at extremes
    - Obesity: raised intra-abdominal pressure reduces CSF volume
    - Possibly sex: males have denser CSF
    - Raised intra-abdominal pressure (obesity, pregnancy) compresses the intervertebral foramina and lowers CSF volume, increasing spread
    - CSF volume varies widely, so block height is hard to predict
  - Spinal anatomy: kyphosis; increased lumbar lordosis in pregnancy increases spread

## Factors affecting block quality
- Intrathecal opioids

## Factors affecting block duration
- Choice of LA
  - Bupivacaine: 1.5-2.5hr to 2 dermatome regression
  - Dose and block height explain the wide range
- Dose determines duration
  - With dose fixed, neither volume nor concentration of isobaric bupivacaine or tetracaine changes duration
- Block height: higher blocks regress faster, so isobaric LA usually lasts longer than hyperbaric
- Vasoconstrictors: epinephrine (0.1–0.2 mg) or phenylephrine (2–5 mg) prolong the block by about 25-50%
  - Mechanism: lower spinal cord blood flow reduces LA uptake and lengthens nerve root exposure
  - Risk of ischaemic complications; epinephrine has been linked to cauda equina syndrome via anterior spinal artery ischaemia

## Unilateral spinal
- Useful in the elderly (less hypotension)
- Low-dose hypobaric solution
- Keep lateral for 5–10 minutes, then supine; little evidence for more than 15 minutes
- Consider a 25-gauge or 27-gauge pencil-point needle with the opening towards the operative side
- Inject slowly to give laminar flow and a one-sided block

## Saddle block
- Blocks S2-4 roots
- Indication: perianal surgery
- Hyperbaric solution, inserted sitting
- Keep sitting for at least 5 minutes, up to about 30mins, for the block to settle

## Taylor approach
- L5-S1 is the widest interspace; useful entry for very difficult neuraxial cases
- First described in 1940
- Does not depend on patient flexion
- Technique
  - Insert 1 cm medial and inferior to the PSIS
  - Angle 45-55 degrees cephalad and medially, judged by sacral width, to reach the midline at L5–S1
  - First resistance is ligamentum flavum, as with paramedian

## Continuous spinal anaesthesia
- Enter the subarachnoid space with a Tuohy needle; thread the catheter 2–3 cm in
- If the catheter will not pass, rotate the Tuohy needle 180°
- Much higher PDPH risk (larger needle)
- Risk of accidental intrathecal injection of drugs
- Cauda equina syndrome reported with fine catheters: FDA advises against catheters smaller than 24 gauge

## Segmental thoracic spinal anaesthesia
- Spinals are traditionally lumbar, below the conus, to avoid cord injury
- MRI (supine) shows the mid to lower thoracic cord lies anteriorly, leaving a CSF space between dura and cord

## Workflow summary
- Before
  - Bring the patient in early; examine; monitoring as for GA
  - Sedation if needed
- Spinal
  - Position and palpate
  - Scrub while the set is opened
  - Clean and drape
  - Draw up LA, fentanyl, bupivacaine; mix the bupivacaine
  - Wipe off excess antiseptic
  - Landmark and infiltrate; leave the LA needle in
  - Introducer, then spinal needle; rotate 360 to confirm flow
  - Aspirate, inject, aspirate, inject slowly
  - Remove everything together
  - Wipe off antiseptic; cold spray for level; discard sharps
- After
  - Keep the patient in the chosen position
  - Monitor BP; give phenylephrine as needed
  - Continue as for GA

## Flashcards
- Q: Where does the spinal cord usually end, and in what proportion of adults does it reach L3?
  - A: L1/L2; 2%
- Q: How long after injection does intrathecal LA stop spreading?
  - A: About 15 to 25 minutes
- Q: What sensory level is needed for caesarean section and for TURP?
  - A: T4 for caesarean section; T10 for TURP
- Q: What doses of intrathecal vasoconstrictor prolong a spinal, and by how much?
  - A: Epinephrine 0.1–0.2 mg or phenylephrine 2–5 mg; by about 25-50%
- Q: Where is the needle inserted in the Taylor approach?
  - A: 1 cm medial and inferior to the PSIS, angled 45-55 degrees cephalad towards L5–S1
