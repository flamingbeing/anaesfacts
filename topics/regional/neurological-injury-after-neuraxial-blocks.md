# Neurological injury after neuraxial blocks

Damage to cord or roots after central neuraxial block (CNB) comes from trauma, neurotoxicity, infection, ischaemia or haematoma; exclude non-anaesthetic causes and image fast when red flags appear.

## Anaesthetic causes
- Vertebral canal haematoma
- Vertebral canal abscess
- Direct needle or injection trauma
  - Disrupts a single nerve or the cord
  - Warning: pain or paraesthesia on injection
- Cauda equina syndrome (CES)
  - Reported permanent injury with spinal lidocaine
  - First with high doses through continuous spinal catheters; also after single shots
  - Bowel and bladder dysfunction with leg paralysis
  - Prevention: aspirate CSF before and after injecting; some advise stopping at half the dose if CSF can no longer be aspirated
- Transient neurologic symptoms (TNS)
  - Also linked to lidocaine
  - Pain in back, buttocks and legs with no motor or sensory deficit
  - Usually settles in a few days
  - More common in lithotomy
- Chemical (aseptic) arachnoiditis
  - Inflammation of the arachnoid and subarachnoid space
  - Variable presentation, often pain with progressive paraesthesia, numbness or leg weakness
  - Prevention: preservative-free drugs; low-concentration chlorhexidine (0.5%) left to dry before needling
  - Linked with chlorhexidine contaminating needles
- Bacterial meningitis
  - Prevention: strict asepsis, full PPE
- Spinal cord ischaemia
  - Proposed causes: prolonged hypotension, vasoconstrictors added to local anaesthetic, haematoma compressing arterial supply

## Non-anaesthetic causes
- Surgical injury
- Obstetric
  - Compressive neuropathy
    - About 1:100 births
    - Fetal head pressing on the lumbosacral trunk, positioning, or instrumental delivery
    - Lasts 6 weeks to 2 months; almost all resolve in that time
  - Ischaemic neuropathy
    - 1:500,000 births
    - Prolonged hypotension or fetal head obstructing the internal iliac arteries in long labour

## Prevention
- Explain and document the risks
- Examine and document any existing deficit before the block
- Consider anticoagulation, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, infection and sepsis
- Asepsis: handwash, hat, mask, gloves, gown, 0.5% chlorhexidine
  - Prepare chlorhexidine away from needles; let it dry on the skin
  - Do not touch equipment that enters the patient
- Level: lowest palpable space; avoid above L3,4 (anaesthetists often misjudge the level, usually one space too high)
- Persistent pain on needle insertion, catheter placement or injection: withdraw the needle or stop injecting
- Position carefully to prevent compressive neuropathy
- Review after surgery; test leg strength (Bromage scale)
- Avoid prolonged epidural catheters; inspect the site daily
- Any deficit: assess promptly (within 30 minutes) with senior anaesthetist review

## Assessment of a new deficit
- History
  - Predisposing conditions: backache, obesity, disc disease, diabetes, malignancy, coagulopathy, infection, previous trauma
  - Drugs: especially anticoagulants, steroids, hypoglycaemics
  - Block details: type, difficulty, possible dural puncture, bloody tap, spinal catheter, drug type, baricity and concentration, additives, asepsis, site, pain or paraesthesia during the procedure
  - Current symptoms: pain, altered or absent sensation, weakness, sphincter dysfunction
  - Red flags
    - Sudden severe back pain
    - Radicular leg pain
    - Leg numbness and weakness
    - Bladder or anal dysfunction
- Examination
  - Full neurological examination and back examination
  - Assess leg dysfunction without delay and exclude limb- or life-threatening causes
  - Worsening symptoms, or onset after a symptom-free gap, suggest an enlarging mass (haematoma or abscess): take seriously
- Management
  - Any red flag: urgent spinal MRI to exclude epidural abscess or haematoma
  - Rapid neurosurgical referral for possible decompression
  - Permanent injury sets in between 6 and 12 hours from symptom onset

## Flashcards
- Q: Which local anaesthetic is linked to both cauda equina syndrome and TNS after spinal?
  - A: Lidocaine
- Q: Chlorhexidine concentration for neuraxial asepsis?
  - A: 0.5%, allowed to dry before needling
- Q: How fast should a new deficit after neuraxial block be assessed?
  - A: Within 30 minutes, by a senior anaesthetist
- Q: Time window before compression from haematoma or abscess causes permanent injury?
  - A: 6 to 12 hours from symptom onset
- Q: Incidence of obstetric compressive neuropathy?
  - A: About 1:100 births
