# Epidural Anaesthesia

Catheter in the epidural space for continuous or intermittent drug delivery, for up to several days after surgery.

## Indications
- Perioperative analgesia, and labour or peripartum analgesia (especially in pre-eclampsia)
- Supplement to GA, such as major abdominal surgery
- Primary anaesthetic: abdominal, thoracic and lower limb surgery possible, with technique varying by level

## Limitations
- Ambulatory surgery: spinal or peripheral nerve blocks usually preferred
  - Epidural is slower in onset, and brings urinary retention, prolonged immobility, PDPH and later discharge
- Children: usually placed under anaesthesia, especially caudal

## Types
- Lumbar epidural
- Combined spinal-epidural (CSE)
- Thoracic epidural
- Caudal

## Comparisons
- Physiological effects, contraindications and use as primary anaesthetic versus GA: similar to spinal anaesthesia
- Versus peripheral nerve blocks for analgesia: epidurals usually have more side effects

## Surface landmarks for spinal level
- Vertebra prominens: C7
- Root of the spine of the scapula: T3
- Inferior angle of the scapula: T7
- Rib margin: L1
- Top of the iliac crest: L4

## Test dose
- Purpose: exclude intravascular or intrathecal catheter placement
- Aspirate first, looking for blood or CSF
- Then give a small test dose of 3 ml; assess at once and every 3-5 min after
  - Classic test dose: 3 ml of 1.5% lidocaine with 15 mcg adrenaline (NYSORA)
- Check sensory and motor onset and level: some onset expected, but not too fast
  - Significant motor block suggests intrathecal placement
- Ask about systemic symptoms: dizziness, tinnitus, metallic taste, palpitations
- With adrenaline: look for tachycardia, such as a rise of 10 beats/min within 1 min

## Dosing
- Load in increments of 3-5 ml every 3-5 min, with the same precautions
  - The 3-5 min gap allows assessment of response and avoids systemic toxicity
- Treat top-ups like loading doses: confirm catheter position first
- Volume of local anaesthetic determines spread
  - Lumbar: 1-2 ml per segment to be blocked
  - Thoracic: 0.7 ml per segment
  - Caudal: 3 ml per segment

## Factors affecting spread
- Injectate concentration and volume
- Injection site
  - Densest and fastest block at the injection site
  - Place the catheter at the dermatomes of the incision, so a lower dose works and side effects fall
  - Thoracic: spreads evenly but is resisted in the lumbar region by larger roots, allowing a segmental thoracic block
    - Spares lumbar and sacral segments, limiting sympathetic block, hypotension, bladder dysfunction and leg weakness
  - Lumbar: spreads more cranially than caudally
    - Onset is delayed at L5-S1 because these roots are larger
- Age
  - Dose needed falls with age
  - Proposed reasons: narrower intervertebral foramina, less epidural fat, changed epidural compliance
- Weight
  - Morbid obesity: raised intra-abdominal pressure compresses the epidural space, so a given dose blocks higher
- Height
  - Short patients (5 ft 2 in or less): commonly reduce to 1 ml per segment instead of 2 ml
  - Bromage: add 0.1 ml per segment for each 2 in of height above 5 ft
  - Safest: incremental doses with monitoring of effect
- Pregnancy
  - Greater sensitivity to local anaesthetics and general anaesthetics
  - Possible roles for progesterone and endorphins; evidence on spread is conflicting

## Factors that do not affect spread (unlike spinal)
- Patient position at initiation
- Baricity of the solution

## Maintenance options
- Continuous epidural infusion: more cardiovascular stability
- Programmed intermittent bolus (PIB): better spread than continuous infusion
- Patient-controlled epidural analgesia (PCEA)

## Flashcards

  - A: 3 ml of 1.5% lidocaine with 15 mcg adrenaline

  - A: A rise of 10 beats/min within 1 min
