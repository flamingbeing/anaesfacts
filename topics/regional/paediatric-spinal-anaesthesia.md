# Paediatric Spinal Anaesthesia

Technically easy, low-toxicity neuraxial block, mainly for ex-premature infants (less postoperative apnoea) and older children with neuromuscular disease; short-lived and spreads unpredictably.

## Overview
- Less common than epidural block in children
- Technically easy
- Small local anaesthetic doses, so low risk of systemic toxicity

## Indications
- Ex-premature neonates who may be hard to extubate even after minor surgery
  - Classic case: inguinal hernia repair, common in premature infants
  - Less postoperative apnoea than after GA
  - Apnoea can still happen: monitor after surgery as for GA
- Older children with muscular or neuromuscular disease at higher risk from GA
  - Technique as in adults
  - With light sedation, sensible 12-year-olds cooperate well enough

## Contraindications
- Surgery expected to last more than 1 hour (unless an epidural is placed as well)
- Otherwise as for adults

## Anatomy
- Spinal cord in term neonates usually ends at L3, but may reach L4
- CSF volume: 4 mL/kg in infants (2 mL/kg in adults)
  - 50% in the spinal canal (25% in adults)
  - Greater dilution of local anaesthetic, which helps explain the short block duration
- Depth to the lumbar subarachnoid space: about 10 mm at birth, 16 mm at 3 years

## Technique in neonates
- Similar to adults
- Sitting or lateral position (personal preference)
  - Avoid neck flexion, which can obstruct the airway
- IV access is mandatory
- Midline approach at L4/L5 or L5/S1, below the end of the cord
- Short pencil-point needles exist
  - Avoid very fine needles: quick recognition of CSF flow matters as infants will move
- Onset is very fast; offset is also fast, often regressing after 30-60 min
  - Probably partly from the larger relative CSF volume
- A caudal block is often added to prolong analgesia, making it a combined spinal-epidural technique
- Assessing the block is difficult
  - Cold spray response
  - Paradoxical respiratory muscle movement
  - Loss of response to a low-current tetanic stimulus
- Spread is less predictable than in adults; high blocks are fairly common
  - Never raise the feet above the head (e.g. placing a diathermy pad) or a high block may follow
- No sedation in this group: like GA, it risks postoperative apnoea
- No postoperative analgesia from the spinal: plan analgesia for when the block wears off suddenly

## Dosage
- Heavy bupivacaine 0.3-1 mg/kg = 0.07-0.2 mL/kg of 0.5% solution
- Lidocaine relatively contraindicated: very short block and transient neurological symptoms

## Complications
- Fairly high failure rate: 10-20%
- Hypotension rare; bradycardia occasionally
- Post-dural puncture headache (PDPH)
  - Rare in neonates, but persistent CSF leak has been reported
  - Infants cannot report headache: suspect it with irritability or behaviour change
  - In older children PDPH is relatively common and probably under-recognised
  - Children may have dizziness, nausea or hearing loss more than headache
  - Bed rest probably only delays symptoms
  - Treatment: adequate hydration, simple analgesics; caffeine may help
  - Most resolve within 6 days with conservative treatment
  - Epidural blood patch only if conservative treatment fails: 0.3 mL/kg of blood, fully aseptic
  - Blood patch risks: back stiffness, paraesthesia, subdural haematoma

## Flashcards
- Q: Dose of heavy bupivacaine for infant spinal?
  - A: 0.3-1 mg/kg (0.07-0.2 mL/kg of 0.5%).
- Q: Where does the spinal cord end in a term neonate?
  - A: Usually L3, sometimes as low as L4; puncture at L4/L5 or L5/S1.
- Q: CSF volume in infants versus adults?
  - A: 4 mL/kg versus 2 mL/kg, with 50% (versus 25%) in the spinal canal.
- Q: How long does an infant spinal typically last, and what is the failure rate?
  - A: Regresses after about 30-60 min; failure rate 10-20%.
- Q: Volume of blood for a paediatric epidural blood patch?
  - A: 0.3 mL/kg.
