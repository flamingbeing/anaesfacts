# Obturator Nerve Block

Block of the anterior and posterior obturator branches in the adductor fascial planes, mainly to stop adductor jerk and add knee analgesia.

## Indications
- Painful adductor spasm
- Prevent thigh adduction during transurethral bladder surgery
- Extra analgesia after major knee surgery
- Possibly analgesia after hamstring tendon harvest for ACL reconstruction
- Does not reliably block the hip articular branches: they usually leave above the proximal thigh level of the block

## Anatomy
- From the lumbar plexus, anterior primary rami of L2–L4; runs down the medial side of psoas into the pelvis
- Usually splits into anterior and posterior branches before leaving through the obturator foramen
- Anterior branch
  - At the femoral crease: between pectineus and adductor brevis fascia
  - Further down: between adductor longus and adductor brevis
  - Motor to adductor longus, brevis and gracilis; skin of the medial thigh (very variable extent)
- Posterior branch
  - Between adductor brevis and adductor magnus
  - Mainly motor to the adductors; may send articular branches to the medial knee
- Hip articular branches usually come off the main nerve before it divides, only sometimes from the branches
- Accessory obturator nerve in 8–30% of patients: from L3 and L4, runs with the femoral nerve, supplies the hip
- Mnemonic for adductors, anterior to posterior: "Alabama" (adductor Longus, Brevis, MAgnus)

## Position
- Supine, thigh slightly abducted and externally rotated

## Distal branches approach
- Aim for a cross-section of the distal branches
- Probe on the medial proximal thigh 1–3 cm below the inguinal crease
- Identify the adductor muscles
- Nerves look hyperechoic, thin, flat and spindle-shaped within the adductor fascia
  - Anterior branch: between adductor longus and brevis
  - Posterior branch: between adductor brevis and magnus
- 5–7 mL around each branch

## Distal interfascial approach
- At the femoral crease; two separate injections, one per branch
  - Anterior branch: between pectineus and adductor brevis (or adductor longus and brevis)
  - Posterior branch: between adductor brevis and magnus
- Concept: fill the fascial planes; the nerves themselves need not be seen, only the muscles and their fascia (like a TAP block)
- Technique
  - Probe on the femoral crease medial to the femoral vein; find the femoral vessels
  - Slide medially along the crease to show the adductors and fascia
  - First tip between pectineus and adductor brevis: 5–10 mL
  - Advance to between adductor brevis and magnus: another 5–10 mL
  - Spread must be in the fascial plane, not intramuscular

## Proximal interfascial (subpectineal) approach
- Single injection in the plane between pectineus and obturator externus
- Linear probe on the inguinal crease, perpendicular to skin
- Tilt 40–50 degrees cranially until the inferior edge of the superior pubic ramus appears (hyperechoic, deep and lateral to pectineus)
- Target plane lies deep to pectineus, separating it from obturator externus

## Flashcards
- Q: Roots of the obturator nerve?
  - A: L2–L4 anterior primary rami
- Q: Where do the obturator branches lie in the thigh?
  - A: Anterior between adductor longus (or pectineus) and brevis; posterior between adductor brevis and magnus
- Q: Volumes for the distal interfascial obturator block?
  - A: 5–10 mL in each of the two planes
- Q: How common is an accessory obturator nerve?
  - A: 8–30% of patients (from L3 and L4)
- Q: Why does an obturator block not reliably help hip pain?
  - A: Hip articular branches usually leave above the level of the block
