# Topical and Regional Anaesthesia for Eye Surgery

Options run from topical drops to retrobulbar, peribulbar and sub-Tenon's blocks, trading akinesia against risk.

## Overview
- Block types: retrobulbar, peribulbar, sub-Tenon's
  - Retrobulbar was the traditional block but is now rarely used because of safety concerns
  - Modified retrobulbar and peribulbar blocks are alternatives; sub-Tenon's has grown in popularity
- No large studies compare safety or efficacy between techniques; choice is largely personal preference
- Often performed by ophthalmologists; in some settings by anaesthetists

## Anatomy of the orbit
- Irregular pyramid: base at the front of the skull, axis pointing posteromedially to the apex
  - Apex: optic foramen (optic nerve and vessels) plus superior and inferior orbital fissures (other nerves and vessels)
- Depth 40-50mm; volume about 30ml
  - 7ml is globe and muscle cone; the rest is loose connective tissue
- Axial length (corneal surface to retina): about 25mm in adults (range 12-35mm); often measured before surgery

## Nerve supply
- Motor to extraocular muscles: remember "LR6 SO4"
  - Lateral rectus: sixth (abducens) nerve
  - Superior oblique: fourth (trochlear) nerve
  - The rest (superior, inferior and medial rectus, inferior oblique): third (oculomotor) nerve
  - Full akinesia needs cranial nerves III, IV and VI blocked
- Sensory: mainly the ophthalmic division of the fifth (trigeminal) nerve
  - Corneal pain afferents
    - Nociceptors are free nerve endings of A-delta and C fibres
    - Run in the lacrimal, frontal and nasociliary nerves, through the superior orbital fissure, forming V1
    - Cell bodies in the semilunar (trigeminal) ganglion; second-order neurones run to the pons and end in the nucleus of the spinal tract
- Vision: second (optic) nerve
- Parasympathetic: from the Edinger-Westphal nucleus with the third nerve, synapsing in the ciliary ganglion, then the short ciliary nerves
- Sympathetic: from T1, synapse in the superior cervical ganglion, then join the long and short ciliary nerves
- Inferotemporal injection into the lateral fat compartment usually blocks
  - Nasociliary, lacrimal, frontal, supraorbital and supratrochlear branches of V1
  - Infraorbital branch of the maxillary division
- Medial compartment injection (needle between caruncle and medial canthus) usually blocks
  - Medial branches of the nasociliary nerve, long ciliary nerves, infratrochlear nerve
  - Medial parts of the supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves

## Blood supply
- Ophthalmic artery (branch of the internal carotid)
  - Enters through the optic canal, inferolateral to the optic nerve, inside its meningeal sheath
  - Tortuous in elderly and hypertensive patients: prone to needle injury and heavy bleeding
- Venous drainage: superior and inferior ophthalmic veins

## Techniques that anaesthetise the cornea
- Topical local anaesthetic
- Subconjunctival local anaesthetic
- Regional blocks
  - Intraconal (retrobulbar)
  - Extraconal (peribulbar)
  - Sub-Tenon's
- Intracameral injection into the anterior chamber

## Techniques compared
- Retrobulbar: injection into intraconal fat
  - Advantages
    - Faster, effective analgesia and globe akinesia
    - Small volume: lower orbital tension and less chemosis than peribulbar
  - Disadvantages
    - Optic nerve injury from the needle
    - Retrobulbar haemorrhage is more likely, with ischaemic injury to the optic nerve head
    - Globe perforation, optic nerve sheath puncture, systemic toxicity from misplaced drug
    - May need a separate eyelid block
- Peribulbar: injection into extraconal fat
  - Advantages
    - Effective analgesia and akinesia
    - Fewer local and systemic complications: less risk of posterior globe perforation, optic nerve injury or brainstem anaesthesia
    - Larger volume spreads to give more reliable orbicularis oculi akinesia; extra facial nerve block rarely needed
  - Disadvantages
    - Slower onset (at least 5mins)
    - Larger volume: more chemosis and a greater rise in IOP, which can further compress an already compromised optic nerve head circulation
- Sub-Tenon's: blunt cannula into the sub-Tenon potential space
  - Advantages
    - No needle in the posterior orbit: even lower risk of globe perforation, optic nerve injury and brainstem anaesthesia
    - Major bleeding rare: a good choice in anticoagulated patients
  - Disadvantages
    - Slower onset (at least 5 mins)
    - Chemosis and subconjunctival haemorrhage can obscure the field and promote later scarring
    - Leak of local anaesthetic from the entry site reduces effectiveness
    - Akinesia variable and volume-dependent
    - Orbital and retrobulbar haemorrhage, globe perforation, central spread and orbital cellulitis still reported
  - Contraindication: multiple previous eye operations with scarring (e.g. scleral buckle, retinal or glaucoma surgery)
- Topical: simplest way to anaesthetise the anterior chamber; can be the sole technique
  - Advantages
    - No needle, no injection pain; high patient satisfaction
    - No ocular or systemic block complications (little systemic absorption from ocular mucosa)
    - No chemosis or subconjunctival haemorrhage
    - Vision returns quickly after surgery
  - Disadvantages
    - No akinesia, so eye movements can occur
      - Needs a cooperative, well-selected patient and a skilled surgeon
    - Touch or discomfort may be felt; may need IV sedation
    - Short-acting, so repeated doses are often needed, especially with drops
  - Uses
    - Short uncomplicated procedures not needing full akinesia (e.g. phacoemulsification cataract surgery via a small corneal incision)
    - Fully anticoagulated patients (avoids needle bleeding risk)
    - Monocular patients (avoids risk of perforating the only seeing eye)

## Topical anaesthesia
- Drops or gel on cornea and conjunctiva: lidocaine 2% jelly, proparacaine 0.5% solution or tetracaine 0.75% solution
- Gels give higher anterior chamber levels than equal doses of drops and may give better surface analgesia
  - Gels can block antiseptics, so apply them after the antiseptic
- Topical local anaesthetic can cloud the cornea: instil into the conjunctival sac, not onto the cornea
- The surgeon may add intracameral 1% lidocaine into the anterior chamber just before starting
  - Hydrodissection pressure during phacoemulsification is uncomfortable and not covered by topical alone

## Regional blocks: effects
- Give profound anaesthesia and akinesia of the eye
- Raised IOP
  - Any orbital injection presses on the globe and raises IOP
  - Size of rise depends on injected volume, compartment volume, and speed of absorption and spread
  - Compartment anatomy varies, so even small volumes can raise tension
  - Raised pressure pushes vitreous forward into the anterior chamber and can compromise surgery
  - Watch for proptosis and check by digital palpation during injection
  - Pressure always falls with time; some wait at least 30 min before surgery
  - External compression (e.g. Honan balloon at 20–30 mm Hg) lowers vitreous pressure
  - Avoid pressures >30 mm Hg, which can impair retinal perfusion
- Local anaesthetic blocks the afferent limb of the oculocardiac reflex, so reflex bradycardia is extremely rare

## Complications
- Systemic
  - Subarachnoid spread with brainstem anaesthesia (e.g. via the optic nerve dural sheath)
    - Cardiorespiratory depression or arrest; needs airway support and CPR
  - Intravascular injection (e.g. into a branch of the inferior orbital vein)
    - Seizures, unconsciousness and cardiorespiratory collapse
- Orbital
  - Chemosis (common): settles with compression and time
  - Raised IOP (see above)
  - Retrobulbar haemorrhage (1–2% incidence)
    - Importance depends on the rise in IOP
    - Large rises can tamponade retinal artery flow, causing optic nerve ischaemia and blindness
  - Intraneural injection
  - Optic nerve damage (<1% incidence)
    - Usually painful
    - Can cause sudden CNS toxicity
  - Globe penetration (<1% incidence)
    - Can cause retinal detachment
  - Intramuscular injection and muscle palsies (uncommon)
    - May injure extraocular muscles; a likely cause of postoperative strabismus
  - Corneal abrasion
    - From a compression device, or after surgery when the lid opens as motor block wears off, exposing an anaesthetised cornea

## Retrobulbar haemorrhage
- Superficial bleeding is not sight-threatening but can cause an unsightly periorbital haematoma
- Signs: arterial bleeding causes sudden, marked, palpable rise in IOP, proptosis and upper lid entrapment
- Management
  - Tell the surgeon at once
  - Fundoscopy, tonometry and ultrasound
  - Urgent measures may be needed to lower IOP
  - Continuous ECG monitoring: the oculocardiac reflex can occur as blood tracks out of the muscle cone

## Globe penetration or perforation
- More likely in myopic patients with long axial length (>25 mm)
- Features
  - Varies from severe eye pain with sudden visual loss to no symptoms at all
  - Sudden deviation of the globe
  - Operator may feel more resistance, especially with a blunt needle
  - Very soft eye; possible vitreous haemorrhage
- Management
  - Tell the surgeon at once
  - Ophthalmoscopy or ultrasound to assess damage
  - Planned surgery is usually cancelled; refer to a retinal surgeon
  - Retinal detachment may need vitrectomy and reattachment surgery
- Worst case: unrecognised perforation followed by intraocular injection; less than 2 mL can cause globe rupture and permanent blindness

## Contraindications
- Absolute
  - Patient refusal
  - Local anaesthetic allergy
  - Infection or marked orbital inflammation
- Relative
  - Myopia
  - Unable to lie flat long enough: cardiorespiratory disease, learning difficulties, severe tremor or involuntary movements, confusion, psychiatric illness
  - Children
  - Communication difficulty (e.g. profound deafness)
  - Bleeding disorder
    - Anticoagulation is generally a contraindication, but many will block patients on warfarin with INR in the therapeutic range
  - Previous scleral buckling or orbital space-occupying lesions (e.g. thyroid proptosis)

## Drugs
- Local anaesthetics
  - Lidocaine 2%, 5–10 ml gives about 90 min of anaesthesia
    - Do not exceed 2%: higher concentrations risk myotoxicity
  - Others: bupivacaine 0.75%, ropivacaine 0.75%
  - Lidocaine 2% mixed with bupivacaine 0.75% can be used
- Hyaluronidase is usually added to improve spread
  - Commonly 1 to 7.5 units/mL; as little as 0.75 units/mL may work
  - Raises tissue permeability and spread of local anaesthetic
  - Lessens the rise in orbital pressure from the injected volume
  - Improves block quality
  - Lowers the risk of extraocular muscle injury
- Adrenaline (epinephrine) 1:200 000 is used by some to prolong the block
  - Avoided in most, on the presumption that it may worsen cardiac disease or cause optic ischaemia
  - Lidocaine plus bupivacaine usually lasts over an hour, enough for most surgery

## Needles
- 25G sharp needles are common
- Blunt needles are less comfortable; supporters claim better feel and safety, but there is no evidence they are safer
- Lengths 16–50 mm available
  - Needles >25 mm may carry more risk to the optic nerve; shorter needles do not remove the risk

## Retrobulbar block
- Now rarely used because of safety concerns
- Local anaesthetic placed inside the muscle cone
  - Needle tip usually at or just behind the posterior border of the globe, not deep in the orbit
- Deeper and smaller volume than peribulbar
  - Traditionally via a long needle (up to 50 mm) deep in the orbit
- Faster, more reliable anaesthesia and akinesia than peribulbar, but more complications
- Technique
  - Patient looks straight ahead
  - 24 mm, 25G needle inserted through the lower lid, inferotemporally, lateral to the lateral limbus
  - Bevel facing the globe, advance straight back parallel to the orbital floor until past the equator (10–15 mm from the front of the globe)
  - Redirect slightly medially and upwards to enter the cone at the level of the posterior globe
    - The optic nerve lies in the medial half of the orbit: keep the tip from crossing medial to the globe's central axis
  - Aspirate, then inject 4–5 ml local anaesthetic
  - Apply pressure to aid spread (avoid orbital compression in glaucoma)
  - Assess akinesia after 5 min

## Peribulbar block
- Extraconal: no attempt to enter the cone; developed as a safer alternative to retrobulbar
- Differences: shorter needle, different angle, larger volume
- Technique
  - Patient looks straight ahead
  - 16 mm 25G needle, percutaneous, inferotemporal, lateral to the lateral limbus
  - Advance straight back parallel to the orbital floor to about 2.5 cm
    - If bone is contacted, redirect slightly upwards
    - Tip should be extraconal, near the orbital wall past the equator but in front of the posterior border of the globe, protecting the optic nerve
  - Aspirate, then inject 8-10 mL local anaesthetic; hyaluronidase (3.75 to 15 units/mL) often added to aid spread
    - Rapid conjunctival swelling (chemosis) means the needle is too superficial: reposition (some swelling with the full volume is normal)
  - Light pressure with a soft pad or Honan balloon to aid spread (avoid orbital compression in glaucoma)
  - Test eye movements after 5 min; add supplementary blocks if needed

## Supplementary blocks
- Needed in some patients; up to 5 ml local anaesthetic
- Superonasal: through the upper lid directly above the medial limbus, angled tangentially away from the globe
- Medial canthus
  - Needle through the conjunctiva medial to the caruncle, parallel to the medial orbital wall
  - Retrobulbar injection here may endanger the optic nerve
  - A single medial compartment injection alone can give adequate anaesthesia

## Sub-Tenon's (episcleral) block
- Blunt cannula technique
- Anatomy
  - Tenon's capsule: thin connective tissue layer around the globe
    - Anteriorly lies on the conjunctiva and fuses with it at the limbus
    - Posteriorly surrounds the globe and fuses with the optic nerve dura
  - Sub-Tenon's space: potential space between Tenon's capsule and sclera
  - Local anaesthetic here spreads back into the retro-orbital space, blocking sensory and motor nerves
- Technique
  - Topical local anaesthetic into the lower fornix with a few drops of adrenaline 1:10 000 to limit subconjunctival haemorrhage
  - A few drops of aqueous iodine 5% on the conjunctiva for asepsis
  - Small (Barraquer) lid speculum to hold the lids open
  - Stand at the head; patient looks up and out ("look at my chin")
  - Inferonasal quadrant: small opening (no more than 2 mm) through conjunctiva and Tenon's capsule; a tunnel should be seen heading into the fornix
    - Some pass closed scissor tips to about 10 mm to form a short tunnel
  - Pass a blunt curved 19G, 25 mm cannula into the tunnel, tip hugging the sclera, until the syringe is vertical at 15–20 mm depth; this places drug behind the equator
    - If obstructed, gentle side-to-side movement or a small pre-injection may help
  - Aspirate, then inject 4-5 ml slowly
    - A further 2 to 3 mL may be needed if akinesia is poor
  - Gentle pressure on the closed eye for a few minutes

## Flashcards
- Q: Which cranial nerves must be blocked for full globe akinesia?
  - A: III, IV and VI (LR6 SO4).
- Q: Above what axial length is globe perforation more likely?
  - A: More than 25 mm (myopic eyes).
- Q: What volumes are injected for retrobulbar, peribulbar and sub-Tenon's blocks?
  - A: Retrobulbar 4–5 ml; peribulbar 8-10 mL; sub-Tenon's 4-5 ml (plus 2 to 3 mL if needed).
- Q: What Honan balloon pressure is used, and what is the upper limit?
  - A: 20–30 mm Hg; above 30 mm Hg risks retinal perfusion.
- Q: Why is lidocaine kept at no more than 2% for eye blocks?
  - A: Higher concentrations risk myotoxicity.
