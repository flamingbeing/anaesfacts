# Digoxin

Cardiac glycoside from the woolly foxglove (Digitalis lanata): slows AV conduction and is a weak positive inotrope, with a narrow therapeutic window.

## Presentation

- Tablets 62.5–250 mcg; elixir 50 mcg/ml
- IV solution 100–250 mcg/ml
- IM route not recommended: absorption unreliable, painful, causes tissue necrosis

## Administration

- Slow digitalisation (no loading dose)
  - Loading not needed to start digoxin in heart failure (ACCF/AHA)
  - Start maintenance 0.125–0.25 mg daily
  - Steady state after about five half-lives (T1/2ß): roughly 7–10 days
- Rapid loading
  - Usual when used as an antiarrhythmic; slow elimination means steady state would otherwise take about a week
  - Oral: 500 mcg, then 250 mcg every 6 h, to a total of 750–1500 mcg
    - Onset 30 min–2 h; peak 2–8 h
  - IV: 500 mcg, then 250 mcg every 6 h, to a total of 750–1500 mcg
    - Onset 15–30 min; peak 1–4 h
    - Maximal effect within 1–3 h; digitalisation complete within 12 h; maintenance needed after 12–24 h
  - Watch the response: omit further doses once heart rate is adequate; stop if bradycardia develops
- Measure serum levels
  - Sample at least 6 h after a dose, ideally 12 h, so distribution into tissues is complete
  - Advanced kidney disease or haemodialysis: sample at least 12–24 h after the previous dose

## Dosage

- Maintenance oral: 62.5–250 mcg daily
  - Under 70 years, normal renal function: 250 mcg daily
  - Over 70 years or renal dysfunction: 125 mcg daily
  - Over 70 years and renal dysfunction: 62.5 mcg daily
  - Duration of action 2–4 days
- Maintenance IV: duration of action 2–4 days
- Use the lower loading range if hypokalaemic, hypomagnesaemic, hypercalcaemic, hypoxic or hypothyroid (more sensitive)
- Renal failure: reduce doses
  - End-stage kidney disease: loading dose reduced by 50%
  - Dialysis: 62.5 mcg every 48 h, about 10–25 percent of the normal-renal-function dose, every 48 h
- Body weight (lean)
  - 45–70 kg: loading dose in the lower range, 0.75–1 mg
  - 71–90 kg: upper range, 1–1.5 mg
  - Under 45 kg: 50 percent of the normal loading dose
- Obesity: dose on ideal body weight
- Therapeutic range 0.5–2 mcg/l
  - 0.5–1 ng/ml may be optimal for outpatients
  - 1–2 ng/ml may improve contractility; reasonable in closely monitored ICU patients
  - Therapeutic and toxic levels overlap widely

## Mechanism of action

- Direct: inhibits cardiac Na+/K+ ATPase, so intracellular Na+ rises
  - Altered resting membrane potential prolongs phase 4 spontaneous depolarisation
  - AV node: absolute refractory period increases, conduction velocity falls
  - Heart rate falls; ventricular response to supraventricular tachyarrhythmias is reduced
  - Raised intracellular Na+ reduces sodium-calcium exchanger activity, so free intracellular Ca2+ rises
  - Positive inotropy by a non-adrenergic route; excitability and force of contraction increase
- Indirect: more acetylcholine release at cardiac muscarinic receptors (vagal activity increases)
  - Slows conduction and lengthens refractory period in the AV node and bundle of His
- In AF the atrial rate is too fast for 1:1 conduction; slowing the AV node lowers ventricular rate, lengthens coronary flow and filling time, and cardiac output rises

## Effects

- Cardiac output stays the same or rises even as rate falls (inotropy)
- SA node and atrial effects unpredictable; ventricular excitability usually increases (main hazard)
- Minimal activity on the normal heart

## Adverse effects

- Low therapeutic ratio; side effects are fairly common
- Cardiac: arrhythmias and conduction block
  - Ventricular ectopics, bigeminy, any AV block including third-degree, junctional rhythm, atrial or ventricular tachycardia
  - Caused by intracellular calcium overload, giving afterdepolarisations through calcium-sensitive channels
- "Digoxin effect" on ECG (shows the patient is taking it, not toxicity)
  - Downsloping ST depression ("reverse tick", "Salvador Dali sagging")
  - Flat or inverted T waves
  - Short QT
  - Long PR
- GI: anorexia, nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain
- Neurological (prominent in chronic toxicity): lethargy, fatigue, delirium, confusion, disorientation, weakness
- Visual: abnormal red–green perception
  - Xanthopsia (yellow vision) is classic but often absent and not needed for diagnosis
  - Also diplopia, photophobia, reduced acuity, photopsia, scotomas, blindness
- Gynaecomastia with long-term use
- Rash, rarely, sometimes with eosinophilia

### Toxicity

- Levels above 2.5 mcg/l are associated with toxicity; serious problems are unusual below 10 mcg/l
  - Severity correlates poorly with level
  - More than 30 mg is invariably fatal without digoxin-specific Fab
- Cardiac effects matter most, in both acute and chronic toxicity
  - Almost any arrhythmia except rapidly conducted atrial arrhythmias
  - Frequent ventricular ectopics (most common), including bigeminy and trigeminy
  - Bradycardia, slow AF
  - Any AV block (1st, 2nd and 3rd degree)
  - VT, including polymorphic and bidirectional VT
  - Mechanism: increased automaticity (more intracellular calcium) and slower AV conduction (vagal effect)
- AF rate control: junctional escape beats (equal longest R-R intervals) are common once rate is controlled
  - More digoxin deepens AV block and gives runs of regular junctional rhythm
  - A shift from single junctional escapes to periodic junctional rhythm usually means toxicity
- Also GI, neurological and visual features (as above)
- Often triggered by acute illness (e.g. gastroenteritis with dehydration or acute kidney injury)
- Hyperkalaemia: Na+/K+ ATPase block in heart and skeletal muscle raises extracellular potassium; key marker of acute toxicity and predicts death

### Treatment of toxicity

- Supportive
  - Correct hyperkalaemia
  - Correct hypokalaemia (worsens cardiac toxicity)
  - Symptomatic bradycardia: atropine or pacing rather than catecholamine infusions (may provoke arrhythmias)
  - Ventricular arrhythmias: lidocaine or phenytoin
    - Phenytoin suppresses normal pacemaker activity, improves conduction depressed by digoxin, and blocks Na+ channels (class I action)
- Digoxin-specific antibody (Fab) fragments
  - Indicated for level above 20 mcg/l, life-threatening arrhythmias, uncontrolled hyperkalaemia, or end-organ hypoperfusion; give in all severe poisoning
  - IgG fragments that bind digoxin more tightly than its receptor; the inactive complex is cleared by the kidneys
  - Hypersensitivity or anaphylaxis possible on re-exposure
- Gastric lavage with caution: extra vagal tone may cause bradycardia or arrest
- Activated charcoal may help within 1–2 h of ingestion; adjunct only

## Contraindications

- Ventricular arrhythmias, ventricular ectopics or VT (may precipitate VF)
- AV block without a pacemaker
- DC cardioversion: toxic levels may cause severe ventricular arrhythmias; withhold for 24 h before elective cardioversion
- Myocardial infarction (controversial)
  - Against: inotropy increases myocardial oxygen consumption, may worsen LV damage and arrhythmia
  - For: improved haemodynamics may help endocardial supply
- HOCM (outflow obstruction)
- WPW or other accessory pathway, especially pre-excited AF (speeds accessory-pathway conduction; risk of VF)
- Myocarditis
- Raised toxicity risk
  - Drugs raising levels: amiodarone (P-glycoprotein inhibition), quinidine, verapamil (not other calcium channel blockers), captopril, erythromycin, carbenoxolone
  - Drugs lowering levels: antacids, cholestyramine, phenytoin, metoclopramide
  - Diuretics (low potassium and magnesium)
  - Hypercalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypokalaemia
    - Digoxin and K+ compete for the same pump site, so low K+ augments digoxin; monitor potassium closely on diuretics
  - Hypothyroidism (smaller volume of distribution)
  - Renal impairment (adjust dose)
  - Elderly: very sensitive; adjust for falling renal function and body weight

## Indications

- Rate control in AF and atrial flutter
  - Less effective than beta blockers or calcium channel blockers; often needs one of these added
- Heart failure
  - Early gains in cardiac output may not last; other drugs may do better

## Pharmacokinetics

- Gut absorption varies with formulation; oral bioavailability 75%
- Protein binding 30%
- Volume of distribution large, 7 l/kg
  - High levels in heart and kidney; skeletal muscle is the largest store
  - Increases in thyrotoxicosis, falls in hypothyroidism
- Minimal hepatic metabolism; excreted mostly unchanged in urine (glomerular filtration and active tubular secretion)
  - Adjust dose in renal failure; no adjustment in liver failure
- Elimination half-life about 36 h; much longer in renal failure
