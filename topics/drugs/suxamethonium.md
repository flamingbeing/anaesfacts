# Suxamethonium

The only depolarising neuromuscular blocker in clinical use: fastest onset and shortest duration, the classic RSI drug.

## Presentation
- Colourless solution, 100mg in 2ml
- Store at 4°C (refrigerated) to prevent hydrolysis
- Structure: two acetylcholine molecules joined through their acetyl groups
- Decamethonium (C10), once used in the UK, is now a research-only drug

## Administration
- IV for intubation; IM possible (e.g. laryngospasm without IV access)
- Not for infusion: phase 2 block is unpredictable

## Dosage
- ED95 0.3mg/kg (BJA CME 2004)
- Intubating dose 1-1.5mg/kg IV (BJA CME 2004)
  - Onset 30sec (fastest of all)
  - Duration 5-10min; recovery starts within 3 min, complete within 12–15 min
  - Some teaching quotes duration as 3-5mins
- Laryngospasm, IV: 0.25 to 1 mg/kg breaks it; about 0.5mg/kg is typical (1/2 to 1/4 of the intubating dose)
  - 0.1 mg/kg has been reported to break laryngospasm while keeping spontaneous breathing during adult bronchoscopy
- Laryngospasm, IM: 4mg/kg (2-4 times the intubating dose)
  - Full paralysis takes 3–4 min, but laryngospasm breaks in 45 s–1 min
- Children
  - IV 2mg/kg for infants, 3mg/kg for neonates; duration about the same as or a little shorter than the adult 1 mg/kg dose (6–8 min)
  - IM 4mg/kg
  - Neonates need more because of a larger ECF and so a larger volume of distribution and faster distribution

## Mechanism of action
- Non-competitive; agonist at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction
- Initial depolarisation, seen as fasciculations
- Not hydrolysed by true acetylcholinesterase (and plasma cholinesterase is absent at the junction), so it stays bound and the end plate cannot repolarise
- The end plate stays depolarised: a zone of inexcitable membrane with raised potassium permeability blocks muscle action potentials (phase I block)

### Phase I block (depolarising or accommodation block)
- Fasciculations; no fade and no post-tetanic potentiation
- Ends as the drug diffuses away from the junction while plasma cholinesterase lowers the plasma level
- Cannot be reversed by anticholinesterases; neostigmine makes it deeper and longer

### Phase II block (dual or non-depolarising block)
- After repeated doses (>4mg/kg in total) or prolonged infusion; after a single dose in atypical plasma cholinesterase
- Volatile anaesthetics speed its onset
- Proposed mechanisms
  - Postjunctional desensitisation: prolonged agonist exposure changes receptor shape so it stops responding to suxamethonium or ACh; probably protects the cell from overexcitation and ionic exhaustion (ACh itself is broken down too fast to do this)
  - Prejunctional block: less ACh release and mobilisation, giving fade (as with non-depolarisers)
- Looks like non-depolarising block: fade, tetanic fade, post-tetanic potentiation
- Anticholinesterases can antagonise it but unpredictably; better to allow spontaneous recovery

## Effects
- Fasciculations raise oxygen consumption and can shorten safe apnoea time
- Bradyarrhythmias
  - Muscarinic stimulation at the SA node
  - Risk: children (high vagal tone), repeated doses
  - Prevent with atropine pretreatment
- Myalgia from fasciculations
  - Risk: young ambulatory women
  - Prevent with precurarisation (small dose of a non-depolariser)
- Hyperkalaemia
  - Normal response: potassium rises by about 0.5 mmol/L (normal junctional receptors)
  - Pathological: upregulated extrajunctional receptors (fetal gamma subunit in place of adult epsilon) spread over the whole sarcolemma when muscle loses neural input, stay open longer, and release large amounts of potassium
    - Burns (>10%) and crush injury: high risk from 24–72 hours after injury, up to 18 months
    - Denervation and upper motor neurone lesions (stroke, spinal cord injury with paraplegia): high risk from 24–72 hours
    - Immobilisation: modest upregulation from as early as 3–4 days
    - Myopathies and muscular dystrophies (e.g. Duchenne) and other neuromuscular disorders
- Intraocular pressure rises by 5–10 mmHg
  - Peaks at 1–2 min after IV injection and lasts as long as the block
  - Multifactorial: more choroidal blood volume, extraocular muscle tone, resistance to aqueous outflow
  - Avoid in open globe injury
- Intragastric pressure rises, balanced by a matching rise in lower oesophageal sphincter pressure
- No rise in ICP

## Adverse effects
- Malignant hyperthermia trigger
- Suxamethonium apnoea: prolonged block with low plasma cholinesterase activity
- Hypersensitivity
  - About 50% of NMBD hypersensitivity reactions; about 1 in 4000 administrations
  - Classic type 1 (IgE) anaphylaxis, whereas non-depolarisers mostly cause non-immune (anaphylactoid) reactions
  - More likely after previous exposure

## Contraindications
- Hyperkalaemia risk
  - Existing hyperkalaemia (e.g. acute renal failure with high potassium; end-stage renal failure alone is not a risk)
  - Burns (>10%), crush injury (e.g. CK already high)
  - Stroke, spinal cord injury
  - Myopathies, muscular dystrophies, neuromuscular disorders
- Malignant hyperthermia susceptibility
- Known plasma cholinesterase deficiency or previous suxamethonium apnoea
- Known allergy
- Open globe injury

## Indications
- Intubation, especially RSI (fastest onset, shortest duration); short action limits it almost entirely to intubation
- Difficult airway
- Laryngospasm

## Pharmacokinetics
- Hydrolysed by plasma cholinesterase (pseudocholinesterase, butyrylcholinesterase, BChE: one enzyme), made in the liver
  - So rapid that only 20% of an IV dose reaches the neuromuscular junction
  - Products: choline and succinylmonocholine (weakly active), which plasma cholinesterase breaks down further to succinic acid and choline
- <10% excreted unchanged in urine

## Suxamethonium apnoea and plasma cholinesterase deficiency
### Causes
- Genetic (abnormal enzyme structure); normal dibucaine number is 80; heterozygotes occur
  - Atypical (Ea): commonest, dibucaine number about 20
  - Silent (Es): no working enzyme, dibucaine number 0
  - Fluoride-resistant (Ef): less common, found by fluoride number
- Acquired: less enzyme made
  - Severe liver disease (cirrhosis or acute failure; must be advanced to cause apnoea)
  - Malignancy, especially with cachexia or starvation
  - Malnutrition
  - Renal failure (several mechanisms incl. less synthesis; dialysis removes enzyme)
- Physiological states
  - Age: neonates and the elderly have lower levels
  - Pregnancy and postpartum: activity falls by about 25% (haemodilution, hormones); usually not significant without a genetic variant
  - Thyrotoxicosis: probably faster turnover
- Drugs (direct inhibitors or competing substrates)
  - Neostigmine and organophosphates: neostigmine inhibits BChE even more strongly than AChE, so suxamethonium after neostigmine gives a very long block
  - Metoclopramide, esmolol, cyclophosphamide
  - Oral contraceptives (oestrogen lowers synthesis)

### Genetics
- One gene on chromosome 3 (3q26)
- Normal enzyme (gene E1u): tetrameric glycoprotein of four identical subunits
- Variants come from single amino acid substitutions; four alleles make 10 genotypes
  - Eu (usual): 96% of people are homozygous normal
  - Ea (atypical, dibucaine-resistant)
    - Eu:Ea (commonest variant): duration about 30mins
    - Ea:Ea: duration about 2hr
  - Ef (fluoride-resistant)
  - Es (silent): almost no ability to hydrolyse suxamethonium; homozygotes stay paralysed for several hours until non-specific esterases clear it
- Longest blocks: homozygous silent, homozygous atypical, heterozygous silent/atypical

### Presentation
- Delayed emergence
- Train-of-four: phase I gives weak but equal twitches (no fade); phase II (common in prolonged apnoea) shows fade

### Management
- Keep the patient sedated and ventilated until the block wears off; no neostigmine
- FFP supplies plasma cholinesterase and can reverse it, but is not recommended now because of transfusion risks
- Purified BChE (Cholase) exists in some regions but is rare and costly
- Afterwards: investigate, counsel, alert card, family screening (as for MH)

### Investigations
- Plasma cholinesterase activity (quantity)
  - Normal typically 5,000–12,000 U/L (lab dependent)
  - Detects acquired causes (liver disease, pregnancy, malnutrition): normal structure, low amount
  - A normal level does not exclude a structural variant
- Dibucaine number (quality: percentage inhibition by dibucaine)
  - Normal enzyme inhibited about 80%; variants less
  - Eu:Eu 83-88%; Ea:Ea only 20%; other combinations 20–80%
- Fluoride number: same idea with sodium fluoride, detects Ef
- Molecular genetic testing: gold standard; mainly for family screening or research after a severe unexplained case

## Flashcards
- Q: Adult IV intubating dose, onset and duration of suxamethonium?
  - A: 1-1.5mg/kg; onset 30 sec; duration 5-10 min
- Q: IM suxamethonium dose for laryngospasm?
  - A: 4mg/kg; laryngospasm breaks in 45 s–1 min
- Q: Normal potassium rise after suxamethonium, and when do burns become high risk?
  - A: About 0.5 mmol/L; burns >10% from 24–72 hours after injury up to 18 months
- Q: Dibucaine number in normal and homozygous atypical enzyme?
  - A: Normal 83-88% (about 80); Ea:Ea about 20%
- Q: Total dose after which phase II block occurs?
  - A: More than 4mg/kg (repeated doses or infusion)
