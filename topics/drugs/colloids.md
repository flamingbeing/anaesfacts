# Colloids

IV fluids with large solute molecules that stay mainly in plasma and hold water there by oncotic pressure.

## Definition
- Chemical: a suspension of small particles in a continuous fluid phase
- Clinical: a solution whose large solute molecules do not pass easily from plasma into interstitial fluid
  - The retained molecules exert colloid osmotic pressure (oncotic pressure), keeping water in the vessels
  - Stays largely intravascular because the endothelium is relatively impermeable to the particles

## Capillary fluid exchange
- Net flow (Q) between capillary and interstitium depends partly on two opposing forces:
  - Capillary hydrostatic pressure (P): pushes fluid out
  - Plasma colloid osmotic pressure (COP): pulls fluid in
- Supine, capillary pressure (Pc) averages about 20 mm Hg: 30 mm Hg at the arterial end, 10 mm Hg at the venous end
- Normal plasma COP is about 28 mm Hg, so the balance normally favours fluid entering capillaries, which protects plasma volume
- About 80% of plasma COP comes from albumin
  - Hypoalbuminaemia therefore shifts fluid out of capillaries and promotes interstitial oedema

## Types
- Human plasma derivatives
  - 5% albumin
  - 25% albumin
- Semi-synthetic
  - Gelatins
  - Dextrans
  - Starches

## Volume effects
- Crystalloids dilute plasma COP, so they leave the circulation
- Iso-oncotic colloids keep COP normal and stay in the circulation
  - Fluid COP of 20–30 mm Hg (similar to plasma)
  - Plasma volume rises by roughly the volume infused (range 70–130% of infused volume)
- Hyperoncotic colloids raise COP and draw interstitial fluid into the circulation
  - Fluid COP >30 mm Hg
  - Plasma volume usually rises by more than the volume infused
  - Most marked with 25% albumin: COP 70 mm Hg, plasma volume rise 3 to 4 times the infused volume
- Volume for volume, colloids are at least three times as effective as crystalloids at expanding plasma volume
  - So cardiac output improves with much smaller infused volumes

## Usage
- Replace blood loss ml for ml
- Not indicated for other losses, except acute restoration of intravascular volume (resuscitation)

## Adverse effects
- All colloids can cause anaphylactoid reactions; 20% of these are severe

## Crystalloid versus colloid resuscitation
- For crystalloids
  - No proven survival benefit from colloids
  - Crystalloids are cheaper
- Against crystalloids
  - Large volumes are needed (at least 3 times the colloid volume)
  - This drives oedema and a positive fluid balance, both linked to higher morbidity and mortality in critical illness
- For colloids
  - Better volume expansion
- A possible case-based approach
  - Dehydration (even loss of extracellular fluid): crystalloid, e.g. Ringer's lactate
  - Life-threatening haemorrhage needing a fast rise in plasma volume: iso-oncotic colloid, e.g. 5% albumin
  - Hypovolaemia driven by hypoalbuminaemia: hyperoncotic colloid, e.g. 25% albumin

## Albumin solutions
- Albumin roles
  - Main determinant of plasma COP
  - Main transport protein in blood
  - Antioxidant
  - Keeps blood fluid by inhibiting platelet aggregation
- Human albumin solution (HAS): heat-treated human serum albumin pooled from thousands of donors (a blood product)
- Available as 5% (50 g/L) and 25% (250 g/L), in 0.9% NaCl

### 5% albumin
- Usually given in 250 mL aliquots
- Iso-oncotic: COP 20 mm Hg; plasma volume rises by about 100% of the volume infused
- Volume effect starts to wane at 6 hours and may be gone by 12 hours
- Considered safe for resuscitation, except possibly traumatic head injury
  - One large study found higher mortality with albumin than with isotonic saline in head injury

### 25% albumin
- Given in 50–100 mL aliquots
- Hyperoncotic: COP 70 mm Hg (more than twice plasma); plasma volume rises 3 to 4 times the infused volume
- Volume effect starts to wane at 6 hours and may be gone by 12 hours
- Works by pulling fluid from the interstitium, so interstitial volume falls as plasma volume rises
  - Does not replace lost volume: do not use for resuscitation in blood loss
  - Reserve for hypovolaemia caused by hypoalbuminaemia
- Linked to more renal injury and death in circulatory shock, as with other hyperoncotic colloids

### Albumin: general points
- Anaphylactoid reactions very rare
- Expensive
- Infection transmission (e.g. prions) cannot be excluded

## Starches (hydroxyethyl starch, HES)
- Modified polysaccharide: long branched glucose polymers with hydroxyl (OH) substitutions that resist enzyme breakdown
- Made from hydrolysed maize
- Cleared by amylase hydrolysis in blood until fragments are small enough for renal excretion

### Features
- Molecular weight (MW)
  - High MW 450 kilodaltons (kD), medium 200 kD, low 70 kD
  - High MW lasts longer: amylase breaks it into smaller molecules that are still osmotically active
  - Fragments are renally cleared once they reach 50 kD
- Molar substitution ratio (OH per glucose), from zero to one
  - Higher ratio: longer action (OH groups resist breakdown) and more coagulopathy risk
- Preparations are described by concentration, MW and molar substitution ratio
  - Most are 6% solutions in 0.9% NaCl
  - Prefix gives the substitution ratio: pentastarch = 0.5, tetrastarch = 0.4
  - Hetastarch (most used in the United States): high MW (450 kD), high ratio (0.7)
  - Tetrastarch (newest in the United States): lowest MW (130 kD), lowest ratio (0.4); sold as Voluven

### Advantages
- Low risk of anaphylactoid reaction (lower than albumin)
- Sustained volume effect
  - 6% HES performs much like 5% albumin; oncotic pressure, and possibly volume rise, are higher
  - Can last up to 24 hours with high MW preparations such as hetastarch
  - Lower MW preparations last at least 6 hours, though the effect starts to fade within one hour

### Disadvantages
- Itch in up to 13%
- Coagulopathy
  - Inhibits factor VII and von Willebrand factor and reduces platelet adhesiveness
  - First blamed on high MW; a high OH/glucose ratio is now thought to matter more
  - Clinically significant only with large volumes (e.g. >50 mL/kg of tetrastarch)
- Renal injury
  - Linked to renal injury and death with hetastarch, pentastarch and tetrastarch
  - High COP (30 mm Hg hetastarch, 36 mm Hg tetrastarch) is implicated; mechanism unclear
  - Seen mainly in life-threatening illness (severe sepsis, circulatory shock)
  - No link in less sick patients; some studies show benefit there
- Hyperamylasaemia
  - Amylase binds HES molecules, so renal amylase clearance falls
  - Serum amylase may rise to 2–3 times normal, settling within one week of stopping
  - Lipase is unaffected

## Dextrans
- Glucose polymers made by the bacterium Leuconostoc grown in sucrose
- Introduced in the 1940s; little used in the United States because of feared adverse reactions
- Common preparations: 10% dextran-40 and 6% dextran-70, both in 0.9% NaCl
  - Dextran 70: mean molecular weight 70000
  - Dextran 40: mean molecular weight 40000

### Features
- COP of 40 mm Hg for both
- Greater plasma volume rise than 5% albumin or 6% hetastarch
- Persist in the circulation longer than gelatins
  - Large molecules broken down by tissue dextran-1,6-glucosidase; small ones excreted renally
- Dextran-70 may be preferred: lasts 12 hours versus 6 hours for dextran-40

### Disadvantages
- Intermediate risk of anaphylactoid reactions
- Coagulopathy (dose related)
  - Impaired platelet aggregation, lower factor VIII and von Willebrand factor, more fibrinolysis
  - Minimised by keeping the daily dose to 20 mL/kg or less
- Renal injury: osmotic, like HES, but rare with dextrans
- Coat red cells and interfere with crossmatching; red cells must be washed
- Raise the erythrocyte sedimentation rate

## Gelatins
- Made from bovine collagen
- Gelofusin: 4% succinylated gelatin
- Volume effect
  - Initially 80%
  - Only 20% still intravascular 90 min after giving
- Advantages
  - Cheap
  - Minimal coagulopathy
  - No renal impairment
- Disadvantages
  - Lower initial and poorly sustained volume effect
    - Suits short-lived plasma expansion (e.g. during regional or general anaesthesia)
  - Highest rate of anaphylactoid reaction

## Balanced colloids
- General term for colloid solutions with differing electrolyte make-ups
