# Benzodiazepines

Class overview: GABAA modulators giving sedation, amnesia, anxiolysis and anticonvulsant effects, but no analgesia.

## Structure

- Core of two rings: a benzene ring and a seven-membered diazepine ring (five carbons, two nitrogens)
- Activity also needs a carbonyl group at position 2 of the diazepine ring, a second benzene ring, and a halogen on the first benzene ring

## Mechanism of action

- Enhance transmission at GABAA receptors (GABA is inhibitory)
- GABA is the main inhibitory transmitter in the CNS, acting at GABAA and GABAB receptors
- GABAA receptor
  - Ligand-gated chloride (Cl−) channel of five subunits (2α, β, δ, γ; each with variants) around a central pore
  - GABA makes the channel open more often; more Cl− flows in and the neurone hyperpolarises
  - Benzodiazepines bind the α subunit of the activated receptor and boost this Cl− conductance
  - Mostly, but not only, postsynaptic; widespread in the CNS
  - The α-subunit type decides whether the effect is anxiolytic or sedative
- GABAB receptor
  - Metabotropic (G-protein and second messengers); raises K+ conductance, hyperpolarising the membrane
  - Pre- and postsynaptic in many brain regions and in the spinal dorsal horn
  - Baclofen acts only here, reducing spasticity
- Benzodiazepine receptor subtypes
  - BZ1 (spinal cord, cerebellum): anxiolysis
  - BZ2 (spinal cord, hippocampus, cerebral cortex): sedation and anticonvulsant action

## Effects

- Sedation (useful without GA, e.g. monitored anaesthesia care, MAC)
- Amnesia (useful when poor haemodynamics limit how much volatile can be given)
- Anxiolysis (useful premedication, e.g. anxious children)
- No analgesia: combine with other agents
- Anticonvulsant (useful in local anaesthetic toxicity)
- Onset and peak differ for sedative versus anticonvulsant action within the same drug

## Adverse effects

- Variable response; some show paradoxical excitement
- Mild cardiovascular depression
- Mild respiratory depression, marked when combined with opioids
- Loss of balance: fall risk
- Dependence
- Accumulation with large doses
- Overdose: flumazenil (Romazicon) 0.1 mg IV every 5 min
  - Elimination half-life 1 hr; each dose lasts about an hour, so re-sedation can occur
  - Reverses respiratory depression only partly

## Indications

- In anaesthesia: premedication, sedation for minor procedures
- More widely: anxiety, insomnia, seizures
- Seizures
  - Anticonvulsant duration depends on redistribution to peripheral tissues
  - Diazepam acts slightly faster but redistributes quickly into fat; clinically effective for only 20–30 minutes (2003 data)
  - Short action of diazepam and midazolam carries a risk of seizure relapse
  - IV lorazepam (Ativan) is usually preferred for its longer effect
- Anxiety
  - Widely prescribed; few patients with valid indications misuse them
  - Compared with older sedatives: less tolerance, less abuse potential, wide safety margin in pure overdose
  - Relative contraindication: past alcohol or substance misuse
  - Situational or generalised anxiety: low doses (diazepam 2 to 5 mg three times daily) to limit sedation
  - Sedation usually wears off within 2 weeks
  - More than 30 mg/day of diazepam (or equivalent) is almost never needed
  - Panic disorder: alprazolam (high potency, short acting) has the longest track record; clonazepam (long acting) is increasingly used
  - SSRIs are increasingly replacing benzodiazepines for anxiety and panic
  - Low-potency, long-acting drugs reduce rebound and withdrawal in generalised anxiety
  - Elderly: more sedation and psychomotor impairment at the same dose

## Examples

- Alprazolam (Xanax) 0.25 mg: anxiolytic; half-life about 11 hours
- Clonazepam 0.5 mg: anxiolytic and REM sleep movement disorder; half-life about 25 hours
- Lorazepam (Ativan) 0.5 mg at night: IM form calms acute agitation; half-life about 10 hours
- Diazepam (Valium) 5 mg at night: rectal form for epilepsy; half-life about 30 hours
- Z-drugs
  - Zopiclone: usually 3.75 to 7.5 mg
  - Zolpidem CR: usually 6.25 to 12.5 mg

## Flashcards
- Q: Site of benzodiazepine binding on the GABA-A receptor?
  - A: The alpha subunit
- Q: Flumazenil dose for benzodiazepine overdose?
  - A: 0.1 mg IV every 5 min
- Q: Why can re-sedation occur after flumazenil?
  - A: Its effect lasts about an hour (half-life 1 hr)
- Q: Preferred benzodiazepine for seizures and why?
  - A: IV lorazepam for its longer effect
- Q: Do benzodiazepines provide analgesia?
  - A: No; combine with other agents
