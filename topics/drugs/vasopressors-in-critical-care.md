# Vasopressors and Inotropes in Critical Care

Drugs that support blood pressure and flow in critically ill patients; overview of the main agents and doses.

## General issues

- Give via a central line (risk of tissue necrosis)
  - Extravasation necrosis is a risk with every vasoconstrictor infusion; that is why large central veins are advised
  - If any vasopressor leaks from a peripheral vein, infiltrate the area with phentolamine (alpha blocker): 5–10 mg in 15 mL normal saline
- At high vasoconstrictor doses, drug side effects are hard to separate from the effects of shock itself

## Noradrenaline

- Mainly alpha-mediated peripheral vasoconstriction; weak beta agonist
- Preferred catecholamine in septic shock
  - Not for better survival (mortality is the same whichever catecholamine is used), but for fewer side effects than dopamine or adrenaline
- Hypotension despite noradrenaline usually leads to adding vasopressin, though this has not been shown to improve outcome

## Vasopressin

- Antidiuretic hormone (ADH); named for its vasoconstrictor effect
- Vasoconstriction via V1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle
- Used to raise blood pressure in septic shock resistant to noradrenaline or dopamine; no survival benefit
- Plasma half-life of exogenous vasopressin 5–20 min, so give by continuous infusion
- Septic shock dose: 0.01–0.04 units/min; 0.03 units/min most used

## Dobutamine

- Synthetic catecholamine; inodilator (positive inotrope and vasodilator)
- Mainly beta agonist with weak alpha activity
  - Beta1: heart rate and stroke volume increase
  - Beta2: peripheral vasodilation
  - Blood pressure usually unchanged, but can vary
- Used to raise cardiac output in decompensated systolic heart failure
- Infusion: 5-20mcg/kg/min

## Dopamine

- Endogenous catecholamine and noradrenaline precursor; effects depend on dose
  - Low (<3 mcg/kg/min): dopamine receptors in renal and splanchnic beds
  - Moderate (3-10mcg/kg/min): beta effects in heart and peripheral circulation
  - High (>10mcg/kg/min): dose-dependent alpha effects in systemic and pulmonary circulation
- Used where renal protection matters, e.g. kidney transplants

## Adrenaline

- Endogenous catecholamine released by the adrenal medulla under stress; most potent natural beta agonist
- Alpha, beta1 and beta2 effects: dose-dependent rises in heart rate, stroke volume and blood pressure
- Metabolic effects: lipolysis, more glycolysis and lactate production, hyperglycaemia
- Drug of choice in anaphylactic shock; also used in cardiac arrest
- As effective as other catecholamines in septic shock, but less used because of side effects
- Infusion: 0.02-0.2mcg/kg/min (usually 1–2 mcg/min)
- Greater risk of unwanted cardiac stimulation than other catecholamines (harmful in coronary artery disease)
- Raises serum lactate

## Phenylephrine

- Potent vasoconstrictor with few ICU uses
- Pure alpha agonist; widespread vasoconstriction
  - Can cause bradycardia, lower stroke output (usually with cardiac dysfunction), and renal and gut hypoperfusion
- Not recommended in septic shock

## Phosphodiesterase inhibitors: milrinone

- Phosphodiesterase III inhibitor; inodilator
- Effects
  - Increased inotropy, chronotropy and lusitropy (faster ventricular relaxation)
  - Arterial and venous dilation
- Infusion: 0.375-0.75mcg/kg/min

## Oral agents

- Midodrine

## Flashcards

- Q: First-choice vasopressor in septic shock, and why?
  - A: Noradrenaline; fewer side effects than dopamine or adrenaline (mortality is the same)
- Q: Vasopressin dose in septic shock?
  - A: 0.01–0.04 units/min, most often 0.03 units/min
- Q: Treatment of vasopressor extravasation?
  - A: Infiltrate phentolamine 5–10 mg in 15 mL normal saline
- Q: Adrenaline infusion range?
  - A: 0.02-0.2mcg/kg/min (usually 1–2 mcg/min)
- Q: Milrinone infusion range?
  - A: 0.375-0.75mcg/kg/min
