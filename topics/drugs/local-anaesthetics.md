# Local Anaesthetics

Weak bases that block neuronal sodium channels; amides or esters, with potency, onset and duration set by lipid solubility, pKa and protein binding.

## Structure

- Three parts: (a) lipophilic aromatic ring, (b) intermediate ester or amide link, (c) tertiary amine
- Two classes, named by the link between the lipophilic and hydrophilic ends: amides and esters
  - Quick check: amides have an "i" in the prefix; esters do not
- Amides
  - More stable; shelf-life up to 2 years
  - Metabolised in the liver by microsomal (cytochrome) enzymes
    - Much slower than plasma hydrolysis, so they accumulate more easily in liver dysfunction or low hepatic blood flow
    - Duration relates more to protein binding
  - Allergy very rare; reactions may be to additives such as the stabiliser methylparaben
- Esters
  - Less stable in solution
  - Rapidly hydrolysed by plasma pseudocholinesterase
    - Exception: cocaine, partly metabolised in the liver and partly excreted unchanged by the kidneys
  - Higher allergy risk, from the metabolite para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA)

## Concentrations and dilution

- Percentages
  - 0.25% = 2.5mg/ml
  - 0.5% = 5mg/ml
  - 1% = 1g/100mL or 10mg/mL
  - 2% = 20mg/ml
- Adrenaline
  - 1:1,000 = 1mg/ml
  - 1:1,000,000 = 1mcg/ml
  - Commonest strength in regional anaesthesia: 1:200,000 = 5mcg/ml

## Presentation

- Made up as hydrochloride salts for water solubility
- Often contain sodium metabisulfite (preservative) and a fungicide; multi-dose bottles contain 1 mg/ml methyl parahydroxybenzoate
  - Only additive-free single-dose ampoules are suitable for subarachnoid use (glucose 80 mg/ml in "heavy" bupivacaine is the exception), as preservatives risk arachnoiditis

## Mechanism of action

- Block the Na+ channel, binding preferentially to channels in the inactivated state and stopping ion flow
- Unionised, lipid-soluble drug crosses the membrane, is protonated in the axoplasm, and the ionised form binds the inner face of the channel, holding it inactive
  - Possible extra mechanism, "membrane expansion": unionised drug dissolves in the membrane and swells the channel–lipoprotein matrix, inactivating it
- Once enough channels are bound, the membrane cannot reach threshold and no action potential forms
- In vitro, block increases with stimulation rate, as the drug is drawn to open channels

## Physicochemical properties

- Weak bases with pKa above 7.4, so mostly ionised at neutral pH
- Potency: mainly lipid solubility
  - Lipid solubility helps passage through nerve sheaths and membranes; set by the aromatic ring and substitutions on it and the amine
  - e.g. bupivacaine is more lipid soluble and potent than articaine, so is used at 0.5% (5 mg/mL) rather than 4% (40 mg/mL)
  - Vasodilator activity also affects how much drug reaches the nerve
- Duration: mainly protein binding
  - Low binding gives short action; high binding gives long action
  - Also affected by volume, concentration, lipophilicity, pKa, pH, repeat doses, vasoconstrictors and accuracy of injection
- Onset: mainly pKa
  - High pKa: more ionised drug, which cannot cross the membrane, so slower onset
  - Low pKa: more unionised drug available to cross, so faster onset
  - Adding bicarbonate raises the pH of the acidic solution towards the pKa, giving more unionised drug and faster onset
    - e.g. 1.0 mL NaHCO3 8.4% in 10.0 mL lidocaine 2% raises pH from 6.5 to 7.2
    - Also lengthens and intensifies the block
    - Less injection pain (pain comes from low pH and cold solution)
    - Little gain with bupivacaine or ropivacaine
  - Very high lipid solubility can slow clinical onset: drug spreads poorly in tissue fluid and is taken up by nearby fat and myelin
- Vasodilator activity varies and affects duration
  - Most cause vasodilation at low concentration (prilocaine > lidocaine > bupivacaine > levobupivacaine/ropivacaine) and vasoconstriction at high concentration
  - Cocaine only vasoconstricts: it blocks neuronal catecholamine reuptake (uptake 1) and inhibits MAO
  - Adrenaline or felypressin (synthetic vasopressin analogue without antidiuretic effect) is added to slow systemic absorption and prolong action
    - Do not infiltrate lignocaine with adrenaline into end-arterial areas: fingers, toes, ears, penis, nose
    - Adrenaline also warns of intravascular injection: inject large volumes slowly and listen to the pulse oximeter; sudden tachycardia means stop
    - Maximum adrenaline dose is debated; reduce in severe ischaemic heart disease
      - 1:200,000 for peripheral nerves and skin
      - Dentists use 1:80,000, in small volumes
      - Suggested maximum 4mcg/kg (i.e. 0.2mg for a 50kg person)
    - Adrenaline does not much prolong bupivacaine or ropivacaine, but slows their absorption and lowers peak plasma levels, reducing toxicity risk
- Infected tissue: local anaesthetics work poorly
  - Acidity further cuts the unionised fraction
  - Increased local blood flow removes drug faster

## Pharmacokinetics

- Absorption
  - Depends on the drug's own vascular effect, any added vasoconstrictor, and the injection site
  - Accidental intravascular injection gives very high levels and possible CNS or cardiovascular toxicity
    - Less than 10 mg lidocaine into a carotid or vertebral artery quickly raises CNS levels: coma, possible apnoea and cardiac arrest
- Distribution
  - Esters are little bound; amides are more protein bound (bupivacaine > ropivacaine > lidocaine > prilocaine)
    - α1-acid glycoprotein binds with high affinity; albumin binds more in total because there is more of it
    - Free fraction rises when binding falls: pregnancy, neonates, renal failure, acute stress
  - Protein binding affects placental transfer: bupivacaine is more bound than lidocaine, so less crosses
    - Fetal acidosis increases the ionised fraction, trapping drug in the fetus (ion trapping)
- Metabolism and elimination
  - Esters: rapid hydrolysis by plasma cholinesterase and other esterases to inactive products; short elimination half-life
    - Para-aminobenzoate is a main metabolite, linked to hypersensitivity, especially in atopic patients
    - Cocaine is the exception: hepatic hydrolysis to water-soluble metabolites excreted in urine
    - Little crosses the placenta, because of rapid breakdown
  - Amides: hepatic metabolism by amidases, much slower than plasma hydrolysis, so they accumulate with continuous infusion
    - Reduced hepatic blood flow or liver dysfunction slows metabolism

## Toxicity

- See Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)

## Uses in anaesthesia

- Local infiltration: IV cannula insertion; surgical infiltration of the incision
- Nerve blocks
- Spinal and epidural block
- Mixed with propofol

## Flashcards

- Q: What does 1:200,000 adrenaline contain?
  - A: 5mcg/ml
- Q: Which physicochemical property mainly sets onset, potency and duration?
  - A: Onset: pKa; potency: lipid solubility; duration: protein binding
