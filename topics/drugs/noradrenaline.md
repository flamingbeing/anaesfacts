# Noradrenaline

Endogenous catecholamine (adrenaline minus a methyl group) used as a vasopressor: mainly alpha-1, modest beta-1, raising blood pressure through SVR; first-line in septic shock.

## Presentation

- Clear solution, **4 mg in 4 ml (1 mg/ml)** (Levophed)

## Administration

- Dilute **2 or 4 mg in 50 ml D5** (**0.04 or 0.08 mg/ml**): single or double strength (double usual)
  - Product monograph: oxidises during infusion in saline alone; dextrose reduces oxidation
  - Oxidation speeds up at **pH above 5.5-6**: never mix with bicarbonate, alkaline-buffered antibiotics or barbiturates
  - Other studies: at usual clinical concentrations it is chemically stable for seven days at room temperature and in ambient light, in D5W or NS
- Extravasation can cause tissue necrosis

## Dosage

- Infusion 0.05–0.5 mcg/kg/min (Peck and Hill)

## Mechanism of action

- Alpha and beta agonist, **alpha predominant**
- Raises blood pressure mainly by increasing SVR
- **Baroreceptor reflex bradycardia** can make cardiac output fall even as pressure rises

## Effects

- Infused noradrenaline acts a little differently from noradrenaline released at nerve endings
- Cardiovascular
  - **Mainly alpha-1, modest beta-1**: strong vasoconstrictor, weaker direct inotrope
    - Beta-1 effects at very low doses; **alpha-1 soon dominates as the dose rises**
    - Weaker beta agonist than dopamine and the other catecholamines
  - Powerful **peripheral vasoconstriction** of large arteries, arterioles and veins, so **blood pressure increases** through **higher SVR**
  - Cardiac output: **little net change, or may fall**, from higher afterload and reflex bradycardia
  - Heart rate: minimal chronotropy; **may not rise**, as reflex bradycardia masks SA node stimulation
  - **Myocardial oxygen consumption** increases
  - **Coronary flow increases**, from higher diastolic pressure and local vasodilators released by stimulated myocytes
  - Venoconstriction increases venous return
- Pulmonary: **PVR may increase**
- Splanchnic: marked vasoconstriction; renal and hepatic blood flow fall
- Skin: vasoconstricted

## Adverse effects

- Excess peripheral vasoconstriction: ischaemia or gangrene of extremities
- Extravasation: tissue necrosis
- Prolonged infusion may injure myocytes directly (apoptosis via protein kinase A activation and raised cytosolic calcium influx)
- Pregnancy: less uterine blood flow, possible fetal bradycardia; may also contract the uterus and cause fetal asphyxia
- Interaction: although direct-acting, use with caution with MAOIs, as effects may be exaggerated and prolonged

## Indications

- **Septic shock**: first-line vasopressor
  - Not because of better survival (mortality is the same whichever catecholamine is used) but because it has **fewer adverse effects than dopamine or adrenaline**
  - If refractory, dopamine or vasopressin is often added, without evidence of better outcome

## Pharmacokinetics

- Released noradrenaline is mostly cleared by **uptake 1**: active reuptake into the nerve terminal, then recycled or broken down by **MAO** (sympathetic nerves lack COMT); this is the main route of inactivation
- **Uptake 2**: diffusion away from the nerve into the circulation (less important), then **COMT** metabolism to inactive VMA and normetadrenaline, conjugated with glucuronide or sulfate and excreted in urine
- **Short half-life, about 2 minutes**, from rapid metabolism
- Up to 25% is taken up in one pass through the lungs (unlike adrenaline and dopamine)

## Flashcards

- Q: Standard noradrenaline dilutions?
  - A: 2 or 4 mg in 50 ml D5 (0.04 or 0.08 mg/ml)
- Q: Why dilute noradrenaline in dextrose and avoid bicarbonate?
  - A: It oxidises in saline alone and faster at pH above 5.5-6; dextrose reduces oxidation
- Q: Why can cardiac output fall with noradrenaline?
  - A: Raised afterload and baroreceptor reflex bradycardia outweigh its modest beta-1 effect
- Q: Main route of inactivation of released noradrenaline?
  - A: Uptake 1 into the nerve terminal, then MAO or recycling
- Q: Why is noradrenaline first-line in septic shock?
  - A: Fewer adverse effects than dopamine or adrenaline, not better survival
