# Anticholinergics (antimuscarinics)

Drugs that competitively block ACh at muscarinic receptors; atropine is the model for the class.

## Overview

- Compete with ACh for the muscarinic receptor binding site; the block is competitive, so enough ACh can overcome it
- Most are non-selective and act much like atropine
- Little nicotinic block in general; quaternary ammonium agents block nicotinic receptors more and can affect ganglionic or neuromuscular transmission
- Quaternary agents (methscopolamine, ipratropium, tiotropium) do not cross the blood-brain barrier or membranes readily

## Toxicity (anticholinergic syndrome)

- Dry as a bone, mad as a hatter, hot as a hare, red as a beet, blind as a bat, full as a flask
- Compare with cholinergic crisis from anticholinesterases

## Agents

- Atropine
- Glycopyrrolate
- Hyoscine hydrobromide (scopolamine)
- Hyoscine butylbromide (Buscopan)
- Ipratropium and tiotropium bromide
  - Quaternary; used only for the airways
  - Inhaled action almost confined to mouth and airways; poorly absorbed from lung and gut, so dry mouth is the only common side effect
  - Ipratropium blocks all muscarinic subtypes, including presynaptic M2 receptors on postganglionic nerve endings in the lung; the extra ACh release may partly offset its block of M3-mediated bronchoconstriction
  - Tiotropium is relatively selective for M1 and M3, with lower M2 affinity, so less presynaptic ACh release
- Oxybutynin (overactive bladder)
- Tropicamide (cycloplegic)
- Benztropine (Parkinson's disease)
- Drugs with antimuscarinic side effects: many H1 antihistamines, phenothiazines and tricyclic antidepressants; in high doses they mimic atropine poisoning

## Uses in anaesthesia

- Block unwanted muscarinic effects of anticholinesterases during reversal of neuromuscular block
- Pairings matched for time course
  - Neostigmine with glycopyrrolate (both slower onset, longer acting)
  - Edrophonium with atropine (both faster onset, shorter acting)

## Effects (based on atropine)

- Heart
  - Main effect is tachycardia
  - Usual clinical doses (0.4-0.6 mg) often cause brief slowing, from block of M1 receptors in the parasympathetic ganglion supplying the SA node
    - Modest (4-8 beats per minute); usually absent after rapid IV injection
  - Larger doses block M2 receptors on SA node pacemaker cells, removing vagal tone: progressive tachycardia
    - Maximal heart rate (e.g. with exercise) is unchanged
  - Biggest effect in healthy young adults with high vagal tone; in infants and the elderly even large doses may not speed the heart
    - 2 mg IM raises resting heart rate by ~35-40 beats per minute in young men
  - Prevents or reverses reflex vagal bradycardia or asystole: irritant vapours, carotid sinus stimulation, eyeball pressure, peritoneal stimulation, contrast injection at cardiac catheterisation
  - Also reverses bradycardia or asystole from choline esters, anticholinesterases, other parasympathomimetics and electrical vagal stimulation
  - May improve AV conduction by removing vagal tone
- Circulation
  - Alone, little effect on blood pressure: most vessels have little cholinergic supply
  - Fully blocks the vasodilation and hypotension caused by choline esters
  - Toxic (occasionally therapeutic) doses dilate skin vessels in the blush area ("atropine flush"), perhaps to shed heat when sweating is blocked
- Respiratory
  - Blocks vagal airway tone: bronchodilation
  - Reduces bronchoconstriction from histamine, bradykinin and eicosanoids (via their reflex vagal component); basis for use with β agonists in asthma and COPD
  - Antisialagogue: dries nose, mouth, pharynx and bronchi
- Gastrointestinal tract
  - Antispasmodic; formerly used for peptic ulcer disease
  - Fully blocks effects of ACh and parasympathomimetic drugs on the gut, but only partly blocks vagal stimulation, because
    - Vagal fibres also synapse with non-cholinergic intramural neurons
    - Vagal gastrin release is mediated by gastrin-releasing peptide, not ACh
  - Parietal cells secrete acid in response to 3 agonists (gastrin, histamine, ACh); muscarinic stimulation of enterochromaffin-like cells also releases histamine; atropine blocks only the muscarinic part
  - Secretions
    - Saliva is especially sensitive: mouth becomes dry, swallowing and speaking can be hard
    - Cephalic and fasting gastric secretion fall markedly; intestinal phase only partly
    - Doses that cut acid also affect saliva, accommodation, micturition and motility, so H2 antagonists and PPIs have replaced them
  - Motility
    - Prolonged reduction in tone, amplitude and frequency of peristalsis from stomach to colon
    - Needs relatively large doses, as the enteric nervous system can run motility on its own
    - Antiemetic
- Eye
  - Blocks iris sphincter and ciliary muscle: mydriasis and cycloplegia
  - Topical atropine lasts long: accommodation and pupil reflexes may take 7-12 days to recover, so shorter-acting agents are preferred as mydriatics
  - Systemic doses barely affect intraocular pressure, except in those prone to angle-closure glaucoma, where it may rise dangerously
- Other smooth muscle
  - Urinary tract: reduced ureter and bladder tone and contraction; cannot be achieved without also drying saliva and tears and blurring vision
  - Biliary tract: mild antispasmodic effect, not enough to overcome opioid-induced biliary spasm (nitrates work better)
  - Sweat glands: small doses block sympathetic cholinergic sweating; skin hot and dry; temperature can rise with large doses or hot surroundings ("atropine fever")
- Central nervous system
  - Therapeutic atropine doses: minimal effect, perhaps mild medullary stimulation
  - Toxic doses: central excitation with restlessness, irritability, disorientation, hallucinations, delirium (central anticholinergic syndrome)
  - Still larger doses: depression, paralysis, coma, then circulatory and respiratory failure
  - Glycopyrrolate does not cross the blood-brain barrier: no central effects
  - Scopolamine has marked central effects at low doses (better brain penetration), so atropine is preferred for many uses
    - Drowsiness, amnesia, fatigue, dreamless sleep with less REM sleep
    - Prevents motion sickness
  - Treatment of central anticholinergic syndrome: physostigmine (centrally acting anticholinesterase)
  - Used for extrapyramidal symptoms of Parkinson's disease and of conventional antipsychotics; antipsychotics with strong antimuscarinic action cause fewer extrapyramidal effects
