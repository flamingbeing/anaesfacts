# Crystalloids

Solutions of salts or sugars whose small molecules pass freely from plasma into the interstitium, so they mainly expand interstitial volume.

## General features

- Main component is sodium chloride
- Sodium sets ECF volume and spreads evenly through the ECF; kept extracellular by Na/K-ATPase
- Plasma volume is only 25% of interstitial volume, so only 25% of infused crystalloid stays in plasma and 75% goes to the interstitium
- Classically said to expand blood volume by 1/3 of the volume given
- Urine and GI losses: replace ml for ml
- Redistributes quickly (within 15mins) from vessels to interstitium
- Excreted quickly

## 0.9% sodium chloride

- Called "normal" or "isotonic" saline, though neither term is accurate
- Compared with plasma
  - Higher sodium (154 vs. 140 mEq/L)
  - Higher osmolality (308 vs. 290 mOsm/L)
  - Much higher chloride (154 vs. 103 mEq/L)
  - Lower pH (5.7 vs. 7.4)
- Contains no protein, so plasma oncotic pressure falls slightly, with 2 effects
  - Favours fluid movement into the interstitium (Starling)
  - Glomerulo-tubular imbalance: GFR rises at once and proximal water reabsorption falls, so urine flow increases straight away; a purely local, non-hormonal effect; fluid returns to the vessels and diuresis continues until the load is cleared
- More interstitial oedema than lower-sodium fluids (Hartmann's, Plasma-Lyte)
  - Lower plasma oncotic pressure
  - Higher sodium load raises interstitial tonicity and suppresses renin-angiotensin-aldosterone, so water is retained
- Renal perfusion may fall, probably from chloride-induced renal vasoconstriction
- Total ECF increase (1,100 mL) is slightly more than the volume infused: it is mildly hypertonic to ECF and draws water out of cells
- Intravascular retention: 24% at 1hr, 22% at 6hr
  - Good for initial resuscitation; expands blood volume more than Hartmann's

## Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis

- Caused by saline's high chloride relative to plasma (154 versus 103 mEq/L)
- Strong ion difference (SID)
  - SID: difference between fully dissociated (strong) cations and anions in ECF; for plasma roughly [Na] minus [Cl]
  - Bicarbonate is not a strong ion, so it is not counted
  - Electroneutrality means a change in SID needs an opposite change in [H+]; SID and pH move in the same direction
  - Normal plasma SID 40 mEq/L (roughly 140 – 103 mEq/L)
  - A fluid's SID sets its effect on plasma pH
    - 0.9% saline: SID zero (154 – 154 = 0), so it lowers plasma SID and pH
    - Ringer's lactate: SID 28 mEq/L (130 + 4 + 3 – 109 = 28) once the lactate is metabolised; near plasma, so little pH effect
- Balanced crystalloids barely affect acid-base
  - Lower chloride avoids hyperchloraemic acidosis
  - Lactate, acetate, malate and gluconate are metabolised to bicarbonate
  - In one study, saline at 30 mL/kg/h dropped blood pH from 7.41 to 7.28 over two hours; Ringer's lactate at a similar rate left pH unchanged, as its chloride is close to plasma

## Plasma-Lyte 148

- Buffered with acetate and gluconate
  - Acetate may be better than lactate
    - Metabolised by most tissues, not just a working liver
    - Lactate stays usable as a marker
    - Acetate metabolism is well kept in profound shock; lactate metabolism is markedly impaired
    - Acetate does not alter glucose; given lactate can become glucose by gluconeogenesis and cause hyperglycaemia
    - But acetate has been linked to haemodynamic instability, vasodilation and negative inotropy in high-volume renal replacement therapy
  - Gluconate may not be turned into bicarbonate
- Contains potassium and magnesium
  - Potassium: contraindicated in end-stage renal failure and hyperkalaemia

## Sterofundin

- Buffered with acetate and malate
- Contains potassium, calcium and magnesium
  - Potassium: contraindicated in end-stage renal failure and hyperkalaemia
  - Calcium: do not give in the same line as blood products

## Hartmann's solution (Ringer's lactate)

- Mildly hypotonic: calculated in vivo tonicity about 254 mOsmol/kgH2O
- Lactate buffered
- Contains potassium and calcium
  - Potassium: contraindicated in end-stage renal failure and hyperkalaemia
  - Calcium can bind the citrate anticoagulant in stored red cells and cause clotting, so Ringer's solutions are contraindicated as a diluent for packed red cells
  - No clotting if the Ringer's volume is no more than 50% of the red cell volume
- Intravascular retention: 18% at 1hr, 14% at 6hr
  - Faster diuresis, perhaps from mild hypotonicity suppressing ADH or lower chloride avoiding renal vasoconstriction
- Contraindications
  - Neurosurgery (slightly hypotonic)
  - Liver failure (lactic acidosis)
- History
  - Sydney Ringer, British physician studying isolated frog hearts, made a sodium chloride solution with calcium and potassium in 1880 to support cardiac contraction and cell survival
  - Alexis Hartmann, American paediatrician, added sodium lactate as a buffer to treat metabolic acidosis

## Ringer's acetate

- Mildly hypotonic
- Same as Ringer's lactate except acetate replaces lactate, because large volumes of lactate might raise plasma lactate when clearance is poor (e.g. liver disease)
- Acetate is metabolised in muscle rather than liver, so a reasonable choice in liver failure

## Dextrose-containing fluids

- Volume effects
  - Dextrose raises osmolality (50 g adds 278 mOsm/L); 5% dextrose starts near plasma osmolality
  - Cells take up and metabolise the dextrose, so it effectively becomes hypotonic and the free water moves into cells
  - Spreads through all body water; only 1/12 stays intravascular, so poor for restoring intravascular volume
- Calories and protein sparing
  - Dextrose gives 3.4 kcal per gram; 5% dextrose (50 grams per litre) gives 170 kcal per litre
  - 3 litres a day (125 mL/h) gives 3 x 170 = 510 kcal/day, enough non-protein calories to limit protein breakdown
  - Once used to feed patients who could not eat; now replaced by enteral feeding or TPN
- Hyperglycaemia
  - High risk and many harms in ICU patients, so avoid dextrose fluids where possible
- Lactate
  - With poor circulation, 5% dextrose can cause lactic acid production and a marked rise in serum lactate

## Trials (balanced crystalloid vs. saline)

- SMART (NEJM 2018), ICU patients: fewer major adverse kidney events at 30 days with balanced fluids (14.3% vs 15.4%); NNT 94
- SALT-ED (NEJM 2018), non-critically ill ED patients: fewer major adverse kidney events with balanced fluids (4.7% vs 5.6%); NNT 111; no difference in hospital-free days
