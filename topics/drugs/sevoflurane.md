# Sevoflurane

Volatile anaesthetic with low blood:gas solubility, pleasant smell and moderate MAC: the agent of choice for inhalational induction.

## Presentation
- Polyfluorinated isopropyl methyl ether; achiral, unlike the other volatile agents
- Stability and water content
  - With less than 100 ppm water it can be broken down by Lewis acids (electron-pair acceptors, including many metal oxides, H+ and glass) at its ether and halogen bonds, releasing toxic hydrofluoric acid (HF)
  - HF etches glass, exposing more Lewis acid and feeding the reaction
  - So it is made with 300 ppm water (a Lewis acid inhibitor) and kept in polyethylene naphthalate bottles, not glass
  - A dry formulation (less than 130 ppm water) comes in aluminium bottles lined with epoxyphenolic resin lacquer
- Manufacture
  - One-pot method: all ingredients reacted together, then water added to 300 ppm
  - Chloro-fluoro method: the molecule is built with chlorine attached, which is then replaced by fluorine
- Also delivered in ICU for sedation via the Sedaconda ACD (formerly Anaconda) device

## Dosage
- MAC approximately 2%
- MAC40: 1.8% at age 40 (BJA 2003 iso-MAC charts)

## Effects
- Cardiovascular
  - SVR and blood pressure fall
  - Heart rate unchanged
  - Contractility unaffected; heart not sensitised to catecholamines
  - Cerebral and coronary vascular resistance fall
- Respiratory
  - Low pungency: well suited to inhalational induction
  - Predictable respiratory depression: minute volume falls and PaCO2 rises
- Central nervous system
  - More postoperative agitation and delirium in children than with halothane, sometimes lasting beyond early recovery

## Adverse effects
- Severe bradycardia and hypotension reported during induction in children with Down syndrome
- Fluoride
  - Hepatic metabolism by CYP450 2E1 produces inorganic fluoride
  - Possible renal toxicity, but no proven link with postanaesthetic renal dysfunction (unlike methoxyflurane, which also produces fluoride)
- Compound A
  - Breakdown in soda lime gives compounds A, B, C, D and E
    - More with potassium hydroxide than sodium hydroxide absorbents, especially when dry
    - Only A and B (B less toxic) are present in measurable amounts
  - Compound A is nephrotoxic at high enough levels
    - Rat LC50: 300-400 ppm after 3 hours
    - Extrapolated human nephrotoxic threshold: 150-200 ppm
    - Even at 0.25 l.min-1 for 5 hours, levels peak below 20 ppm with normal renal function tests
  - Higher levels with
    - Hot respiratory gases
    - Low-flow techniques (<2L/min)
    - High sevoflurane concentrations
    - Long anaesthetics (>2MAC.hrs)
  - Package insert advice
    - Do not exceed 2 MAC·hours at fresh gas flows of 1 to < 2 L/min
    - Flows < 1 L/min not recommended

## Contraindications
- Some anaesthetists avoid it in known renal impairment (compound A and fluoride concerns)

## Indications
- Inhalational induction
- When fairly rapid onset and offset are wanted
- Asthmatics

## Pharmacokinetics
- Blood:gas partition coefficient 0.7 (relatively low)
- MAC relatively low (1.8)
- Metabolised more than any common volatile except halothane: overall 5%, versus isoflurane (0.2%) and desflurane (0.02%)
