# Monoamine oxidase inhibitors

Old, now seldom used antidepressants that block amine breakdown; the anaesthetic risks are tyramine and indirect sympathomimetic hypertensive crises and serotonergic reactions with pethidine and tramadol.

## Key points

- Old and seldom used
- Hypertensive crisis with tyramine-containing food
- Reversible MAOI (RIMA): moclobemide
- Irreversible MAOI: phenelzine

## Presentation

- Oral; well absorbed from the gut
- Non-selective irreversible: phenelzine, isocarboxazid (both hydrazines), tranylcypromine (non-hydrazine)
  - In the US, only phenelzine, tranylcypromine and isocarboxazid are licensed for depression or panic disorder
- Selective reversible MAO-A inhibitor (RIMA): moclobemide
- Selective MAO-B inhibitor: selegiline (formerly deprenyl)
- Linezolid (antibiotic for MRSA and VRE) is also an MAOI, with the same cautions and contraindications

## Dosage

- Same doses in the elderly as in younger adults: older people often have more MAO, and age does not seem to change metabolism
- Selegiline loses its selectivity at high doses (30 mg per day), so dietary precautions are then needed

## Mechanism of action

- MAO: flavin enzyme mainly on the outer mitochondrial membrane, inside presynaptic neurones; inactivates amines by oxidative deamination (dopamine, serotonin (5-HT, 5-hydroxytryptamine), noradrenaline, adrenaline)
- Two subtypes by substrate:
  - MAO-A: mainly serotonin, noradrenaline and adrenaline (catecholamines)
  - MAO-B: mainly phenylethylamine and tyramine
  - Platelets have only MAO-A; placenta only MAO-B
  - About 60% of brain MAO is type A
- Being on the mitochondrial membrane, MAO only reaches free cytoplasmic amines, not those in storage vesicles, so it keeps cytoplasmic levels low
- Older MAOIs bind MAO irreversibly (especially neuronal MAO in the brain): more noradrenaline is available for release, centrally and in the sympathetic nervous system
  - Effects last a long time because new enzyme is made slowly
- Newer generation (RIMA) inhibits only MAO-A, reversibly

## Effects

- Raised amine neurotransmitter levels, thought to explain the antidepressant action
- Many depressed patients who fail cyclic antidepressants respond
- Also effective in panic disorder
- Animal data: higher volatile anaesthetic requirement, presumably from CNS noradrenaline accumulation

## Adverse effects

- Not first line: serious side effects, hepatic toxicity, lethal in overdose, awkward dosing
- Orthostatic hypotension: the commonest serious effect, worse in the elderly
  - Probably from octopamine, a weak false transmitter that builds up in sympathetic nerve endings and is released instead of noradrenaline; also explains the antihypertensive effect of long-term use
- Phenelzine: anticholinergic-type effects, sedation
- Tranylcypromine: no anticholinergic effects, mild stimulant (insomnia); potentially the most dangerous MAOI
- Sedation, blurred vision
- Impotence, anorgasmia
- Paraesthesia (may respond to pyridoxine)
- Weight gain (common)
- Hepatitis (rare); hydrazines can be hepatotoxic; hepatic enzymes are inhibited
- Minimal, non-seizure-like EEG changes and no arrhythmias (unlike tricyclics)

### Tyramine and the hypertensive crisis
- MAO in liver, gut, kidney and lung normally destroys circulating and dietary amines (tyramine, beta-phenylethanolamine); MAO-A in gut and liver handles tyramine
- With MAO blocked, dietary tyramine reaches sympathetic nerve endings and releases a surge of catecholamines
- Result: hyperadrenergic crisis with hypertension, hyperpyrexia and stroke, like a phaeochromocytoma
- Triggers: tyramine-rich foods (cheese, pickled herring, chicken liver, Bovril, chocolate) and indirect sympathomimetics
- Patients need a tyramine-free diet and should report severe headache, nausea, vomiting or chest pain at once
- Treat hypertension with a peripheral vasodilator such as nitroprusside; persisting arrhythmias with lidocaine or a beta-blocker
- Moclobemide potentiates tyramine less, so diet rules are looser, but all patients should still avoid tyramine-rich foods and indirect sympathomimetics (some are very sensitive)
- Selegiline for Parkinson's disease (MAO-B selective) needs no tyramine-free diet at normal doses

### Drug interactions
- Opioids, sympathomimetics, tricyclics and SSRIs: hypertension, CNS excitation, delirium, seizures, death
- Pethidine (meperidine):
  - Type I (excitatory): agitation, headache, muscle rigidity, hyperpyrexia; from increased brain serotonin activity, as pethidine blocks serotonin reuptake
  - Type II (depressive): hypotension, respiratory depression, coma; from slower pethidine breakdown (MAOIs inhibit N-demethylase)
  - About 20% of MAOI patients have had excitatory reactions to pethidine
  - Toxicity seems to need both MAO-A and MAO-B to be inhibited
  - Also cerebral irritability and cardiovascular instability
- Fentanyl, sufentanil, alfentanil (pethidine derivatives): reactions reported, less often than with pethidine
- Tramadol: avoid
- Morphine: no serotonin reuptake block, but opioid effects may be enhanced
- Indirect sympathomimetics: rely heavily on MAO for breakdown, so exaggerated hypertension and arrhythmias; may also reflect excess noradrenaline release from nerve endings
- Direct sympathomimetics: also broken down by COMT, so less exaggerated, but still use with caution
  - Prefer a direct agent (phenylephrine); receptor hypersensitivity can still exaggerate the BP response
  - Whatever the drug, start at about one-third of the normal dose and titrate to response

### Overdose
- Sympathetic overactivity (tachycardia, hyperthermia, mydriasis), seizures, coma
- Supportive treatment plus gastric lavage
- Dantrolene suggested for muscle rigidity and hypermetabolism

## Contraindications

- Avoid with pethidine and tramadol
- Avoid indirect sympathomimetics (e.g. ephedrine)

## Indications

- Resistant depression
- Obsessive-compulsive disorder
- Chronic pain syndromes
- Migraine
- Panic disorder
- Selegiline: early Parkinson's disease

## Anaesthetic management

- Stopping MAOIs 2 to 3 weeks before elective surgery used to be routine, but this was based on anecdote rather than trials
  - Stopping risks psychiatric relapse
  - Most patients on long-term MAOIs can be anaesthetised safely, avoiding the interacting drugs
- Avoid pethidine and tramadol for analgesia
- Avoid indirect sympathomimetics (e.g. ephedrine); if support is needed, use direct agents with great care, as they too can cause exaggerated hypertension
- Choose a technique that avoids sympathetic stimulation and drug-induced hypotension
- Regional anaesthesia (e.g. in parturients) is acceptable
  - Drawback: hypotension may need a vasopressor
  - Cautious approach: no adrenaline in the local anaesthetic, though no problems reported with 1:200,000
  - Benefit: less need for postoperative opioids
- Etomidate and thiopental have been used for ECT in MAOI patients without problems
- Response to non-depolarising neuromuscular blockers is unchanged

## Pharmacokinetics

- Moclobemide:
  - Fully absorbed but significant first-pass metabolism; oral bioavailability 60–80%
  - Hepatic cytochrome P450 metabolism; up to 2% of Caucasians and 15% of Asians are slow metabolisers
  - Metabolites excreted in urine

## Switching antidepressants

- Stop an MAOI 2 weeks before starting another antidepressant
- Leave 2 weeks between finishing a tricyclic and starting an MAOI

## Flashcards
- Q: Why avoid pethidine in a patient on an MAOI?
  - A: Risk of a type I excitatory serotonergic reaction (agitation, rigidity, hyperpyrexia) or type II depressive reaction (hypotension, respiratory depression, coma)
- Q: Which vasopressor is preferred in a patient on an MAOI, and at what dose?
  - A: A direct-acting agent such as phenylephrine, at about one-third of the normal dose, titrated
- Q: Must MAOIs be stopped before elective surgery?
  - A: No; the old 2 to 3 week stop was based on anecdote and most patients can be anaesthetised safely while avoiding interacting drugs
- Q: How is an MAOI hypertensive crisis treated?
  - A: Peripheral vasodilator such as nitroprusside; persisting arrhythmias with lidocaine or a beta-blocker
- Q: At what daily dose does selegiline become non-selective?
  - A: 30 mg per day
