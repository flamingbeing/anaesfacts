# Barbiturates

Barbituric acid derivatives that depress the CNS by enhancing GABA transmission.

## Mechanism of action
- CNS depressants that enhance GABA transmission

## Uses
- Once common anxiolytics and hypnotics; largely replaced by benzodiazepines, which are safer in overdose
- Still used as anticonvulsants (e.g. phenobarbital, primidone) and general anaesthetics (e.g. sodium thiopental)

## Chemical structure
- Synthetic derivatives of barbituric acid, which is itself pharmacologically inactive
  - Barbituric acid: condensation product of urea and malonic acid
  - Heterocyclic: its ring contains atoms of at least two elements
- C2 position: replacing oxygen with sulfur turns an oxybarbiturate into a thiobarbiturate
  - Thiobarbiturates: very lipid soluble, highly protein bound, fully metabolised in the liver
  - Greater lipid solubility increases potency
  - Oxybarbiturates: less lipid soluble, less protein bound; some are excreted almost entirely unchanged in urine
- C5 side chains
  - Chain length affects duration and sedative potency: three-carbon chains last longer than two-carbon chains
  - Branched chains increase anticonvulsant activity
- N1 methyl group: faster onset and offset, but more excitatory effects (twitching, lower seizure threshold)
- Poorly water soluble at neutral pH
  - Solubility relies on keto-to-enol tautomerism, which is favoured in alkaline solution

## Examples
- Phenobarbital
- Pentobarbital
- Secobarbital
- Amobarbital (sodium amytal)
  - Used in so-called amytal "interviews"
  - Does not force truth-telling; lowers inhibition and slows creative thinking, so subjects are more easily caught off guard or reveal things through emotional outbursts
- Barbital
  - First commercial barbiturate; used as a hypnotic from 1903 until the mid-1950s
  - Name from barbituric acid plus veronal (possibly inspired by chloral hydrate)
