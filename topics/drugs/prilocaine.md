# Prilocaine

Amide local anaesthetic with the lowest neurological and cardiac toxicity of the amides; large doses can cause methaemoglobinaemia.

## Presentation
- 0.5–2.0% solution
- 2% hyperbaric solution (Takipril) for spinal anaesthesia
- 3% solution with felypressin (0.03 unit/ml) for dental use

## Dosage
- Maximum safe dose 6 mg/kg (8 mg/kg with adrenaline or felypressin)
- Onset 5-10 min (FRCA.co.uk)
- Duration 90 min

## Effects
- Less vasodilation than lidocaine, so added adrenaline is not needed

## Adverse effects
- Least neurological and cardiac toxicity of the amide LAs; less CNS toxicity than lidocaine
- Transient neurological symptoms rare (less risk than lidocaine)
- Large doses: the metabolite o-toluidine can cause methaemoglobinaemia, which limits its use
  - Neonates are at special risk: their red cells lack methaemoglobin reductase
  - EMLA cream can cause the same reaction
  - Treatment: ascorbic acid or methylene blue 1 mg/kg (reducing agents)

## Indications
- Similar uses to lidocaine; most often used for IV regional anaesthesia (Bier block)
  - Rapid tissue uptake keeps blood levels low
  - Less CNS toxicity risk than lidocaine
- Spinal anaesthesia for ambulatory surgery (2% hyperbaric): onset and duration similar to lidocaine
- Dental anaesthesia (3% with felypressin)

## Pharmacokinetics
- One of the most rapidly metabolised amide LAs
- Unlike other amides, metabolised in the kidney and lung as well as the liver

## Flashcards
- Q: Maximum dose of prilocaine?
  - A: 6 mg/kg, or 8 mg/kg with adrenaline or felypressin
- Q: Which prilocaine metabolite causes methaemoglobinaemia?
  - A: o-toluidine
- Q: Treatment of prilocaine-induced methaemoglobinaemia?
  - A: Methylene blue 1 mg/kg or ascorbic acid
- Q: Why is prilocaine favoured for IV regional anaesthesia?
  - A: Rapid tissue uptake lowers blood levels and it has less CNS toxicity than lidocaine
- Q: What makes prilocaine metabolism unusual among amides?
  - A: Very rapid, and occurs in the kidney and lung as well as the liver
