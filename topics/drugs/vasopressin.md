# Vasopressin

Posterior pituitary nonapeptide hormone: antidiuretic via V2 receptors, vasoconstrictor via V1 receptors at high concentrations.

## Presentation
- 20 U in 1 ml

## Administration
- IV: dilute 20 U to 20 ml with normal saline (1 U/ml)
- Also SC or IM
- Onset within minutes

## Dosage
- Brain-dead organ donors: 0.5–2.5 U/hr
- Cardiac arrest: single IV dose of 40 units, in place of the first or second dose of adrenaline

## Mechanism of action
- Two main targets: kidney and blood vessels
- V2 receptors (Gs-coupled), basolateral membrane of renal distal tubules and collecting ducts
  - Adenylyl cyclase is activated, cAMP rises, protein kinase A is activated
  - More aquaporin 2 (AQP-2) is made, and stored AQP-2 channels move into the luminal membrane, so the luminal epithelium becomes water-permeable
  - Water taken up through AQP-2 exits the principal cell via aquaporin 3 and 4 on the basolateral side
  - Result: smaller volume of more concentrated urine; free water clearance falls
  - The retained volume raises blood volume, cardiac output and arterial pressure
  - Also: renal medullary blood flow falls; urea reabsorption from the inner medullary collecting duct increases (keeps inner medulla hyperosmolar); cortical collecting duct sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion increase
  - Also: endothelial release of von Willebrand factor and factor VIII
- V1 receptors (Gq-coupled), vascular smooth muscle
  - Phospholipase C makes IP3 and DAG; IP3 releases calcium from smooth endoplasmic reticulum, so cytoplasmic calcium rises
  - Vasoconstriction, mostly in skin, skeletal muscle and splanchnic beds
  - Also: platelet aggregation and degranulation, uterine contraction
- Possibly also:
  - V3 (formerly V1b), anterior pituitary: CNS effects, ACTH release
  - Oxytocin receptors: myometrium and vascular smooth muscle
  - P2 (purinergic) receptors: possible smooth muscle vasodilation and effects on contractility
- Synthetic analogues: terlipressin (V1), desmopressin (V2)
- Antagonists (vaptans): tolvaptan (oral, V2); conivaptan (oral, V1 and V2)

## Effects
- Normal plasma levels are below the vasoconstrictor range; in hypovolaemic shock, very high release adds to the rise in SVR
- In healthy volunteers, exogenous vasopressin does not raise blood pressure
- It does raise blood pressure when hypotension is due to vasodilation: septic or anaphylactic shock, autonomic failure, spinal or general anaesthesia
- Little constrictor action in the pulmonary circulation compared with alpha agonists such as phenylephrine
- High concentrations can reduce renal blood flow and GFR
- May have CNS effects (memory, learning, attention, concentration)

## Adverse effects
- Hyponatraemia, water retention
- Thrombocytopenia (from platelet adhesion)
- Ischaemia, especially above 0.05 U/min: digital, mesenteric, hepatic
- Negative inotropy, possibly from raised SVR and afterload
- Uncommon at infusion rates below 0.04 units/hr; higher rates bring excess vasoconstriction (impaired renal and hepatic function) and water retention

## Indications
- Vasopressor-refractory septic shock: added to noradrenaline when hypotension persists or the noradrenaline dose is high; spares catecholamines
- Relative vasopressin deficiency: sepsis, vasodilatory shock, intraoperative hypotension, CPR
- Pulmonary hypertension: little or no rise in pulmonary artery pressure compared with noradrenaline
- Cardiac arrest (see Dosage)
- Bleeding oesophageal varices: splanchnic vasoconstriction slows bleeding
- Haemophilia, von Willebrand disease
- Evidence
  - VASST (NEJM 2008): no benefit over noradrenaline
  - VANISH (JAMA 2016): early vasopressin reduced renal replacement therapy in septic shock
  - Critical Care 2012 meta-analysis of 9 RCTs: lower mortality and easier catecholamine weaning in vasodilatory shock; possibly lower mortality than noradrenaline in septic shock
  - Intensive Care Medicine 2012 meta-analysis (6 of 10 RCTs): vasopressin or terlipressin gave no short-term survival benefit in vasodilatory shock

## Pharmacokinetics
- Nonapeptide, molecular weight about 1000 Da
- Made in the paraventricular (PVN) and supraoptic (SON) nuclei of the hypothalamus, carried down axons and secreted by the posterior pituitary
- Released bound to neurophysin; the complex splits at once in blood
- Cleared quickly by liver and kidney; half-life 10–20 minutes

## Regulation of secretion
- Stimulate release
  - Rising plasma osmolality: the most potent stimulus; sodium and mannitol strong, hyperglycaemia weaker
  - Falling plasma volume (baroreceptors): less sensitive than osmoreceptors, but once blood volume falls by more than 10% it overrides osmolality; volume defence always wins
  - Angiotensin II, noradrenaline (low concentrations, via alpha-1), dopamine, acetylcholine, histamine, prostaglandins, endotoxin, cytokines
  - Opioids, barbiturates, nicotine, alpha-1 agonists
  - Nausea and vomiting, pain, stress (including surgery)
  - Hypoxia, raised PaCO2, acidosis
  - Exercise, IPPV
- Inhibit release
  - Falling plasma osmolality, rising plasma volume
  - Cortisol, ANP (partly by blocking angiotensin II-driven release), GABA, noradrenaline (high concentrations, via alpha-2 and beta)
  - Alcohol, beta agonists
- Osmoreceptors
  - Central: outside the blood–brain barrier, linked to PVN and SON
  - Peripheral: portal veins, give early warning of ingested osmolality via the vagus
  - Set point (where secretion starts to rise) 280–295 mOsmol/kg H2O; threshold from 280 mOsm/kg
  - Steep response: a 2% rise in effective osmolality increases secretion; almost none below the set point
- Baroreceptors
  - Arterial, venous and especially atrial stretch receptors fire less when volume or atrial pressure falls
  - Afferents in the 9th and 10th cranial nerves to the nucleus tractus solitarius, then hypothalamus
  - Atrial firing normally inhibits release; less firing (hypovolaemia, low CVP) increases release
  - Less sensitive than osmotic control but a much bigger maximum response

## Disease states
- Septic shock: relative vasopressin deficiency
  - Biphasic: high early, then inappropriately low about 6 h after hypotension starts
  - Possible causes: depleted hypothalamic stores; suppression by fluid loading or high catecholamines
- Cardiogenic shock and heart failure: levels maintained or paradoxically raised despite high volume and atrial pressure
  - Sympathetic and renin–angiotensin activation probably override volume control
  - Adds to raised SVR and fluid retention

## Flashcards
- Q: Which receptor mediates the antidiuretic effect of vasopressin, and its G-protein?
  - A: V2, Gs (cAMP, protein kinase A, aquaporin 2 insertion)
- Q: Vasopressin dose in cardiac arrest?
  - A: Single IV dose of 40 units, replacing the first or second adrenaline dose
- Q: Vasopressin dose range for brain-dead organ donors?
  - A: 0.5–2.5 U/hr
- Q: Above what dose does vasopressin-related ischaemia become more likely?
  - A: Above 0.05 U/min
- Q: Why is vasopressin useful in pulmonary hypertension?
  - A: It causes little or no rise in pulmonary artery pressure compared with noradrenaline
