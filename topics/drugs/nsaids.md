# NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors

Weak organic acids that inhibit cyclo-oxygenase (COX); main risks are gastric, renal (and in cirrhosis), hyperkalaemia, bleeding, asthma, and thrombosis with COX-2 inhibitors.

## Key cautions

- Gastric: give with a PPI
- Renal impairment (and cirrhosis), hyperkalaemia
- Bleeding
- Asthma and bronchospasm
- Thrombosis (COX-2 inhibitors)

## Classes

- By structure:
  - Salicylic acids: aspirin
  - Acetic acids: diclofenac, ketorolac, indomethacin
  - Propionic acids: ibuprofen, naproxen
  - Fenamates: mefenamic acid
  - Oxicams: tenoxicam, piroxicam
- By COX selectivity:
  - Non-specific NSAIDs (nsNSAIDs)
  - COX-2 inhibitors

## Administration

- Usually oral or rectal
- Some IV: diclofenac, ketorolac, parecoxib

## Dosage

- Non-specific NSAIDs (PR, oral, IV):
  - Mefenamic acid 500 mg TDS
  - Diclofenac 75 mg BD, or 1 mg/kg rectal TDS
  - Naproxen 550 mg BD
  - Ibuprofen 400 mg BD, or 10 mg/kg TDS PO
  - Ketorolac 15 mg 6-hourly IV
  - Topical: ketoprofen
  - Aspirin is not really used for analgesia; contraindicated in children and breastfeeding
- COX-2 inhibitors (oral, IV):
  - Celecoxib 200 mg BD
  - Etoricoxib 60-120 mg every morning
  - Parecoxib 40 mg IV BD
  - Rofecoxib, the first licensed COX-2 inhibitor, was withdrawn in 2004
- Others:
  - Tenoxicam 20 mg/d
  - Meloxicam 7.5 mg/d to start, can be doubled

## Mechanism of action

- Inhibit COX, so less prostaglandin (including prostacyclin) and thromboxane is made from membrane phospholipids (blocks conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins)
- Less PGE2 and PGF2α: anti-inflammatory effect
- Less central prostaglandin: antipyretic effect
- Less gastric mucosal prostaglandin: risk of ulceration
- Lipoxygenase is not inhibited, so leukotriene production is unchanged
- COX isoenzymes:
  - The key structural difference between COX-1 and COX-2 is an isoleucine/valine swap that opens a hydrophobic side pocket, a separate drug binding site
  - COX-1 (constitutive): prostaglandins for renal blood flow and the gastric mucosal barrier; thromboxane synthesis
  - COX-3: a central variant of COX-1; possibly how paracetamol reduces pain and fever
  - COX-2 (inducible): made after tissue injury, drives inflammation; makes prostacyclin (PGI2) in vascular endothelium

## Effects

- Antipyretic
- Analgesic:
  - Widely used for mild to moderate pain
  - Probably more potent analgesics than paracetamol, but with a ceiling effect
  - No physical or psychological dependence
  - Reduce perioperative opioid use
- Anti-inflammatory
- COX-2 inhibitors vs nsNSAIDs:
  - Similar efficacy for acute pain
  - Fewer GI side effects
  - Less effect on platelet function
  - Do not cause bronchospasm
- nsNSAIDs: no thrombosis risk

## Adverse effects

- Gastrointestinal: dyspepsia, ulceration, haemorrhage; give with a PPI
  - Prolonged use often causes erosions beyond the stomach as well
  - Mucosal defences (mucus, bicarbonate, fast cell turnover, rich blood supply) depend partly on prostaglandins, so are weakened
  - Bleeding is worsened by the platelet effect
  - Risk by drug:
    - Highest: ketorolac, piroxicam
    - Intermediate: diclofenac, naproxen
    - Lower: ibuprofen (below 1.2 g/d)
    - Lowest: COX-2 inhibitors
  - Short-term coxib use (5–7 days) gives ulcer rates similar to placebo
- Renal dysfunction and hyperkalaemia:
  - Renal PGE2 and PGI2 maintain renal perfusion when vasoconstrictors (renin, angiotensin, noradrenaline) are high; NSAIDs remove this, so perfusion falls and acute renal failure can follow
  - With careful selection and monitoring, perioperative renal impairment is uncommon
  - Risk factors: existing renal impairment, hypovolaemia, hypotension, other nephrotoxins including ACE inhibitors
  - Long-term use: NSAID nephropathy (papillary necrosis, interstitial fibrosis)
  - Fluid retention and heart failure
  - COX-2 inhibitors and nsNSAIDs affect renal function similarly
- Asthma and bronchospasm:
  - COX block shunts arachidonic acid to leukotrienes, which cause bronchospasm
  - NSAID-sensitive asthma: acute severe asthma in up to 20% of asthmatics; linked to chronic rhinitis or nasal polyps; usually middle-aged, children relatively spared
  - COX-2 inhibitors are safe in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease
- Bleeding (antiplatelet effect):
  - Less thromboxane A2, so less platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction
  - Thromboxane is released by platelets activated by adenosine, collagen or adrenaline; endothelial prostacyclin does the opposite (vasodilates, inhibits aggregation)
  - Low-dose aspirin prevents arterial thromboembolism by irreversibly blocking platelet thromboxane
    - Lasts the platelet's lifetime: platelets have no nucleus to make new COX, and the block is irreversible
    - Up to 14 days needed to make new platelets
  - Other NSAIDs block COX reversibly; activity returns as plasma levels fall
  - Used to prevent myocardial infarction and stroke; can increase perioperative blood loss
  - COX-2 inhibition does not affect platelets, even at high doses
- Major vascular events with COX-2 inhibitors:
  - Higher relative risk of MI and stroke (fatal and non-fatal), as in the VIGOR study
  - Tip the thromboxane/prostacyclin balance towards aggregation, vasoconstriction and thromboembolism
- Others:
  - Possibly delayed bone healing (especially spinal fusion)
  - Possibly more anastomotic leaks
  - Hepatotoxicity, usually with prolonged or excessive use; up to 15% may get raised transaminases even after short courses

## Contraindications

- COX-2 inhibitors: contraindicated (Committee on Safety of Medicines) in ischaemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, mild heart failure and peripheral vascular disease
- Think carefully with cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes, smoking)
- COX-2 inhibitors carry the same renal risk as nsNSAIDs
- Interactions:
  - Warfarin: displaced from protein binding, effect increased
  - Lithium: levels can rise; monitor

## Indications

- Gout: indomethacin
- Dysmenorrhoea: mefenamic acid
- Postpartum: naproxen
- Children: ibuprofen, diclofenac
- Surgical concerns: more bleeding; bone healing (especially spinal fusion)

## Pharmacokinetics

- Rapid absorption from the small bowel
- Highly protein bound, small volume of distribution; can displace other highly bound drugs (e.g. warfarin)
- Hepatic metabolism; inactive products excreted in urine and bile

## Other agents

- Phenylbutazone:
  - Potent anti-inflammatory
  - Restricted to hospital use in ankylosing spondylitis because of agranulocytosis and aplastic anaemia
  - Highly protein bound (interactions); can impair liver function, cause rash and sodium and water retention
- Tenoxicam:
  - Can be given IV for rapid onset
  - Long elimination half-life (72 hours): once daily, long acting
  - These become drawbacks if side effects occur
- Meloxicam:
  - Tablets and suppositories
  - Limited preferential COX-2 selectivity, quoted as 3 to 50 times more potent against COX-2
  - At 7.5 mg/d, fewer GI side effects than diclofenac; renal side effects similar to other NSAIDs

## Flashcards

- Q: What percentage of asthmatics may have NSAID-sensitive asthma?
  - A: Up to 20%
- Q: What is the dose of parecoxib?
  - A: 40 mg IV BD
- Q: Which NSAIDs carry the highest GI risk?
  - A: Ketorolac and piroxicam
- Q: Why does aspirin affect platelets for their lifetime?
  - A: Irreversible COX inhibition, and platelets have no nucleus to make new COX
- Q: What is the ketorolac dose?
  - A: 15 mg 6-hourly IV
