# Local anaesthetic side effects

Systemic and local adverse effects of local anaesthetics, apart from LAST itself.

## Systemic effects

### Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)
- Covered as its own topic

### Vascular tone
- At higher concentrations, all local anaesthetics except cocaine relax smooth muscle and can cause arteriolar vasodilation
- Low, subclinical concentrations cause vasoconstriction
- Cocaine always vasoconstricts, whatever the concentration

### Hypersensitivity and allergy
- Adverse reactions are fairly common; true allergy is very rare
- Allergy is mostly to esters, via their metabolite para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA)
- If it occurs, consider changing to an amide

### Methaemoglobinaemia
- Large doses of prilocaine or benzocaine (common in topical sprays) oxidise haemoglobin to methaemoglobin
- Reversed with methylene blue 1–2 mg/kg IV

## Local effects

### Neurotoxicity
- Direct toxicity to neurons and myocytes, dependent on time and dose
- Lidocaine is more neurotoxic than bupivacaine, with a risk 6.5 times as high
  - Probably because its lower pKa means more unionised drug enters the axoplasm
- Transient neurologic symptoms (TNS) after spinal anaesthesia
  - Transient hypoaesthesia, paraesthesia and leg or buttock weakness
  - Much more common with lidocaine than with bupivacaine, tetracaine or (probably) 2-chloroprocaine
  - Usually settles within 3 days; occasionally lasts up to 6 months
- Permanent injury (cauda equina syndrome)
  - Linked to 5% lidocaine infused through spinal microcatheters
  - Rare after single-shot spinals
  - Thought to be due to concentrated drug pooling around nerve roots
- 2-chloroprocaine
  - Used for spinals in the 1950s; still widely used epidurally, especially in obstetrics
  - In the early 1980s, nerve injuries followed accidental large intrathecal doses
  - Cause was likely the solution's low pH and metabisulfite preservative
  - Plain, preservative-free 2-chloroprocaine at proper intrathecal doses seems no more neurotoxic than other spinal agents, and may have a lower TNS risk

### Myotoxicity
- Intramuscular injection damages muscle
- Worse with potent, long-acting agents such as bupivacaine
- Transient, with full recovery within 2 weeks

## Flashcards
- Q: Treatment of local anaesthetic-induced methaemoglobinaemia?
  - A: Methylene blue 1–2 mg/kg IV
- Q: Which local anaesthetics cause methaemoglobinaemia?
  - A: Prilocaine and benzocaine in large doses
- Q: Why are allergies more common with esters?
  - A: Their metabolite para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA)
- Q: How long do transient neurologic symptoms usually last?
  - A: Usually settle within 3 days; occasionally up to 6 months
- Q: Which local anaesthetic always vasoconstricts?
  - A: Cocaine
