# Nitric oxide

Endogenous vasodilator (formerly endothelium-derived relaxing factor, EDRF), given inhaled (iNO) as a selective pulmonary vasodilator; acts via guanylyl cyclase and cGMP.

## Presentation

- Endogenous molecule; can also contaminate nitrous oxide cylinders
- Made industrially as a by-product of nitric acid synthesis

## Administration

- Inhaled
- Never stop suddenly: rebound can cause acute pulmonary hypertension, haemodynamic collapse and worse oxygenation
  - Wean gradually, at least every 12 hours, once oxygenation has improved and the patient is stable, on a low dose (5 ppm)
- Monitoring
  - Methaemoglobin at baseline, at 4–6 hours after starting, then daily; lower the dose if methaemoglobin is more than 5%
  - Reduce iNO if NO2 is more than 1 ppm
- Cannot go into MRI on iNO (e.g. no MRI brain for HIE prognosis, no MRI spine)

## Dosage

- Adults, pulmonary hypertension with cardiac surgery: start at 20 ppm (data sheet), up to a maximum of 40 ppm
- ARDS: start at 5 ppm, increase to 10–20 ppm
- Wait at least 10 min between dose changes
- Use the minimum effective dose
- Higher doses risk methaemoglobinaemia (more than 5%) and nitrogen dioxide toxicity (more than 1 ppm)
- Long-term use: keep at 10 ppm or less to limit NO2 exposure

## Mechanism of action

- Free radical with a half-life of only a few seconds, mainly because it reacts quickly with superoxide anion (both have an unpaired electron); superoxide therefore lowers NO bioavailability
- Binds avidly to haem: in haemoglobin (where it is broken down) and in guanylyl cyclase (vascular smooth muscle and most other cells)
- Diffuses freely across membranes; with its short half-life this suits paracrine and autocrine signalling
  - Signalling ends once oxygen and water convert it to nitrates and nitrites
- In vascular smooth muscle next to the endothelium it activates guanylyl cyclase, which turns GTP into cyclic GMP (cGMP)
- cGMP is a second messenger that relaxes smooth muscle by:
  - Blocking calcium entry and lowering intracellular calcium
  - Opening K+ channels, causing hyperpolarisation
  - Activating a cGMP-dependent protein kinase that switches on myosin light chain phosphatase, which dephosphorylates myosin light chains
- PDE5 (a cGMP-specific phosphodiesterase in vascular smooth muscle) breaks down cGMP
  - PDE5 inhibitors (e.g. sildenafil) boost NO-driven vasodilation, e.g. in erectile tissue for erectile dysfunction
- cGMP also inhibits platelet aggregation; PDE5 inhibitors reduce aggregation too

### Synthesis
- Made from a terminal guanidino nitrogen of L-arginine by nitric oxide synthase (NOS)
- Co-factors: oxygen, NADPH, tetrahydrobiopterin, flavin adenine nucleotides
- Three isozymes; the note lists two as endothelial (constitutive and inducible) plus a neural form
- Constitutive NOS (cNOS, type III, eNOS)
  - Found in endothelium, neurones, skeletal and cardiac muscle, platelets
  - Produces NO continuously in blood vessels at rest
  - Calcium- and calmodulin-dependent; stimulated by cGMP
  - Two triggers, both releasing calcium from subsarcolemmal stores:
    - Flow-dependent: shear stress from blood flow, so more flow or haemodynamic stress means more NO
    - Receptor-stimulated: endothelial receptors for acetylcholine (M3 muscarinic), bradykinin, substance P, adenosine and many other vasoactive substances
- Inducible NOS (iNOS, type II)
  - Calcium-independent
  - Very low activity at rest; switched on by stimulation
  - Induced in inflammation by bacterial endotoxin (e.g. lipopolysaccharide) and cytokines (TNF, interleukins)
  - Can make up to 1,000-fold more NO than cNOS: cytotoxic, and forms free radicals causing cell damage and capillary leak
- Neural NOS (nNOS, type I)
  - Neurotransmitter in the brain and peripheral nerves, e.g. non-adrenergic non-cholinergic (NANC) nerves to erectile and other tissues, causing vasodilation
  - Calcium-dependent, like cNOS

## Effects

- Selective pulmonary vasodilation
  - No systemic effect, as it is inactivated rapidly in red cells
  - Intrapulmonary right-to-left shunt can increase, which may worsen oxygenation
- In severe LV failure: higher left atrial pressure and preload may overwhelm the failing LV

## Adverse effects

- Rebound pulmonary hypertension on abrupt withdrawal
- Methaemoglobinaemia
- Nitrogen dioxide (NO2) toxicity
  - NO oxidises with oxygen to NO2, a highly toxic gas causing lung injury
  - No extra NO2 risk found with doses below 80 ppm over days
- Coagulopathy: may increase bleeding risk
- Renal impairment
  - NO regulates renal vascular tone and glomerular function
  - 2016 Cochrane review: higher risk of renal impairment in ARDS
- Interactions: possible with NO donors (local anaesthetics, nitroprusside, glyceryl trinitrate) and other vasodilators acting via cGMP or cAMP

## Contraindications

- Methaemoglobin reductase deficiency

## Indications

- Pulmonary hypertension in adults, including in cardiac surgery
- Persistent pulmonary hypertension in neonates (established use)
- Right heart failure
- Salvage therapy in severe ARDS (e.g. PF ratio less than 100), where hypoxia drives pulmonary vasoconstriction
  - 2016 Cochrane review: PF ratio improved transiently at 24 h, but not at 48 h or 72 h
- Haemolysis, during which plasma NO is consumed

## Pharmacokinetics

- Highly lipid soluble; diffuses freely across cell membranes
- Rapidly converted to nitrates and nitrites in the presence of O2
- Reacts readily with oxidised Hb to form methaemoglobin
