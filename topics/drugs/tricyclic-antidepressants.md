# Tricyclic Antidepressants

Serotonin and/or noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors used for depression and, at lower doses, chronic pain; cardiotoxic and anticholinergic, dangerous in overdose.

## Overview
- Examples: amitriptyline, nortriptyline, imipramine, clomipramine (used in OCD), desipramine, dothiepin
- Named for the original three-ring core; many later agents have a different number of rings
- Were the main antidepressants before SSRIs arrived; now largely replaced as first line because of side effects and interactions
- Structure resembles local anaesthetics and phenothiazines
  - Hydrophobic part joined to an amine through a straight-chain linker
  - Imipramine (the prototype) is a phenothiazine with the sulfur swapped for an ethylene bridge, giving a seven-membered central ring
- Related tetracyclics
  - Maprotiline: clinically similar to imipramine
  - Mirtazapine: may boost central noradrenaline and serotonin activity
  - Neither should be given with MAO inhibitors
- Classification
  - Tertiary amines: block reuptake of both serotonin and noradrenaline (amitriptyline, imipramine, clomipramine)
  - Secondary amines: mainly block noradrenaline reuptake (desipramine, nortriptyline)
  - Secondary amines cause less sedation, postural hypotension and anticholinergic effect, with similar analgesic efficacy

## Administration
- Oral, usually at night (ON)
- Pain relief may appear as early as two weeks, but amitriptyline often needs six to eight weeks at the best dose before judging it a fair trial
- Taper over a 4-week period to avoid withdrawal
  - Abrupt stop of high doses can give a mild withdrawal syndrome: malaise, chills, coryza, muscle aches
  - Thought to be due to cholinergic supersensitivity
- Tolerance develops to anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, blurred vision, tachycardia) and orthostatic hypotension; tolerance to the wanted effects often does not

## Dosage
- Pain doses are lower than antidepressant doses
- Amitriptyline (tertiary amine) for pain
  - Start low, e.g. 10 mg ON or 25 mg ON
  - Increase by 10 or 25 mg each week towards 75 mg ON if side effects allow
  - Children: PO 0.2-0.5 mg/kg ON; increase by 0.2 mg/kg every 2-3 days
- Nortriptyline (secondary amine) for pain
  - Start at 10 mg ON; increase by 10 or 25 mg each week (e.g. 10 then 25 mg)

## Mechanism of action
- Antidepressant: block presynaptic reuptake of serotonin and/or noradrenaline, raising their synaptic levels
  - Reuptake block is immediate, but the clinical effect takes 2 to 3 weeks
  - So amine build-up is probably only the first step in a longer chain of changes; some antidepressants have no effect on reuptake
- Analgesic: mechanism uncertain; proposed
  - Noradrenaline and serotonin reuptake inhibition
  - Enhancement of central endogenous opioids
  - Anti-inflammatory action, possibly via sodium channel block similar to local anaesthetics
- Also block muscarinic, histamine and alpha-adrenoceptors; non-specific sedation

## Effects
- Mnemonic for side effects: 3 As
  - Arrhythmias and cardiotoxicity (QTc prolongation), worst in overdose
  - Acidaemia (central respiratory depression)
  - Anticholinergic effects (blurred vision, dilated pupils)

## Adverse effects
- Use with caution in the elderly and in cardiac disease; can be fatal in overdose (cardiotoxicity, seizures, CNS depression)
- Central nervous system
  - Sedation (greatest with amitriptyline and doxepin); can help depressed patients with insomnia
  - Lower seizure threshold, especially maprotiline and clomipramine; occasional seizures in epilepsy; children particularly vulnerable
  - May enhance the CNS excitation of enflurane
  - Weakness and fatigue, similar to phenothiazines
  - Extrapyramidal effects rare; fine tremor in about 10%, mainly the elderly
  - With an MAO inhibitor: CNS toxicity with hyperthermia, seizures and coma
- Anticholinergic
  - Dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision, tachycardia
  - Highest with amitriptyline, lowest with desipramine
  - Elderly are more sensitive; delirium can occur even at therapeutic doses
  - Severe toxicity often from several anticholinergic drugs together (including over-the-counter diarrhoea or sleep remedies)
  - Increased sensitivity to catecholamines with halothane or sympathomimetics
- Cardiovascular
  - Orthostatic hypotension and a modest rise in heart rate are the commonest (from noradrenaline reuptake block)
  - Falls and fractures from orthostatic hypotension in the elderly
  - Hypotension under general anaesthesia: uncommon but reported
  - ECG changes: wide QRS, long PR, heart block, T wave changes; can cause AF and VF
    - From slowed atrial and ventricular conduction
    - These changes are usually benign and fade with continued use
  - Quinidine-like direct myocardial depression (slowed sodium entry alters repolarisation and conduction); may add to the cardiac depression of anaesthetics
- Potentiates warfarin
- Weight gain

## Drug interactions
- Mostly from anticholinergic action and catecholamine reuptake block
- Displaced from albumin by phenytoin, aspirin and scopolamine (hyoscine)
- Anaesthetics
  - More arrhythmias at induction
  - Lower adrenaline dose needed to cause arrhythmias under volatile anaesthesia
  - In theory, more CNS noradrenaline could raise volatile requirements
- Sympathomimetics: blood pressure response is complex and unpredictable
  - Recently started TCA: more synaptic noradrenaline, so expect exaggerated pressor responses to both direct and indirect agents, most with indirect ones such as ephedrine
  - Use smaller doses of a direct-acting agent, titrated to effect
  - Chronic TCA (over 6 weeks): alpha-receptor downregulation reduces sympathetic transmission
    - Direct or indirect agents both acceptable; consider starting at about one-third of the usual dose
    - Usual agents may fail (receptors desensitised or stores depleted); a potent direct agent such as noradrenaline may be the only thing that works
- Anticholinergics
  - Additive effects; centrally acting premedication raises the risk of postoperative delirium (central anticholinergic syndrome)
  - Glycopyrrolate is theoretically safer
- Antihypertensives: rebound hypertension after stopping clonidine suddenly may be worse and longer
- Opioids: in animals, TCAs enhance opioid analgesia and respiratory depression; titrate opioids carefully

## Contraindications
- History of overdose or suicidality
- May be contraindicated after myocardial infarction

## Indications
- Depression (poor effect in reactive depression)
- Adjunct for chronic pain: neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia and other chronic pain syndromes

## Pharmacokinetics
- Well absorbed orally (highly lipid soluble)
- Highly bound to plasma and tissue proteins; large volume of distribution, up to 50 L/kg
- Hepatic microsomal oxidation, then glucuronide conjugation
  - Metabolic rate varies 10- to 30-fold between patients; slower in the elderly
  - Active metabolites: imipramine to desipramine and nortriptyline; amitriptyline to nortriptyline
- Elimination half-time 17 to 30 hours; excretion takes 1 week or longer; once-daily dosing works
- Therapeutic plasma level (parent plus active demethylated metabolites) 100 to 300 ng/mL; toxicity likely above 500 ng/mL

## Overdose
- Common in depressed patients; can progress quickly from alert to unresponsive
- Cardiovascular
  - Dose-related QT prolongation and QRS widening; right bundle branch block
  - QRS longer than 100 milliseconds: higher risk of seizures and ventricular arrhythmias
  - QRS longer than 0.16 seconds: ventricular arrhythmias more likely
  - Blood pressure high or low; severe cases have refractory hypotension progressing to PEA
  - Usual terminal events: intractable myocardial depression or ventricular arrhythmias
- Central: excitation, then seizures (linked to QRS over 0.1 seconds), then coma; mydriasis and hyperthermia
- Anticholinergic: tachycardia, mydriasis, flushed dry skin, urinary retention
- Coma lasts 24 to 72 hours (often resolves by 24 hours) and may need airway support
- Risk of dangerous arrhythmias persists for up to 10 days: keep ECG monitoring
- Plasma levels do not predict seizures or arrhythmias

## Overdose management
- Early: gastric lavage then activated charcoal
- Haemodialysis and forced diuresis do not help (high protein binding)
- Supportive care, aimed at CNS and cardiac toxicity
- Seizures (may come before cardiac arrest): treat aggressively with a benzodiazepine (e.g. diazepam), then a longer-acting drug such as phenytoin if needed
  - Seizure acidosis raises the free drug fraction and promotes arrhythmias
- Ventricular arrhythmias: lidocaine and phenytoin
- Hypotension (vasodilation, alpha block or myocardial depression)
  - Fluid loading
  - Alkalinise plasma to pH above 7.45 with IV sodium bicarbonate or hyperventilation; temporarily reverses cardiotoxicity
  - Avoid inotropes where possible (can provoke arrhythmias); use sympathomimetics or inotropes if hypotension persists despite fluids and alkalinisation
- Anticholinesterases to reverse anticholinergic effects are not recommended: can cause seizures, bradycardia and heart failure

## Flashcards
- Q: Typical starting and target amitriptyline doses for chronic pain?
  - A: 10-25 mg ON, increased by 10 or 25 mg weekly towards 75 mg ON
- Q: QRS duration in TCA overdose that raises risk of seizures and ventricular arrhythmias?
  - A: Over 100 milliseconds (ventricular arrhythmias more likely over 0.16 seconds)
- Q: Target pH for alkalinisation in TCA cardiotoxicity?
  - A: Above 7.45, by IV sodium bicarbonate or hyperventilation
- Q: Vasopressor choice in a patient on long-term TCAs (over 6 weeks)?
  - A: Start at about one-third of usual dose; a potent direct agent such as noradrenaline may be the only effective one
- Q: How long should TCAs be tapered over?
  - A: A 4-week period
