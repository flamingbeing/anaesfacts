# Morphine

Naturally occurring phenanthrene opioid and the reference against which all other opioids are compared; poorly lipid soluble, with active glucuronides that build up in renal failure.

## Presentation

- Tablets, suspensions, suppositories, slow-release capsules and granules, in many strengths
  - Morphine slow-release tablet (MST)
  - Mist morphine (oral solution); morphine syrup in children
- IV: 10 mg in 1 ml vial; dilute to 10 ml for 1 mg/ml

## Administration

- Oral: **three times less potent than IV**
- Subcutaneous route usually avoided: low lipid solubility means slow absorption
- Epidural (2–4 mg) and intrathecal (0.2–1.0 mg) use is linked with delayed respiratory depression, again from low lipid solubility

## Dosage

- Oral
  - 5–20 mg 4-hourly
  - Children (syrup): 0.2-0.3 mg/kg every 6 hours
  - Onset 20-30 min, peak 60-90 min, duration 3-6 hr
- IM
  - 0.1–0.2 mg/kg 4-hourly
  - Peak 30 min, duration 3-4 hr (Peck and Hill)
- IV
  - **0.05-0.2 mg/kg**, given in 1-2 mg boluses
  - Titrate to effect: start at 0.1 mg/kg, then increments of 0.05 mg/kg up to 0.2 mg/kg
  - Onset 3-5 min (or 5-10 min)
  - Peak 10 min (Peck and Hill) (or 20-30 min)
  - Duration 3-4 hr (Peck and Hill)
  - These doses are only a guide; dose or frequency may need increasing
- Infusion, children: 0-40 mcg/kg/hr

## Mechanism of action

- Mu-opioid receptor agonist (reference opioid)

## Effects

- Analgesia: especially good for **visceral pain**, less so for sharp or superficial pain

## Adverse effects

- **Histamine release**: bradycardia, bronchospasm, hypotension
  - Slower injection limits bronchospasm and hypotension
  - Rash and itch from histamine can be reversed by naloxone
- **Sphincter of Oddi contraction**: raises biliary pressure; clinical importance unknown
- Delayed respiratory depression after neuraxial use
- Renal failure: accumulating metabolites cause respiratory depression, oversedation and delayed waking; M3G may lower seizure threshold and cause opioid-induced hyperalgesia

## Pharmacokinetics

- **Least lipid-soluble opioid**
- Weak base (pKa = 8.0): ionised in the acid stomach, so absorbed only once it reaches the more alkaline small bowel
- **Oral bioavailability 30%**, from hepatic first-pass metabolism
- Brain levels fall slowly (low lipid solubility), so plasma levels do not track its effect
- Mainly **hepatic metabolism**
  - Up to 70% to morphine-3-glucuronide (M3G): affects arousal, possibly a mu antagonist
  - Morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G): 13 times more potent than morphine, similar duration
  - Some N-demethylation
- **M6G and M3G are renally excreted and accumulate in renal failure: adjust the dose**
  - M6G (mu agonist): **respiratory depression, oversedation, delayed waking**
- Neonates: more sensitive, from immature hepatic conjugation
- Elderly: higher peak levels, from a smaller volume of distribution

## Flashcards

- Q: IV morphine dose range and titration?
  - A: 0.05-0.2 mg/kg in 1-2 mg boluses; start 0.1 mg/kg, then 0.05 mg/kg steps to 0.2 mg/kg
- Q: Oral bioavailability of morphine and why?
  - A: 30%, due to hepatic first-pass metabolism
- Q: Which morphine metabolite is more potent and by how much?
  - A: Morphine-6-glucuronide, 13 times more potent
- Q: Why is morphine a problem in renal failure?
  - A: M6G and M3G accumulate: respiratory depression, oversedation, lower seizure threshold
- Q: Epidural and intrathecal morphine doses?
  - A: Epidural 2–4 mg; intrathecal 0.2–1.0 mg (risk of delayed respiratory depression)
