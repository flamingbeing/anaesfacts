# Propafenone

Sodium channel blocking antiarrhythmic much like flecainide, with weak beta-blocking activity; second-line for resistant SVT and ventricular arrhythmias.

## Presentation

- Film-coated tablets only in the UK

## Dosage

- Oral: initially 600–900 mg, then 150–300 mg twice or three times daily
- IV (used, though not marketed in the UK): 1–2 mg/kg

## Mechanism of action

- Blocks fast Na+ influx into cardiac tissue, prolonging phase 0 of the action potential
- Prolongs action potential duration and refractory period, mostly in conducting tissue
- Raises threshold potential and the ventricular fibrillation threshold, so excitability falls
- Some beta-blockade at higher doses

## Adverse effects

- Usually well tolerated
- Cardiac: caution in heart failure (weak beta-blockade)
- Non-cardiac
  - Minor nervous system effects
  - Gastrointestinal effects more prominent at higher doses
  - May worsen myasthenia gravis
  - May trigger asthma (beta-blockade)
- Interactions: raises plasma levels of digoxin and warfarin

## Indications

- Second-line for resistant SVT, including atrial fibrillation and flutter
- Ventricular tachyarrhythmias

## Pharmacokinetics

- Almost completely absorbed; oral bioavailability 50% at first
- Rises disproportionately to nearly 100% as first-pass enzymes saturate
- More than 95% protein bound
- Hepatic metabolism; tiny amounts excreted unchanged
- The metabolising enzyme shows genetic polymorphism: some patients respond more strongly

## Flashcards

- Q: Which drug is propafenone most like?
  - A: Flecainide (sodium channel blocker), with added weak beta-blockade
- Q: Why use propafenone cautiously in asthma and heart failure?
  - A: Its beta-blocking action can trigger bronchospasm and worsen heart failure
- Q: Why does propafenone bioavailability rise with dose?
  - A: First-pass enzymes saturate, taking it from 50% to nearly 100%
- Q: Which drug levels does propafenone raise?
  - A: Digoxin and warfarin
