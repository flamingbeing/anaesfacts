# Induction agents

Drugs that produce unconsciousness, usually within one arm-brain circulation time; wake-up after a bolus is mainly from redistribution.

## Overview
- Any drug that renders a patient unconscious can serve as an induction agent
- Usual IV agents: propofol, thiopental, etomidate, ketamine
  - Act within one arm-brain circulation time (time for the drug to travel from the injection site, usually the arm, to the brain)
- Benzodiazepines and opioids can also cause unconsciousness, but onset is unpredictable and action long at induction doses, so neither is usually used alone
- Induction can also be inhalational, or a combination of both

## Classification by chemical structure
- Phenols: propofol
- Barbiturates: thiopentone
- Imidazoles: etomidate
- Phencyclidines: ketamine
- Benzodiazepines: midazolam

## Pharmacology
- Part of the dose binds plasma protein; the rest is free
  - Binding depends on properties such as lipid solubility and ionisation
- Route: venous blood, right heart, lungs, left heart, systemic circulation
- About 70% of cardiac output goes to the vessel-rich organs (brain, liver, kidney), so much of the bolus reaches the brain first
- Rate of entry into the brain depends on
  - Arterial concentration of free drug
  - Lipid solubility
  - Degree of ionisation
- Free, lipid-soluble, unionised drug crosses the blood-brain barrier fastest
- Exact mechanism unknown; GABA-A, NMDA and acetylcholine receptors are candidate sites
- Redistribution
  - After the initial surge into the CNS and vessel-rich tissues, drug moves into less perfused tissue, mainly skeletal muscle
  - Plasma level falls, drug leaves the CNS down the reversed gradient, and the patient wakes
  - Metabolism and clearance matter little after one bolus but more after infusions or repeat doses
- Fat
  - Poorly perfused, so contributes little to early redistribution after a bolus
  - After infusions or repeat doses it becomes a reservoir and can delay waking

## Low cardiac output states
- In shock and the elderly, a larger share of cardiac output goes to the brain, so more drug reaches it
  - Always reduce the induction dose
- Lower cardiac output also slows arrival at the brain, delaying onset
- Key to safe induction: slow titration of a reduced dose

## Ideal IV induction agent
- Fast onset and short action (in case of problems needing wake-up, or for short procedures)
- Physical properties
  - Water soluble and stable in solution
  - Stable in light
  - Long shelf life
  - No pain on IV injection
  - Painful if injected into an artery
  - Non-irritant subcutaneously
  - Low rate of thrombophlebitis
  - Cheap
- Pharmacokinetic properties
  - Onset within one arm-brain circulation time
  - Rapid redistribution to vessel-rich tissue
  - Rapid clearance and metabolism
  - No active metabolites
- Pharmacodynamic properties
  - High therapeutic ratio (toxic dose : minimally effective dose)
  - Minimal cardiovascular and respiratory effects
  - No histamine release or hypersensitivity
  - Not emetic
  - No involuntary movements
  - No emergence nightmares
  - No hangover effect
  - No adrenocortical suppression
  - Safe in porphyria

## Flashcards
- Q: What fraction of cardiac output goes to the vessel-rich organs?
  - A: About 70% (brain, liver, kidney)
- Q: What causes rapid waking after a single induction bolus?
  - A: Redistribution from the CNS to other tissues, mainly skeletal muscle
- Q: How should induction be adjusted in low cardiac output states?
  - A: Reduce the dose and titrate slowly; more drug reaches the brain but onset is slower
- Q: Why can repeat doses or infusions delay waking?
  - A: Drug equilibrates with fat, which acts as a reservoir
