# Parasympathetic Drugs

Drugs acting on the parasympathetic system, mainly at muscarinic receptors: parasympathomimetics and parasympatholytics.

## Overview

- Most parasympathetic effects come from acetylcholine released at post-ganglionic nerve endings, acting on muscarinic receptors
- Muscarinic receptors are the main target for direct-acting drugs
- Agonists and antagonists alike show little selectivity between muscarinic subtypes

## Parasympathomimetics: muscarinic agonists

- Direct agonists are rarely used, but still have a few specific roles
- Pilocarpine: semi-synthetic alkaloid, probably the most used direct muscarinic agonist
  - Mainly topical for glaucoma; has been given systemically for xerostomia
  - Systemic effects: hypotension, bradycardia, bronchoconstriction, bronchorrhoea, sialorrhoea

## Parasympathomimetics: acetylcholinesterase inhibitors

- Non-specifically block acetylcholine breakdown in the synapse, giving parasympathomimetic effects
- Neostigmine: most commonly used anticholinesterase
  - Peak effect 7-10 minutes after a single dose; lasts up to 1 hour
- Pyridostigmine: slower onset and longer action than neostigmine
  - Can be given orally, so useful in myasthenia gravis
- Edrophonium bromide ("Tensilon"): much faster onset and shorter action than neostigmine
  - Duration about 10 minutes
  - Mainly used to diagnose myasthenia gravis

## Parasympatholytics: muscarinic antagonists (anticholinergics)

- Atropine
- Glycopyrrolate
- Hyoscine (scopolamine): very lipid soluble, so central effects dominate
  - Drowsiness, amnesia, analgesia
  - Strong antisialogogue and antiemetic
- Ipratropium: synthetic atropine derivative
- Pirenzepine: unusual in being relatively selective for M1 receptors
  - Reduces gastric acid secretion and gut smooth muscle activity
  - Used for peptic ulcer disease, though not widely
