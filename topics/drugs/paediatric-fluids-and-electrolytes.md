# Paediatric fluids and electrolytes

Children have more body water, faster turnover and a higher risk of dehydration and hyponatraemia than adults.

## Physiology
- Total body water is high in neonates and infants
  - Term neonate: 75% of body weight (adult 60%); premature neonate up to 85%
  - Reaches adult values after 1 year of age
  - Leads to a larger volume of distribution for water-soluble drugs
- Extracellular fluid is proportionately larger: 40% of body weight in the neonate, up to 60% in premature neonates (adult 20%)
  - Intracellular water rises as muscle mass increases
- Infant water turnover is more than double the adult rate: about 40% of extracellular water
  - Due to higher evaporative loss (large surface area to body weight ratio) and higher respiratory water loss (higher minute ventilation)
- Dehydration follows easily from reduced intake or extra losses because of:
  - High water turnover (evaporative and respiratory losses)
  - Poor renal concentrating ability

## Clinical assessment
- Anticipate deficits in:
  - Gastrointestinal losses (vomiting, diarrhoea, pancreatitis)
  - Ileus
  - Sepsis
  - Burns
  - Trauma
  - Postoperative bleeding
- Hypovolaemia and dehydration are clinical diagnoses
  - Capillary refill time over 2 sec
  - Tachycardia (also from pain, fever, anxiety)
  - Oliguria
  - Cool peripheries, wider core-peripheral temperature gap
  - Sunken fontanelle, sunken eyes
  - Crying without tears, dry mucous membranes
  - Reduced consciousness: "the child that is confused might not be perfused"
  - Thready pulse
  - Hypotension is a late sign, often only after more than 30% of blood volume is lost
- Shock: oxygen and nutrient demand of the cells exceeds delivery

## Preoperative fluids and resuscitation
- Restore circulating volume with crystalloid (0.9% saline, Hartmann's) or colloid (plasma protein solution, Gelofusine, starch); blood products for major or ongoing loss
- Replace extracellular losses with isotonic crystalloid (0.9% saline, Hartmann's)
- Burns, pyloric stenosis and diabetic ketoacidosis follow specific protocols
- Estimated blood volume
  - Neonate 90 mL/kg
  - Infant 85 mL/kg
  - Child 80 mL/kg
  - Adult 70 mL/kg
- Shock: 20 mL/kg boluses of crystalloid or colloid, then reassess
- Not stable after 40, 60 and 80 mL/kg (depending on the situation and expected ongoing loss): consider intubation, inotropes and blood products
  - Blood after 15-20% of circulating volume lost
  - Fresh frozen plasma after 50% lost
  - Platelets after 100% lost
  - Rapid testing of specific factors with targeted replacement can reduce product use, risk and cost

## Intraoperative fluids
- Aims: replace any deficit, give maintenance, replace intraoperative losses
- Preoperative deficit
  - Rarely an issue in elective cases: fasting times are short and children unable to drink get IV fluids
  - Non-elective deficits are usually corrected during preoperative resuscitation
- Maintenance: dextrose-containing fluid or dextrose-free crystalloid (Hartmann's, 0.9% saline); dextrose-free is preferred
  - Glucose is usually unnecessary except in premature babies, neonates and other at-risk children (e.g. on parenteral nutrition)
  - Hypoglycaemia is rare in healthy children under anaesthesia; plasma glucose usually increases with the surgical stress response
  - 4% or 5% dextrose fluids usually cause hyperglycaemia during surgery
  - Glucose fluids are isotonic in the bag but become hypotonic once the glucose is metabolised, so act as free water and risk hyponatraemia
  - Perioperative ADH release (stress, pain, hypovolaemia, drugs) lowers plasma sodium and osmolarity further
  - Children become hyponatraemic more readily than adults and suffer more CNS effects; now seen as a major perioperative risk
- Intraoperative losses (exposure, handling, inflammation), guide by surgery type:
  - Superficial surgery: 1-3 ml/kg/h
  - Abdominal, chest or hip surgery: 3-4 ml/kg/h
  - Extensive abdominal surgery: 6-8 ml/kg/h
- Most surgical losses are isotonic: replace with crystalloid, colloid or blood as needed

## Maintenance regimens in infants and children
- Formulae come from the link between weight and metabolic rate
  - Infants need 100 kcal/kg/day, children 75 kcal/kg/day
  - 1 mL of water per kcal
- 4-2-1 regimen (per hour)
  - 4 mL/kg for the first 10 kg
  - plus 2 mL/kg for the next 10 kg
  - plus 1 mL/kg for each kg after that
  - 17 kg child: 40 + 14 = 54 mL/hour; 24 kg child: 40 + 20 + 4 = 64 mL/hour
- Electrolytes
  - Sodium 2-4 mmol/kg/day
  - Potassium 2-3 mmol/kg/day
- Extra losses, replaced by type
  - Nasogastric: volume for volume with Hartmann's or 0.9% saline with 10 mmol KCl per 500 mL
  - Stoma: 50-75% of losses as 0.9% saline with 10 mmol KCl per 500 mL
- Give through a volumetric pump with a pressure limit
- Common maintenance fluids
  - 0.45% saline/5% dextrose
  - 0.45% saline/2.5% dextrose
  - 0.225% saline/5% glucose
  - 0.18% saline/4% glucose
  - 10% dextrose often used in premature babies and neonates
- Check electrolytes if maintenance runs beyond 24-48 hours
  - Formulae are a guide only and tend to overestimate needs, so derangements (especially hyponatraemia, which can be devastating) occur
- Some anaesthetists restrict postoperative maintenance to 50-75% of the calculated volume

## Neonatal maintenance
- The newborn's extracellular compartment is expanded; it contracts in the first few days with weight loss and negative water and sodium balance (normal)
- Restrict fluid until this postnatal weight loss has happened; needs then rise to those of a growing infant
- Water requirement rises over the first 4 days
  - Day 1: 60 mL/kg/day
  - Day 2: 90 mL/kg/day
  - Day 3: 120 mL/kg/day
  - Day 4: 150 mL/kg/day
  - Day 5 to 1 month: 150 mL/kg/day
  - Day 6 to 1 month (preterm): 180 ml/kg/day
- Electrolytes (added)
  - Sodium 2-4 mmol/kg/day (stable, unlike water needs)
  - Potassium 2-3 mmol/kg/day
- Dextrose
  - Usually 10% solution for maintenance
  - 20% occasionally needed in septic or fluid-restricted neonates
  - Anaesthetist will push dextrose into the drip
- Use the best weight (babies lose weight for 7 to 10 days after birth before regaining)
- Caveats
  - After significant intrapartum stress, babies void late and need less fluid over the first 24-48 hours
  - Very small or immature infants under 1000 g have higher insensible losses

## Ward fluid calculations
- Dehydration deficit (litres) = body weight x percentage dehydration
  - 10 kg child, 3% dehydrated: 10 x 0.03 = 0.3 litres = 300 ml deficit
- Maintenance by Holliday-Segar (used over 1 year of age)
  - 100 ml/kg/day for the first 10 kg
  - 50 ml/kg/day for the second 10 kg
  - 20 ml/kg/day for each kg after that
- Newborns under 14 days or under 3 kg
  - Need more water (higher losses from large surface area to body weight ratio)
  - Use neonatal maintenance tables; under 1 year of age use neonatal IV maintenance requirements
  - Adjust to clinical state, urine output and weight change; in a sick neonate (beyond day 3 of life) consider limiting IV fluid to 100–120 ml/kg/day
- Oral intake: infants usually get 150-200 ml/kg/day, but feeding is baby-led
- Ongoing losses
  - Input-output charting
  - 10 ml/kg per stool, 2 ml/kg per vomit
- Speed
  - Rehydrate evenly over at least 24–48 hours
  - 20 kg child, 5% dehydrated: maintenance 1,500 ml/day plus deficit 1,000 ml, so 2,500 ml/day for the first 24 hours, then 1,500 ml/day
- Higher needs: fever, phototherapy, radiant warmer, ongoing losses (vomiting, diarrhoea, high stoma output, burns)
- Lower needs: stable postoperative children, renal or cardiac failure, SIADH

## Choice of IV fluid
- Base the choice on age plus the underlying problem and any electrolyte or glucose abnormality
  - Under 1 month (neonate): 10% dextrose combination drip
  - 1–3 months: 5% dextrose with 0.45% NaCl
  - Over 3 months: 5% dextrose with 0.9% NaCl
- Resuscitate with isotonic 0.9% saline
- Fluid bolus 10-20 ml/kg over 20 min

## Air bubbles
- Shunts may still be partly patent in young children: clear all air from IV lines to avoid paradoxical air embolism and cardiovascular collapse

## Electrolytes
- Requirements otherwise as in adults
- Sodium
  - Deficit = 0.6 x weight (kg) x (135 - Na+)
  - 3% NaCl contains 513 mmol/L Na+; normal saline 154 mmol/L; D/S(M) (dextrose saline) 38.5 mmol/L
  - Maintenance 2-3 mmol/kg x weight (kg)
- Potassium
  - Maintenance 1-2 mmol/kg x weight (kg)
  - Neonates have a high red cell load, so K up to 6 mmol/L is normal

## Flashcards
- Q: Estimated blood volume in a neonate, infant and child?
  - A: 90, 85 and 80 mL/kg (adult 70 mL/kg)
- Q: Hourly maintenance by the 4-2-1 rule for a 24 kg child?
  - A: 40 + 20 + 4 = 64 mL/hour
- Q: Neonatal water requirement on day 1 and day 4?
  - A: 60 mL/kg/day on day 1, rising to 150 mL/kg/day by day 4
- Q: Shock bolus in a child, and when to consider intubation, inotropes and blood?
  - A: 20 mL/kg then reassess; consider them after 40, 60 and 80 mL/kg without stability
- Q: Why are glucose-containing maintenance fluids risky perioperatively?
  - A: They become hypotonic once glucose is metabolised and, with ADH release, cause hyponatraemia
