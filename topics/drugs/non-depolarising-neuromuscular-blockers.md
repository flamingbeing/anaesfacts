# Non-depolarising neuromuscular blockers

Competitive antagonists of acetylcholine at the motor end plate; two chemical families (benzylisoquinoliniums and aminosteroids) with different side effects and clearance.

## Classification by structure

- All are quaternary ammonium compounds
- Benzylisoquinoliniums:
  - Examples: atracurium, cisatracurium, mivacurium, doxacurium, d-tubocurarine
  - Two quaternary ammonium groups linked by a thin methyl chain; chiral centres in the chain give several stereoisomers
  - Broken down in plasma more readily than aminosteroids
  - No vagolytic effect; more likely to release histamine
- Aminosteroids:
  - Examples: rocuronium, vecuronium, pancuronium, rapacuronium
  - Androstane skeleton with ACh-like groups added at the A and D rings
  - Mostly rely on organ function for excretion; some are deacetylated in the liver to metabolites that may still block, then cleared by the kidney
  - Little histamine release
  - Rocuronium and vecuronium are monoquaternary

## Classification by duration

- Ultra-short: (suxamethonium), gantacurium
- Short: mivacurium, rapacuronium
- Intermediate: atracurium, cisatracurium, rocuronium (reversible with sugammadex), vecuronium
- Long: pancuronium, pipecuronium, doxacurium, d-tubocurarine (prototype)

## Mechanism of action

- At the junction:
  - Compete with ACh for the alpha subunit of postsynaptic nicotinic receptors (nAChR) at the motor end plate, without activating them
  - Binding is dynamic (constant association and dissociation): raising ACh levels lets ACh win the receptors, the basis of anticholinesterase reversal
  - Large safety margin: more than 70% of receptors must be occupied before a nerve stimulator detects block
  - At least 92% occupancy is needed for complete block
- Before the junction:
  - ACh acts on prejunctional nicotinic receptors as positive feedback, boosting its own release during high-frequency (>2 Hz) stimulation
  - All clinical non-depolarising drugs also block these receptors, so ACh mobilisation cannot keep up
  - Seen clinically as fade (tetanic and TOF): twitch height falls with successive stimuli
  - Alpha-bungarotoxin relaxes muscle without fade

## Onset and duration

- Onset time and duration: rocuronium < vecuronium < atracurium / cisatracurium < pancuronium
- Factors affecting onset:
  - Dose, which relates to potency: low-potency drugs (e.g. rocuronium) act faster than high-potency ones (e.g. vecuronium), because the larger molar dose gives a steeper gradient into the NMJ
  - Delivery to the heart: cardiac output; rate and site of injection (central vs peripheral)
  - Delivery to the muscle: regional muscle blood flow (high flow, fast onset; low flow, delayed), and perhaps distance from the heart
- Core-to-periphery pattern of onset and recovery:
  - Laryngeal muscles: fastest; best guide to intubating conditions
  - Diaphragm: fast; recovers before peripheral muscles
  - Orbicularis oculi: fast; tracks laryngeal onset, useful to time intubation
  - Adductor pollicis: slow; standard for monitoring recovery (last to recover)

## Effects and adverse effects

- Allergy to anaesthetic drugs is rare, but NMBs are the commonest cause
- Other side effects are uncommon
- Tachycardia: pancuronium
- Histamine release and hypotension: atracurium, mivacurium, d-tubocurarine
- Rocuronium: no effect on heart rate or blood pressure

## Indications

- Maintaining muscle relaxation during surgery (longer acting than suxamethonium)
- Facilitating intubation, though good intubating conditions take clearly longer than with suxamethonium

## Pharmacokinetics

- Polar, so they do not cross lipid membranes; small volume of distribution
- Benzylisoquinoliniums are generally hydrolysed in plasma (atracurium, mivacurium)
- Aminosteroids end their action mainly by biliary and renal excretion, with some hepatic metabolism; unchanged drug goes into urine or bile

## Factors that prolong block

- Drugs:
  - Volatile anaesthetics
  - Local anaesthetics
  - Antibiotics: aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin), tetracyclines, polymyxins
    - Erythromycin, penicillins and cephalosporins do not prolong block
  - Anticholinesterases (e.g. drugs for Alzheimer's disease)
  - Calcium channel blockers
  - Magnesium, lithium
- Electrolytes and acid-base:
  - Hypocalcaemia and hypermagnesaemia, via effects on membrane potential
    - Magnesium competes with calcium at prejunctional voltage-gated channels, so less calcium enters to trigger ACh vesicle release
  - Hypokalaemia: hyperpolarises the muscle membrane, so it is harder to depolarise
  - Acidosis, especially for the monoquaternary aminosteroids (rocuronium, vecuronium)
    - The tertiary amine gains a proton (N+), giving a second positive charge and higher affinity for the receptor alpha subunits
    - Acidosis also blunts anticholinesterases (neostigmine)
- Physiological states:
  - Hypothermia
    - Pharmacokinetic: slower Hofmann degradation (atracurium, cisatracurium) and slower hepatic and renal clearance
    - Pharmacodynamic: NMJ more sensitive; muscle contracts less forcefully
  - Renal failure, hepatic failure
  - Myasthenia gravis, Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS)
  - Some other neuromuscular diseases

## Factors that oppose block

- Demyelinating lesions, denervation, immobilisation, sepsis: ACh receptors are upregulated, so much larger doses are needed for the same block
- Hypercalcaemia, hyperkalaemia, alkalosis
- Drugs: theophylline, carbamazepine

## Benzylisoquinoliniums

### Tubocurarine (d-tubocurarine)
- Slow onset, long duration
- Marked histamine release, so hypotension with reflex tachycardia
- Large doses can cause ganglion block, worsening these effects
- Excreted unchanged in urine, some in bile
- Longer action in renal failure and in the elderly

### Mivacurium
- Main advantage: short action; routine neostigmine reversal may not be needed because enzymatic breakdown is rapid
- Dosage:
  - Given undiluted
  - ED95 0.08mg/kg (BJA 2004)
  - Intubating dose 0.15mg/kg; quoted ranges 0.1-0.2mg/kg and 0.15-0.2mg/kg (BJA 2004)
  - Onset 2-4mins; duration 12-18mins
  - Infusion 10-14 mcg/kg/min
- Effects:
  - Little direct cardiovascular effect
  - Marked histamine release, with significant hypotension above 0.2 mg/kg; less if injected slowly over 15–30 s
- Pharmacokinetics:
  - Mixture of three isomers; the more potent cis-trans and trans-trans isomers make up 95% and are rapidly hydrolysed by plasma cholinesterase
  - The cis-cis isomer has 10–15 times less blocking activity, is slowly hydrolysed and mostly excreted in urine; probably contributes little
  - Metabolised by plasma cholinesterase: prolonged with atypical enzyme, and in liver or kidney disease if cholinesterase activity is low

### Cisatracurium
- One of the ten stereoisomers in atracurium (the 1R-cis 1′R-cis isomer), about 15% of the mixture
- Dosage:
  - ED95 0.05 (BJA 2004)
  - Intubating dose 0.15-0.2mg/kg; also quoted 0.15mg/kg and 0.1mg/kg (BJA 2004)
  - About four times as potent as atracurium (also given as three to four times)
  - Onset 1.5-2min; duration 20-35min (slightly longer onset and duration than atracurium)
  - Infusion 1.5 mcg/kg/min
- Effects:
  - No histamine release; no direct cardiovascular effect
  - Higher potency means slower onset, but the dose can be raised to speed onset because histamine release is so unlikely
  - Smaller doses mean less laudanosine than an equipotent atracurium dose
- Pharmacokinetics:
  - About 77% Hofmann degradation; 15% excreted unchanged in urine
  - No direct ester hydrolysis by plasma esterases (unlike atracurium)
  - Renal failure slightly reduces clearance but does not prolong action

## Aminosteroids

### Pancuronium
- Dosage:
  - ED95 0.07mg/kg (BJA 2004)
  - Intubating dose 0.08-0.1mg/kg; 0.1mg/kg (BJA 2004)
  - Onset 1-3min; duration 60-90min (long), longer in renal impairment
- Effects:
  - Direct vagolytic and sympathomimetic actions: heart rate, blood pressure and cardiac output increase
  - No histamine release
  - May raise ICP
- Pharmacokinetics:
  - 60% excreted unchanged by the kidney, so longer action in renal impairment
  - Small fraction deacetylated in the liver; the 3-hydroxy metabolite has about half the potency of the parent
  - Longer action possible in hepatic insufficiency

### Pipecuronium
- Long-acting aminosteroid

### Rapacuronium
- Short-acting aminosteroid

### Vecuronium
- Presentation: unstable in solution, so supplied as freeze-dried (lyophilised) powder
- Dosage:
  - ED95 0.05mg/kg (BJA 2004)
  - Intubating dose 0.08-0.1mg/kg; quoted range 0.08-0.2mg/kg; 0.1mg/kg (BJA 2004)
  - 0.1 mg/kg gives onset in about 3 min and lasts about 30 min
  - Onset 3-5min (also quoted 2-4mins)
  - Duration 20-35min (also quoted 20mins)
  - Infusion 0.08-0.2mg/kg/hr
- Effects: no histamine release and no direct cardiovascular effect
- Pharmacokinetics:
  - More lipid-soluble than pancuronium, so substantial hepatic uptake and biliary excretion
  - Up to 50% excreted in bile; lower doses needed in hepatic insufficiency
  - About 30–40% deacetylated in the liver; 3-desacetylvecuronium is nearly as potent (∼80%) as the parent
  - In renal failure, 3-desacetylvecuronium can accumulate and add to block during long infusions

## Flashcards
- Q: What receptor occupancy is needed before a nerve stimulator detects block, and for complete block?
  - A: More than 70% to detect; at least 92% for complete block
- Q: Why do non-depolarising NMBs cause fade?
  - A: They block prejunctional nicotinic receptors that boost ACh release at high-frequency (>2 Hz) stimulation
- Q: Which muscle is standard for monitoring recovery, and which best predicts intubating conditions?
  - A: Adductor pollicis for recovery; laryngeal muscles (tracked by orbicularis oculi) for intubation
- Q: Name four electrolyte or acid-base changes that prolong non-depolarising block.
  - A: Hypocalcaemia, hypermagnesaemia, hypokalaemia, acidosis
- Q: Above what dose does mivacurium cause significant hypotension?
  - A: Above 0.2 mg/kg (from histamine release); slow injection over 15–30 s reduces it
