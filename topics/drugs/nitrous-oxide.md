# Nitrous oxide

Low-potency anaesthetic and analgesic gas (MAC 105%), with fast onset and offset; expands gas spaces and inactivates vitamin B12.

## Presentation

- Colourless, non-pungent gas with a slightly sweet smell and taste (suits inhalational induction)
- Not flammable, but supports combustion as well as oxygen does
- Physical properties:
  - Boiling point -88.5°C
  - Critical temperature 36.5°C (above room temperature)
  - Critical pressure 72 bar
  - MAC 105%
  - Blood/gas partition coefficient 0.47
  - Oil/gas partition coefficient 1.4
- Manufacture: ammonium nitrate heated to 250°C (thermal decomposition)
  - NH4NO3 gives N2O + 2H2O
  - Without close temperature control, contaminants form: NH3, N2, NO, NO2, HNO3; removed by scrubbers, water and caustic soda
- Storage:
  - Liquid with vapour above it, in French blue cylinders (C = 450 litres up to G = 9000 litres)
  - Gauge pressure 51 bar at 20°C (quoted as 44 to 54 bar, as SVP varies with temperature)
  - Gauge pressure reflects the SVP above the liquid, so does not reflect contents until only gas is left
  - Filling ratio (mass of N2O / mass of water the cylinder could hold): 0.75 in temperate regions, reduced to 0.67 in tropical regions to avoid explosion at higher temperatures
- Supply:
  - Hospital: almost always H cylinders on a manifold; bulk liquid storage pays only in very large hospitals
  - Local: E cylinders
  - Only weighing the cylinder shows what is left (tare weight, TW, is often stamped on it)
  - E cylinder pressure stays at 745 psig until the liquid runs out, and only starts to fall with about 400 L left
  - Above 745 psig: overfilled (full of liquid)
  - Wood's metal yoke
  - Liquid-to-gas change does not raise tank pressure much (not an ideal gas, easily compressed)
  - Rapid emptying cools the cylinder (latent heat of vaporisation), so pressure falls

## Administration

- Used at 30-70%
- Onset 30-60 sec; fast onset and offset because of low blood/gas solubility
- With oxygen as Entonox (50% oxygen and 50% nitrous oxide)
- Needs good scavenging to protect staff from long-term exposure

## Dosage

- MAC 105%, so 1 MAC cannot be given at atmospheric pressure without hypoxia
- Most anaesthetic machines deliver at most 0.67 MAC (inspired 70%)

## Mechanism of action

- Anaesthesia: non-competitive NMDA antagonism in the CNS
- Analgesia: release of endogenous opioids acting on opioid receptors (morphine-like); reversed by naloxone
- Anxiolysis: GABA-A activation

## Effects

- Cardiovascular (balanced):
  - Mild direct myocardial depression, usually offset by sympathetic stimulation
  - In health, BP, heart rate and circulation are largely unchanged without surgical stimulus
  - In patients who cannot raise sympathetic drive (or in cardiac failure), cardiac output can fall markedly
  - Does not sensitise the heart to catecholamines
- Respiratory (balanced):
  - Tidal volume falls slightly, respiratory rate rises; minute ventilation barely changes
  - Hypoxic ventilatory drive is markedly reduced, so patients can stay apnoeic despite marked hypoxaemia
  - Non-irritant; no bronchospasm
  - May increase PVR; generally avoided in pulmonary hypertension
- CNS:
  - Cerebral blood flow and volume increase, so ICP rises
  - Cerebral oxygen consumption increases
  - Analgesia: 30% by full mask equals 10mg morphine
- No effect on renal or hepatic function, or on uterine or skeletal muscle tone
- Does not potentiate NMBs, unlike the volatile agents
- Concentration effect:
  - Higher inspired concentration gives a faster rise in arterial concentration
  - Uptake into pulmonary capillaries exceeds nitrogen entering the alveolus, so alveolar volume shrinks; this acts like a vacuum, drawing in more gas, raising the agent's fractional concentration and increasing inflow
- Second gas effect:
  - Extension of the concentration effect (a theoretical concept) when N2O is given with another agent (e.g. isoflurane)
  - Rapid uptake of large N2O volumes at induction creates a partial vacuum, increasing uptake of the second agent

## Adverse effects

- Nausea and vomiting: probably multifactorial (bowel and middle ear distension, dopaminergic stimulation); thought to be less common in children
- Expansion of gas-filled spaces:
  - N2O is 20 times more soluble in blood than nitrogen, so enters gas spaces (bowel, pneumothorax) faster than nitrogen leaves
  - Continues until N2O partial pressure in the space equals blood; the space expands (bowel distension, larger pneumothorax)
  - Expands ETT cuffs, which can cause injury
- Vitamin B12 inactivation:
  - Oxidises the cobalt atom in B12, inactivating B12-dependent enzymes such as methionine synthetase, so DNA synthesis is impaired
  - Prolonged exposure: megaloblastic anaemia (bone marrow depression) and neurotoxicity (neuropathy, subacute combined degeneration of the cord), as in B12 deficiency
  - Unknown whether short perioperative exposure matters clinically
  - Recovery needs new methionine synthetase (a few days); folinic acid may help as another source of tetrahydrofolate
  - No effect on DNA synthesis with good scavenging (below 50 ppm); heavy unscavenged use in dental surgeries caused a subacute combined degeneration-like syndrome
  - Teratogenic in animal studies (via B12); prevented by folinic acid; never clearly shown in humans, but often avoided in the first trimester
  - Interaction with methotrexate: both inhibit folate
- Raises plasma homocysteine:
  - Contraindicated in MTHFR (methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase) deficiency; can worsen hyperhomocysteinaemia
  - Possible higher risk of coronary events: ENIGMA suggested a link with postoperative cardiovascular events; ENIGMA II then disproved it
- Diffusion hypoxia (Fink effect, third-gas effect):
  - On emergence breathing room air, large volumes of N2O leave blood into the alveoli faster than gases are taken up, diluting alveolar PO2
  - Effectively the reverse of the second gas effect
  - Needs two relatively insoluble gases at high concentration at once (e.g. N2O switched to room air, mostly nitrogen)
  - Nitrogen is less soluble than N2O, so it stays in the alveoli while N2O keeps pouring out; oxygen is diluted
  - Given at around 50% inspired, tissues can take up 12–14 L over a long case
  - N2O leaving can add up to 1 l/min to alveolar gas
  - Also dilutes accompanying volatile agents, speeding their fall at emergence (Anesthesiology 2011)
  - Prevent with 100% O2 for several minutes at the start of emergence as N2O is stopped

## Contraindications

- Gas-filled spaces:
  - Bowel obstruction
  - Pneumothorax, pulmonary cysts or bullae
  - Intracranial air (after skull fracture or surgery, or air-contrast studies): risk of brain injury
  - Intraocular gas bubble: catastrophic eye injury
  - Middle ear surgery: graft disruption
  - Air embolism
- First trimester of pregnancy
- Repeated general anaesthetics
- Raised ICP
- MTHFR deficiency

## Indications

- Inhalational anaesthetic alongside other agents in general anaesthesia
- Analgesic, e.g. Entonox

## Pharmacokinetics

- Almost entirely excreted unchanged by the lungs; a tiny amount passes through the skin
- Low blood solubility (blood/gas 0.47), so given in high concentration

## Entonox

- 50:50 mixture of N2O and O2
- The two gases dissolve in each other and behave unlike either gas alone
- Below the pseudocritical temperature of -7°C the mixture liquefies and separates (Poynting effect)

## History

- First anaesthetic use on a patient: dentist Horace Wells (with Gardner Quincy Colton and John Mankey Riggs) showed painless dental extraction on 11 December 1844
- Too weak for major surgery; diethyl ether was shown and accepted in October 1846, chloroform in 1847
- After Joseph Thomas Clover's "gas-ether inhaler" in 1876, hospitals commonly started with N2O then deepened with ether or chloroform
