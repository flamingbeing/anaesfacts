# Neuromuscular blockers

Overview of depolarising and non-depolarising neuromuscular blockers (NMBs): shared structure, differences and monitoring.

## Uses

- Facilitate intubation
- Improve surgical conditions
- Laryngospasm (suxamethonium)

## Classes

- Depolarising NMBs: agonists at ACh receptors, not broken down by AChE, so receptor activation is prolonged
- Non-depolarising NMBs: competitive antagonists of ACh at the postsynaptic nicotinic receptor; no conformational change in the receptor

## Structure

- All are quaternary ammonium compounds
- The permanent positive charge (N+) makes them highly water soluble (polar):
  - Volume of distribution limited to extracellular fluid
  - Do not cross the blood-brain barrier or placenta
- Bisquaternary (two permanent positive charges; more potent): e.g. suxamethonium, atracurium, pancuronium
- Monoquaternary (one permanent positive charge plus one tertiary amine): e.g. rocuronium, vecuronium; acidosis can make them more potent

## Differences between classes

- Depolarising (unless phase II block):
  - Fasciculations
  - No fade on TOF, no tetanic fade, no post-tetanic potentiation
  - Not reversed by anticholinesterases
- Non-depolarising:
  - Fade of the train-of-four (TOF) response, tetanic fade, post-tetanic potentiation
  - Reversed by anticholinesterases (or sugammadex)

## Prolonged blockade

- Suxamethonium: reduced plasma (pseudo)cholinesterase
- Non-depolarising agents: see the non-depolarising NMB topic

## Monitoring

- Suxamethonium gives phase I and phase II block
- Non-depolarising block looks essentially the same as phase II block

## Flashcards

- Q: Why do NMBs not cross the placenta?
  - A: Quaternary ammonium compounds: permanently charged and highly polar
- Q: What TOF features mark a non-depolarising block?
  - A: TOF fade, tetanic fade, post-tetanic potentiation
- Q: Which NMBs are bisquaternary?
  - A: Suxamethonium, atracurium, pancuronium (more potent)
- Q: What prolongs suxamethonium block?
  - A: Reduced plasma (pseudo)cholinesterase
