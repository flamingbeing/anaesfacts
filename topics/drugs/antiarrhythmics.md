# Antiarrhythmics

Overview of antiarrhythmic drugs: Vaughan-Williams classes, grouping by clinical use, and key points on amiodarone, class I drugs and digoxin.

## Classification

- Vaughan-Williams classification is the traditional scheme
  - Leaves out digoxin, adenosine and magnesium (sometimes grouped as class V: other mechanisms)
  - Many drugs span classes, e.g. sotalol has class I, II and III actions
- By clinical use
  - SVT: digoxin, adenosine, verapamil, beta-blockers, quinidine
  - VT: lidocaine, mexiletine
  - Both SVT and VT: amiodarone, flecainide, procainamide, disopyramide, propafenone, sotalol
  - Digoxin toxicity: phenytoin

## Vaughan-Williams classes

- Mnemonic for I to IV: "Some Block Potassium Channels" (Sodium, Beta-blockers, Potassium, Calcium)
- Class IA: sodium channel blockers that prolong the action potential (AP)
  - Slow atrial and ventricular conduction: depress phase 0, widen QRS
  - Also block IK, lengthening AP and effective refractory period (ERP), so QT lengthens
  - Procainamide, quinidine
- Class IB: shorten the AP
  - Depress phase 0 mainly in abnormal tissue; little effect on normal cells or the ECG
  - Shorten AP duration but not ERP, as sodium channels recover slowly from inactivation
  - Lignocaine (prototype)
- Class IC: no effect on the AP
  - Strongly slow atrial and ventricular conduction, widening QRS
  - No change in AP or QT
  - Flecainide (prototype), propafenone
- Class II: beta-blockers
  - Slow spontaneous phase 4 depolarisation, so SA node firing falls
  - Slow the AV node, so PR lengthens
  - Propranolol, esmolol
- Class III: potassium channel blockers
  - Block IK repolarising channels, prolonging phase 3 and AP duration
  - Sotalol, amiodarone (also has class I, II and IV actions)
- Class IV: calcium channel blockers
  - Block L-type calcium channels: slower depolarisation and AV conduction, longer PR
  - Verapamil, diltiazem
- Class I compared
  - Sodium channel block strength: IC > IA > IB
  - ERP: IA increases most, then IC; IB decreases it
- All antiarrhythmics can be proarrhythmic

## Class I: sodium channel blockers in more detail

- Block fast sodium channels that drive rapid phase 0 depolarisation in non-nodal cells (atrial and ventricular muscle, Purkinje fibres)
- Flatter phase 0 slope, with lower AP amplitude
- Nodal tissue (SA and AV nodes) depolarises via calcium currents, so no direct effect there through sodium block
- Main result: slower conduction in non-nodal tissue (negative dromotropy)
  - Faster depolarisation normally spreads the impulse quicker between cells
  - Slowing it can break re-entry circuits and stop re-entrant tachycardias
- Effects on repolarisation
  - The subclasses differ in effects on AP duration and ERP: IA increases, IB decreases, IC no change
  - These come from actions on phase 3 potassium (outward K+) channels, not from sodium block itself
  - Phase 0 depression: greatest with IC, least with IB, IA in between

## Amiodarone

- Iodinated compound; it and its metabolites block many ion channels
- Non-competitive beta antagonist
- Uses in AF
  - Rate control: sympatholytic and calcium-blocking actions slow AV conduction
  - Rhythm control: the most effective drug in head-to-head trials; also helps rate
- Needs a loading dose for faster onset
- Side effects
  - Bradycardia; may worsen haemodynamics in hypotension or recent decompensated heart failure (AHA 2014)
  - QTc prolongation
  - Outside the heart: interstitial lung disease, liver toxicity, hypo- or hyperthyroidism, eye toxicity, skin discolouration
  - Many toxicities are dose related

## Dronedarone

- Amiodarone without the iodine groups
- Avoid in permanent AF or heart failure

## Procainamide

- Side effect: drug-induced lupus

## Quinidine

- Side effect: torsades (as with all class IA drugs)

## Digoxin

- Cardiac glycoside
- Rate control in AF (AHA 2014)
- Oral: onset over 1hr (>1hr), peak about 6hrs; not ideal when fast rate control is needed
- Check levels periodically in those at risk of toxicity
- Toxicity
  - Warning signs: yellow vision, loss of appetite, nausea
  - Hyperkalaemia
  - Arrhythmias
  - Confusion
- Risk factors for toxicity
  - Renal impairment
  - Hypokalaemia, hypercalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia
  - Low body weight, older age (use with caution)
- Mortality concerns
  - Linked to higher mortality in the AFFIRM trial
  - Dose-related arrhythmias may contribute
- Contraindications
  - Heart block
  - Hypokalaemia, hypercalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia (increase toxicity)
  - Renal impairment: avoid if possible because of toxicity; adjust dose if used
  - Acute MI: may raise oxygen demand and cause ischaemia (debated)
    - May be considered in ACS with severe heart failure or haemodynamic instability (AHA 2014)
  - Accessory pathway (e.g. WPW)
