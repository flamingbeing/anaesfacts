# Adenosine

Endogenous purine nucleoside that briefly blocks the AV node; used to terminate or diagnose SVT.

## Presentation
- Colourless solution, 3 mg/ml vials
- Store at room temperature

## Administration
- Rapid IV bolus flushed with 10ml normal saline, ideally via a central line

## Dosage
- Adult: 6-12 mg IV bolus
  - For SVT, escalate 6mg, then 12mg, then 12mg
- Paediatric: 0.1-0.2mg/kg

## Mechanism of action
- Acts on A1 receptors in the SA and AV nodes (not found elsewhere in the heart)
  - Opens adenosine-sensitive K+ channels, hyperpolarising the membrane
  - Gi proteins reduce cAMP
  - Result: strong negative chronotropic effect at the AV node
- Adenosine receptors in general
  - Purinergic P1 receptors, G-protein coupled (metabotropic)
  - A1 and A3 inhibit adenylyl cyclase via Gi/o; A2A and A2B stimulate it via Gs

## Effects
- Slower heart rate and suppressed AV conduction
- Vascular smooth muscle relaxation and vasodilation, especially coronary
- Bronchospasm
- Neuromodulation

## Adverse effects
- Short lived because of the short half-life, but can be very distressing
- Cardiac: may trigger AF or flutter by shortening the atrial refractory period
- Vasodilation, flushing
- Bronchospasm: use with caution in asthma
- Interactions
  - Enhanced by dipyridamole, which blocks cellular uptake and so slows breakdown (also a key part of how dipyridamole vasodilates)
  - Antagonised by methylxanthines (theophylline, aminophylline especially) and caffeine

## Contraindications
- Second- or third-degree AV block
- Sick sinus syndrome

## Indications
- Distinguishing SVT from VT
  - SVT slows at least briefly; VT does not
- Converting re-entrant SVT that involves the AV node to sinus rhythm
- AF and flutter are not converted (their circuits do not involve the AV node), but the slower ventricular rate helps ECG diagnosis

## Pharmacokinetics
- Half-life under 10 seconds
- Rapidly taken up into red cells and other cells, then deaminated by adenosine deaminase to inosine
  - Inosine is broken down to hypoxanthine, xanthine and uric acid, excreted renally
  - Some deamination happens in plasma, but more slowly than inside cells
- Naturally occurring in all cells: adenine (purine base) plus D-ribose (pentose sugar)
