# Antidepressants

SSRIs, SNRIs, SARIs, NaSSAs and NDRIs: doses, half-lives, side effects and interactions that matter around anaesthesia.

## Groups

- Four main groups treat depression
  - Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)
  - Tricyclics (TCAs)
  - Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs)
  - Atypical agents (SNRI, SARI, NaSSA, NDRI below)

## SSRIs: overview

### Mechanism of action

- Selectively block neuronal reuptake of 5-HT, so synaptic serotonin and serotonergic activity increase

### Indications

- Most widely prescribed antidepressant class
- Drug of choice for mild to moderate depression
- First line for anxiety states (panic disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder)
- Also social phobia, post-traumatic stress disorder, eating disorders
- No more effective than older antidepressants, but better tolerated
  - Compared with TCAs: fewer anticholinergic effects, no postural hypotension, no slowed cardiac conduction, little effect on seizure threshold
  - Main advantage: relatively safe in overdose (venlafaxine may be the exception)

### Contraindications

- Absolute: mania
- Relative: before surgery when clotting is deranged (thought to impair platelet aggregation)

### Adverse effects

- Common: insomnia, agitation, headache, nausea, diarrhoea
- Gastrointestinal: nausea, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, constipation
- Weight loss or gain
- Autonomic: agitation, tremor, insomnia
- Sexual dysfunction: delayed ejaculation, anorgasmia, low libido, in both sexes
  - A leading cause of stopping treatment; often hidden, so ask if a patient stops for no stated reason
  - Options: lower dose, weekend drug holidays, switch to bupropion, sex therapy, sildenafil
- Platelet dysfunction: block 5-HT uptake into platelets
  - May matter with other antiplatelet drugs
- Elderly: hyponatraemia, GI bleeding
- Children: possible increase in suicidality (controversial)
  - US FDA black box warning on newer antidepressants since September 2004
  - Risk is small and many benefit, so individualise
- Cytochrome P450 inhibition (potentiates warfarin)

### Serotonin syndrome

- Potentially fatal when serotonergic drugs are combined
- Features: hyperreflexia, agitation, clonus, hyperthermia
- Commonest combination: MAOI plus SSRI
- Phenylpiperidine opioids, especially pethidine, have weak serotonin reuptake inhibition and can trigger it
- Fluoxetine with lithium or carbamazepine can also trigger it

### Drugs and prescribing

- Fluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline, fluvoxamine, citalopram, escitalopram
- Newer agents act on both serotonin and noradrenaline pathways
  - Dual reuptake block (venlafaxine), alpha-2 receptor block (mirtazapine)
- All SSRIs are similarly effective; choose on side effects and half-life
- Failure or intolerance of one does not predict the next; usually try several SSRIs before changing class

### Stopping SSRIs

- Abrupt stop of short half-life agents (paroxetine, venlafaxine) can cause dizziness, paraesthesia, myalgia, irritability, insomnia, visual disturbance
- Taper all SSRIs, especially short half-life ones

## Fluoxetine (Prozac)

- First SSRI approved in the US
- Effective, minimally sedating
- 50:50 mix of two equally active isomers

### Dosage

- PO starting dose 20 mg daily
- Therapeutic dose 20-80 mg
- Dose increases usually no more often than every 4 weeks (long half-life)
- Can be taken every other day

### Adverse effects

- Among the most activating SSRIs
  - Insomnia, anxiety or agitation, restlessness that can mimic akathisia
  - Take in the morning
- Nausea, anorexia
- Appetite suppression, may aid weight loss
- Sexual dysfunction, low libido
- Cardiovascular
  - Occasional bradycardia with syncope reported in elderly patients
  - Hypotension uncommon; conduction changes infrequent
  - Overdose of fluoxetine alone does not carry cardiovascular or CNS toxicity risk
- Interactions
  - Most potent hepatic cytochrome P450 inhibitor among SSRIs: raises levels of hepatically cleared drugs
  - Adding fluoxetine to a TCA can raise TCA levels two- to fivefold
  - Neuroleptics and fluoxetine may inhibit each other's metabolism
  - Some antiarrhythmics and alpha-adrenergic antagonists share the same enzymes: their effects are potentiated

### Indications

- Most used SSRI in children (best evidence)
- Chronic pain, e.g. with rheumatoid arthritis (like TCAs)

### Pharmacokinetics

- Well absorbed; hepatic metabolism by cytochrome P450
- Non-saturable enzymes stop levels rising unchecked
- Reduce dose in renal failure (accumulation)
- Long elimination half-life: 1 to 3 days acute, 4 to 6 days chronic
  - Active metabolite norfluoxetine: 4 to 16 days
- Washout before starting an MAOI: about 5 weeks
- No taper needed: long half-life prevents withdrawal

## Sertraline (Zoloft)

- Second SSRI in the US
- PO starting dose 50 mg daily; therapeutic 50-200 mg
- Half-life 25 hours (shorter than fluoxetine); possibly active metabolite 60 to 70 hours
- MAOI washout 14 days
- Weaker hepatic enzyme inhibitor
- Safest SSRI in pregnancy and breastfeeding
- Most GI side effects (more than fluoxetine)
- Activating, but less insomnia and agitation than fluoxetine

## Paroxetine (Paxil)

- Third SSRI in the US
- PO starting dose 10 mg daily; therapeutic 20-60 mg
- Half-life fairly short (24 hours), no active metabolites
- MAOI washout 14 days
- Less P450 inhibition than fluoxetine
- Enhances warfarin effect by competing for protein binding
- One of the two most sedating SSRIs (with fluvoxamine): take at night
- Anticholinergic effects (e.g. dry eyes)
- Avoid in first trimester: higher risk of congenital defects
- Higher breast milk levels than fluoxetine or sertraline
- Otherwise like other SSRIs

## Fluvoxamine (Luvox)

- Useful with coexisting insomnia
- Effective for obsessive-compulsive disorder; otherwise similar range to other SSRIs
- PO starting dose 50-100 mg daily; therapeutic 150-300 mg
- One of the two most sedating SSRIs: take at night
- Nausea and vomiting, possibly more than other SSRIs
- Less P450 inhibition than other SSRIs, but still clinically important interactions

## Citalopram (Celexa)

- Fourth SSRI in the US
- PO starting dose 20 mg daily; therapeutic 20-40 mg
- Dose-dependent QT prolongation: risk of torsades de pointes

## Escitalopram (Cipralex)

- S-isomer of citalopram (the more active stereoisomer)
- PO starting dose 10 mg daily; therapeutic 10-20 mg
- Fewer side effects; less P450 inhibition, so fewer interactions
- Dose-dependent QTc prolongation, possibly less than citalopram

## SNRIs: overview

- Serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, e.g. venlafaxine, duloxetine
- Block reuptake of both noradrenaline and serotonin
- Indications
  - Depression, anxiety disorders
  - Coexisting chronic pain, especially neuropathic pain and pain with depression
  - Fibromyalgia
- Side effect profile generally better than TCAs

## Duloxetine (Cymbalta)

- Indications: major depression, fibromyalgia, diabetic neuropathy
  - First antidepressant with a specific US pain indication (diabetic neuropathy)
- PO 30-60 mg OM to BD; start at 30 mg OD
- Avoid in severe renal impairment and chronic liver disease
- Do not combine with an MAOI (as for venlafaxine)
- Few anticholinergic effects
- Nausea, dry mouth, insomnia, sexual dysfunction
- No significant blood pressure change
- Serotonin syndrome possible with other serotonergic drugs
- Moderate CYP2D6 inhibitor

## Venlafaxine (Effexor)

- First SNRI, introduced in 1993
- Blocks noradrenaline and 5-HT reuptake (dopamine less so); little action at muscarinic, histamine or alpha receptors
- May potentiate CNS dopamine, like TCAs
- Efficacy similar to TCAs with better tolerability; may help neuropathic pain
- PO starting dose 37.5-75 mg/day; therapeutic 75-225 mg/day
- Half-life 5 hours; active metabolite 11 hours
- Do not combine with an MAOI; washout 14 days
- Overdose risk may resemble TCAs (proconvulsant and cardiac effects)
- No anticholinergic effects or postural hypotension, unlike TCAs
- Insomnia, sedation, nausea
- High doses: modest sustained rise in diastolic BP in 5% to 7% of patients
- Metabolised by P450 and weakly inhibits it

## SARI: trazodone

- Serotonin antagonist and reuptake inhibitor
- Blocks serotonin reuptake; an active metabolite may act as a serotonin agonist
- Treats depression; perhaps most useful for insomnia caused by SSRIs or bupropion
- Short half-life: 3 to 9 hours
- Sedation, orthostatic hypotension, nausea, vomiting
- Priapism in males
- No effect on cardiac conduction, but rare arrhythmias
- Less toxic in overdose than TCAs and MAOIs
- Avoid combining with an MAOI

## NaSSA: mirtazapine

- Noradrenergic and specific serotonergic antidepressant
- Less hyponatraemia
- Sedating (useful in insomnia)
- Weight gain (useful with poor appetite)

## NDRI: bupropion (Wellbutrin)

- Noradrenaline-dopamine reuptake inhibitor; structurally related to amphetamine
- Mechanism unclear, probably dopamine and noradrenaline reuptake block; does not inhibit MAO
- Indications
  - Major depression: improvement in 2 to 4 weeks
  - Smoking cessation
  - Not for anxiety disorders (stimulating)
- PO starting dose 100 mg daily; therapeutic 300-450 mg
  - State the formulation: IR, SR or XL (longest acting)
- Less sexual dysfunction (may reverse SSRI/SNRI effects), weight gain and sedation
- More seizures than other antidepressants (about 0.4%), especially at higher doses
  - Contraindicated with history of seizures, stroke, brain tumour, brain or closed head injury
- Rare ataxia and myoclonus
- Early stimulant-like effects in some
- Do not combine with an MAOI: hypertension and serotonin syndrome reported
- Like SSRIs: no anticholinergic effects, no postural hypotension, no significant conduction effects
- Unlike SSRIs: few significant interactions, rarely sexual dysfunction
