# Antipsychotics

Class overview: dopamine (and serotonin) antagonists for psychosis and agitation, with side effects that matter for anaesthesia.

## Classification

- "Antipsychotic" and "neuroleptic" mean the same thing
- Chemically varied: phenothiazines, thioxanthenes, butyrophenones
- By mechanism
  - Typical (first generation): dopamine D2 receptor antagonists
  - Atypical (second generation, serotonin-dopamine antagonists): lower affinity for and occupancy of dopamine receptors, plus 5-HT2A antagonism
- By chemistry
  - Phenothiazines: chlorpromazine, prochlorperazine, promethazine
  - Butyrophenones: haloperidol, droperidol
  - Atypicals: risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, ziprasidone, aripiprazole, clozapine

## Mechanism of action

- Antipsychotic effect: block dopamine (mainly D2) receptors in the basal ganglia and limbic forebrain
  - Extrapyramidal side effects reflect this dopamine blockade
- Antiemetic effect: block dopamine receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone of the medulla

## Onset

- Calming and less agitation straight away, from acute receptor blockade
- Thought disorder improves over 2–4 wk; full benefit takes weeks
- Keep the acute receptor effects separate from the slower chronic effects

## Administration and dosage

- IV: haloperidol 1 mg first, then 0.5 mg boluses
- PO
  - Quetiapine 12.5 mg at night; check ECG daily for QTc prolongation
  - Risperidone 0.5 mg
- Sublingual
- IM depot
  - For poor adherence or poor insight with relapse from non-adherence
  - Side effects last a month, so the first dose must be right
  - Give the oral form first, before the first injection
  - Start low; titrate every 2–4 wk

## Indications

- Agitation, sleep, psychosis, mania, mood stabilisation
- Schizophrenia and other psychoses, mood disorders with or without psychosis, depression with psychotic features, organic psychoses
- Violent behaviour, autism, Tourette's, somatoform disorders, dementia, OCD

## Prescribing approach

- No reason to combine antipsychotics
- First generation drugs are all equally effective; they differ in potency only
  - High potency: haloperidol, fluphenazine
  - Mid potency: perphenazine, loxapine
  - Low potency: chlorpromazine
- Atypicals are as effective as typicals, with a thought-to-be better side effect profile
- Prefer a drug the patient (or a family member) has responded to before
- No response in 4–6 wk: switch; response: titrate
- Duration: at least 6 months, usually lifelong

## Adverse effects

- Sedation
  - From alpha-adrenergic, muscarinic and histamine (H1) blockade
  - Tolerance develops with long-term use
- Opioid interactions: respiratory depression, miosis and sedation from opioids are likely increased; analgesia likely potentiated
- QTc prolongation (rare), predisposing to ventricular tachycardia
- Seizure threshold
  - Many lower it and cause seizure-like EEG patterns
  - Chlorpromazine slows the EEG, with some more burst activity and spiking
  - Sensory evoked potentials: often smaller amplitude and longer latency
- Dopamine blockade (mainly first generation)
  - Extrapyramidal effects (EPSE): acute dystonia, akathisia, pseudoparkinsonism, tardive dyskinesia
  - Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS): hyperthermia, rigidity, autonomic instability and altered consciousness over 24–72 hrs
  - Hyperprolactinaemia (loss of dopamine's inhibition of prolactin): galactorrhoea, gynaecomastia, rarely amenorrhoea, weight gain
  - Antiemetic (a useful effect), best for opioid-induced nausea and vomiting
- Serotonin blockade (mainly second generation): weight gain, metabolic syndrome, diabetes
- Histamine blockade: sedation, weight gain
- Muscarinic M1 blockade: dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, bowel obstruction, blurred vision, sedation
- Alpha blockade
  - Postural hypotension, lower systolic blood pressure
  - May blunt or abolish the pressor effect of adrenaline
  - Exaggerated hypotension during spinal anaesthesia
  - Miosis, sedation
- Sexual dysfunction

## Side effect summary

- Typicals: extrapyramidal symptoms, negative symptoms
- Atypicals: weight gain, metabolic syndrome, sedation, postural hypotension, QT prolongation
- Both: neuroleptic malignant syndrome
- All first generation drugs: young men at highest risk of acute dystonia; elderly women at highest risk of tardive dyskinesia

## By drug

- Haloperidol
  - High risk: EPSE, galactorrhoea
  - Low risk: weight gain, postural hypotension, sedation; least likely to lower seizure threshold
- Sulpiride
  - Low risk: weight gain, postural hypotension, sedation
- Clozapine
  - High risk: sedation, weight gain, hypersalivation; also agranulocytosis, seizures, myocarditis, pulmonary embolism
  - Low risk: tardive dyskinesia (least of all antipsychotics)
- Risperidone
  - High risk: EPSE, galactorrhoea (compared with other second generation drugs)
  - Low risk: sedation
- Olanzapine
  - High risk: weight gain (most of all antipsychotics), sedation
  - Low risk: EPSE, hyperprolactinaemia, QTc prolongation
- Quetiapine
  - High risk: QTc prolongation, postural hypotension, sedation
  - Low risk: EPSE (least likely), sexual dysfunction (least likely), hyperprolactinaemia
- Aripiprazole (lower potency)
  - Low risk: QTc prolongation (least likely), sexual dysfunction, EPSE, dyslipidaemia, weight gain, glucose intolerance, hyperprolactinaemia, postural hypotension, sedation

## By side effect

- Sedation: most with chlorpromazine, olanzapine, quetiapine; least with aripiprazole, sulpiride, haloperidol
- Galactorrhoea: most with haloperidol, risperidone; least with aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine
  - Bromocriptine (dopamine agonist) treats it but may worsen psychosis
- Postural hypotension (α1 blockade): most with chlorpromazine, quetiapine; least with aripiprazole, sulpiride, haloperidol
- Weight gain (5HT2C and H1 blockade): most with clozapine, olanzapine; least with aripiprazole, sulpiride, haloperidol
  - Manage with diet change, metformin
- Sexual dysfunction: most with haloperidol, risperidone, sulpiride, chlorpromazine (priapism); least with aripiprazole, quetiapine
- EPSE: most with first generation drugs (e.g. haloperidol, trifluoperazine) and risperidone; least with quetiapine, olanzapine, aripiprazole
- QTc prolongation: most with quetiapine, ziprasidone; least with aripiprazole, haloperidol
