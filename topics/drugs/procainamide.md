# Procainamide

Class Ia antiarrhythmic like quinidine but less vagolytic; side effects (hypotension, QT prolongation, lupus) limit its use.

## Administration
- Oral or IV
- IV infusion should later be switched to oral therapy

## Dosage
- Oral: up to 50 mg/kg/day in divided doses
- IV: 100 mg slowly, up to a maximum of 1 g
  - Then infusion 2–6 mg/min
- Termination of AF: 20-50 mg/min

## Mechanism of action
- Class Ia: blocks Na+ channels, slowing the rate of rise of phase 0 of the action potential
- Raises threshold potential and lengthens the refractory period without changing action potential duration
- Antagonises vagal tone, less than quinidine

## Adverse effects
- Cardiac
  - IV: hypotension, vasodilation, lower cardiac output
  - Can cause heart block
  - In SVT, ventricular rate may increase
  - QT prolongation and torsades de pointes
- Non-cardiac
  - Long-term: drug-induced lupus with positive antinuclear factor in 20–30% (often slow acetylators)
  - Gastrointestinal upset, fever, rash
  - Produces para-aminobenzoic acid, which reduces the antimicrobial effect of sulfonamides

## Indications
- SVT and ventricular tachyarrhythmias
- As effective as lidocaine for terminating VT

## Pharmacokinetics
- Well absorbed; oral bioavailability 75%
- Short half-life of 3 hours: needs frequent doses or slow-release forms
- Hepatic metabolism by amidases and by acetylation to active N-acetyl procainamide
- Acetylation shows genetic polymorphism (slow and fast acetylators); slow acetylators get more side effects
