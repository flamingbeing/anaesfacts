# Beta Blockers

Beta-adrenoceptor antagonists: slow the heart and reduce contractility; used for hypertension, angina, arrhythmias and to blunt airway responses.

## Classification

- 1st generation: non-selective
  - Propranolol, sotalol
- 2nd generation: beta-1 selective ("cardioselective")
  - Metoprolol, bisoprolol, atenolol, esmolol
- 3rd generation: extra alpha-1 antagonism (vasodilating)
  - Carvedilol, labetalol, nebivolol

## Mechanism of action

- Receptor selectivity
  - Wanted effects come from beta-1 block; beta-2 block mostly gives unwanted effects
  - Bisoprolol, atenolol, metoprolol, esmolol are beta-1 selective, but block beta-2 at high doses
  - Bisoprolol and nebivolol are the most beta-1 selective of the common agents
  - Non-selective agents block vasodilatory beta-2 receptors, so may be less effective than cardioselective ones
- Alpha-1 antagonism
  - 3rd generation agents add vasodilation through alpha-1 block
  - Carvedilol and labetalol are vasodilators
- Intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA): partial agonism
  - Acebutolol, labetalol, pindolol: partial agonists at 1 or more beta receptors
  - Partial agonist: cannot reach the full agonist's maximum response despite good receptor affinity
  - In theory: mild agonist effect when catecholamines are low, antagonist effect when sympathetic tone is high
  - Less likely to cause bradycardia and heart failure in mild cardiac failure; avoid in more severe failure (cardiac output falls further)
  - May reduce antihypertensive effect
- Membrane stabilising activity (MSA)
  - Propranolol, carvedilol
  - Low MSA: metoprolol, labetalol
- Lipid versus water solubility
  - Lipophilic (metoprolol, propranolol, nebivolol): cross the blood-brain barrier, more CNS side effects
    - Hence often used for migraine and essential tremor
  - Hydrophilic (atenolol, sotalol, nadolol): renally excreted, adjust dose to renal function

## Effects

- Cardiac
  - Negative inotropy and chronotropy
    - SA node automaticity falls, AV conduction time lengthens: bradycardia
    - Contractility falls
  - Better myocardial oxygen balance (basis for use in angina and around myocardial infarction)
    - Slower rate lengthens diastole, so coronary perfusion time and oxygen supply increase
    - Lower contractility reduces oxygen demand
    - These outweigh the negatives: longer systolic ejection, ventricular dilation, higher coronary resistance (beta-2 block)
  - Class II antiarrhythmics
- Blood pressure falls; mechanism not fully known, probably includes
  - Lower heart rate and cardiac output
  - Beta-1 block at the juxtaglomerular apparatus: less renin, less angiotensin II, so less vasoconstriction and aldosterone
  - Baroreceptors reset lower
  - Presynaptic beta-2 block reduces noradrenaline release
  - Central effects of some agents
  - Peripheral beta-2 block gives some vasoconstriction: little effect on BP, but cold hands and poor peripheral circulation
- Ocular: intra-ocular pressure falls (less aqueous humour), e.g. timolol

## Adverse effects

- Cardiac failure: use with extreme caution when ventricular function is poor
- Bronchospasm via beta-2 block with any agent at high enough dose
  - Cardioselective agents (atenolol, esmolol, metoprolol) preferred, but still extreme caution in asthma
- Metabolic
  - Blood sugar control involves liver, pancreas, fat, alpha and beta receptors, insulin, glucagon and catecholamines
  - Non-selective block may blunt the glucose response to exercise and hypoglycaemia
  - May raise resting glucose in hypertensive diabetics
  - Masks hypoglycaemia symptoms: avoid non-selective agents with hypoglycaemic drugs
  - Triglycerides increase, HDL falls
- CNS: depression, hallucinations, nightmares, paranoia, fatigue; sedation with lipophilic agents
- Gut: dry mouth, GI upset

## Contraindications

- Asthma
- Heart block
- Low ejection fraction

## Indications

- Hypertension
- Angina
- Around myocardial infarction: fewer arrhythmias, less reinfarction
- Phaeochromocytoma: prevents reflex tachycardia from alpha-blockade
- Hyperthyroidism (propranolol)
- Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (controls infundibular spasm)
- Anxiety with high catecholamines
- Glaucoma (topical)
- Migraine prophylaxis; essential tremor
- Blunting the response to laryngoscopy and extubation (esmolol)
- Arrhythmias (see below)

### Use as antiarrhythmics

- Antagonise catecholamines
  - Bradycardia by prolonging diastolic depolarisation (phase 4)
  - Depress contractility, prolong AV conduction
- Some have membrane stabilising (class I) activity, probably of little clinical importance
- Sotalol also has class III activity (blocks K+ channels, prolongs repolarisation)
- Limited to rate control in paroxysmal SVT, AF and catecholamine-driven sinus tachycardia

## Pharmacokinetics

- Main differences come from lipid solubility
- Low lipid solubility (atenolol): poor gut absorption, little hepatic metabolism, excreted mostly unchanged in urine
- High lipid solubility: well absorbed, extensive hepatic metabolism, shorter half-life (more frequent dosing), cross the blood-brain barrier (sedation, nightmares)
- Protein binding varies
