# Inhaled anaesthetics

Overview of nitrous oxide and the volatile halogenated ethers: actions, effects, metabolism, CO production and individual agents.

## Overview
- Group includes nitrous oxide and volatile halogenated ethers (isoflurane, halothane, sevoflurane, desflurane, enflurane)
  - Xenon has useful properties, but extracting it from air is costly, which limits clinical use
- Allow fast induction, easy titration during the case and fast emergence

## Mechanism of action
- Not fully understood
- GABA enhancement
  - Many inhaled agents boost the GABA system, as do benzodiazepines, propofol and etomidate
  - Potency tracks GABA potentiation, so GABA is thought to be a key target
- Other receptors: interact to varying degrees with many others, including NMDA and acetylcholine receptors

## Uptake and kinetics
- Solubility: desflurane least soluble (blood:gas and tissue), so fastest titration and recovery; halothane most soluble
- Offset (90% decrement time)
  - Desflurane: fastest and most consistent, whatever the case length (5–14 mins)
  - Sevoflurane: quick offset in short cases (less than 2 hours); nears isoflurane in long cases because of tissue accumulation

## Uses
- Volatile-based anaesthesia
  - Amnesia
  - Immobility and modest muscle relaxation
  - Blunting the adrenergic response to surgery
- Bronchodilation, including in ICU (AnaConDa)

## Environmental impact
- Greater greenhouse warming effect than propofol; desflurane and nitrous oxide are the worst

## Effects of volatiles

### Cardiovascular
- Cardiovascular depression: lower contractility and lower SVR (balance differs between agents)
- Heart rate: variable; desflurane can cause tachycardia, sevoflurane has little effect
- Blood pressure falls with dose; with desflurane, sevoflurane and isoflurane the fall in MAP is mainly due to lower SVR
- Isoflurane is more arrhythmogenic than sevoflurane and desflurane
- Sevoflurane prolongs QTc; contraindicated in congenital long QT syndrome
- Desflurane causes sympathetic stimulation
- Ischaemic preconditioning: prior volatile exposure may protect the heart against later ischaemia and reperfusion, possibly reducing perioperative myocardial injury
  - Especially isoflurane
  - Isoflurane also causes coronary steal (blood diverted away from ischaemic areas)

### Respiratory
- Respiratory depression
  - Smaller tidal volume, lower minute ventilation
  - Rate rises slightly or stays the same: shallow, fast breathing
  - Blunted response to hypercapnia and hypoxia
- Bronchodilation, except desflurane
  - Isoflurane and sevoflurane lower airway resistance; desflurane has no significant effect on bronchial tone
  - Desflurane causes laryngospasm and coughing above 6%

### Central nervous system
- Mixed cerebral effects
- Lower cerebral oxygen consumption
- Dose-dependent loss of autoregulation, leading to vasodilation and higher CBF
  - Autoregulation is adequately kept below 1 MAC
  - Above 1 MAC, CBF and therefore ICP increase
- Hyperventilation reverses the cerebral vasodilation

### Other effects
- Nausea and vomiting
- Muscle relaxation
  - Useful for surgery; potentiates neuromuscular blockers
  - May avoid the need for paralysis
  - May be contraindicated in myasthenia gravis
- Malignant hyperthermia trigger (nitrous oxide is not a trigger)
- Renal
  - All agents reduce renal blood flow, GFR and urine output
  - Inorganic fluoride from metabolism may be directly nephrotoxic
  - Sevoflurane forms compound A, but has not been linked to significant clinical renal toxicity
- Hepatic
  - All reduce hepatic blood flow
  - Some are metabolised in the liver to trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), which can cause liver toxicity (halothane hepatic necrosis; weak link with sevoflurane)
- Obstetric: dose-dependent fall in uterine contractility (some avoid them in GA for caesarean section)
- Carbon monoxide: all agents can produce CO with desiccated CO2 absorbents
- For N2O effects, see the nitrous oxide topic

## Metabolism
- Much less is removed by metabolism than is exhaled
- Microsomal enzymes, mainly in liver and kidney; approximate metabolism:
  - Halothane 25%
  - Sevoflurane 5%
  - Enflurane 2.5%
  - Isoflurane 0.2%
  - Desflurane 0.02%
  - Nitrous oxide zero per cent
- Hepatic cytochrome P450 (CYP2E1) breaks carbon–halogen bonds, releasing F−, Cl− and Br−, which may damage liver or kidney
  - The C–F bond is stable and barely metabolised; C–Cl, C–Br and C–I are progressively easier to break

## Carbon monoxide production

### Mechanism
- Dry (desiccated) CO2 absorbents such as soda lime and Baralyme can degrade inhaled agents to CO
- Their strong bases (sodium and potassium hydroxide) remove labile protons from the anaesthetic molecule
- Newer absorbents without KOH or NaOH (e.g. Amsorb) do not form CO, but are fairly expensive

### Factors that increase CO production
- Agent: desflurane at least equal to enflurane, then isoflurane, much more than halothane = sevoflurane
- Drier absorbent
- Absorbent type (Baralyme more than soda lime)
- Higher temperature
- Low fresh gas flow
- Higher agent concentration
- Small patient relative to absorbent amount (more CO exposure per unit body mass)

### Reducing the risk
- Switch the machine off at the end of the day (high oxygen flow left on overnight dries the soda lime)
- Rehydrate dried absorbents
- Use absorbents without strong bases

## The ideal inhaled agent
- No current agent has all these; some "negative" features (e.g. not epileptogenic) reflect side effects of existing agents

### Physical properties
- Stable to light and heat
- Inert with metal, rubber and soda lime
- Preservative-free
- Not flammable or explosive
- Pleasant smell
- Environmentally friendly
- Cheap

### Biochemical properties
- High oil:gas partition coefficient; low MAC
- Low blood:gas partition coefficient
- Not metabolised
- Non-toxic
- Acts only on the CNS
- Not epileptogenic
- Some analgesia

## Specific agents

### Enflurane
- MAC about 1.7%
- Halogenated ethyl methyl ether; structural isomer of isoflurane; now superseded
- High concentrations give a 3 Hz spike-and-wave EEG pattern, consistent with grand mal activity
- Avoided in renal impairment: its metabolism releases F−, which can cause reversible nephropathy
- Has also been reported to cause hepatic necrosis, but less often than halothane because less is metabolised

### Halothane
- MAC about 0.75%
- Halogenated hydrocarbon; unstable in light and corrodes some metals
- Stored with 0.01% thymol to stop free bromine being released
- Dissolves into rubber and can leach back into circuits after the vaporiser is off
- Still has niche use; largely replaced by sevoflurane
- Cardiovascular
  - Cardiac output falls, from raised vagal tone and direct myocardial depression
  - Sensitises the myocardium to catecholamines; use local anaesthetic with adrenaline cautiously
- Respiratory
  - Sweet, non-irritant smell: suitable for inhalational induction
  - Higher concentrations markedly reduce minute ventilation
  - Strong bronchodilator, useful in asthma
- CNS
  - Lowers cerebral oxygen requirement
  - Raises CBF more than any other volatile, with significant ICP increase above 0.6 MAC
- Metabolism
  - Up to 25% undergoes oxidative metabolism by hepatic cytochrome P450 to trifluoroacetic acid, Br− and Cl−
  - In a hypoxic liver, reductive metabolism (producing F− and other reduced metabolites) predominates; these are toxic but probably not the cause of halothane hepatitis
- Hepatotoxicity: two forms
  - Reversible, often subclinical, with raised transaminases; probably from hepatic hypoxia
  - Fulminant hepatic necrosis (halothane hepatitis)
    - Trifluoroacetyl chloride (oxidative metabolite) acts as a hapten, binding liver proteins and triggering antibodies
    - Diagnosis of exclusion
    - Incidence: children 1 in 80 000–200 000; adults 1 in 2500–35 000
    - Risk factors: repeated exposure, obesity, middle age, female sex
    - Mortality 50–75%
  - Avoid if given within the previous 3 months, after a previous reaction to halothane, or with existing liver disease
  - Other volatiles could in theory do the same, but their lower metabolism makes it increasingly unlikely

### Chloroform
- Synthesised in 1831; anaesthetic properties first described in 1842
- John Snow popularised obstetric anaesthesia by giving chloroform to Queen Victoria for the births of Prince Leopold (1853) and Princess Beatrice (1857)

### Diethyl ether
- First public demonstration of modern anaesthesia: October 16, 1846 ("Ether Day"), Ether Dome, Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston
- Largely replaced chloroform thanks to its better therapeutic index

## Flashcards
- Q: Approximate metabolism of sevoflurane and desflurane?
  - A: 5% and 0.02%
- Q: Which volatile is not a bronchodilator and irritates the airway above 6%?
  - A: Desflurane
- Q: Which agent produces the most CO with dry absorbent?
  - A: Desflurane
- Q: Incidence of halothane hepatitis in adults?
  - A: 1 in 2500–35 000; mortality 50–75%
- Q: Above what MAC do volatiles raise ICP?
  - A: Above 1 MAC (halothane above 0.6 MAC)
