# Paediatric neuromuscular blockers and reversal

How age changes the response to suxamethonium, non-depolarising blockers and their reversal.

## Physiological differences
- Immature muscle: the infant diaphragm is paralysed at the same time as peripheral muscles (later in adults)
- Neuromuscular transmission is immature at birth
    - Motor nerves release about a three-fold smaller amount of ACh (maximal release one third of adult)
    - Leads to a three-fold increase in sensitivity to non-depolarising blockers
    - Motor end-plate response to suxamethonium appears normal in newborns
- Large extracellular fluid volume: larger volume of distribution, which alters dose needs
- High cardiac output: faster circulation, so drugs reach and leave their site of action faster

## Suxamethonium
- Neonates and infants need more per kg than older children for the same block
    - Due to rapid distribution into a large extracellular volume, not a changed receptor response
- Doses
    - 3 mg/kg in neonates
    - 2 mg/kg in infants
    - 1-1.5 mg/kg in older children
- Duration similar at all ages
- Fasciculations often absent, especially in young children
- Side effects and contraindications as in adults, plus
    - Severe bradycardia
    - Malignant hyperthermia and hyperkalaemia in undiagnosed myopathies and dystrophinopathies
- So most limit it to rapid-sequence induction and treatment of laryngospasm
- Use falling as alternatives exist (e.g. mivacurium, or TIVA for rigid bronchoscopy)

## Non-depolarising blockers
- Similar doses at all ages
    - Larger volume of distribution gives lower plasma levels than in adults
    - But the immature neonatal junction is more sensitive
    - Net effect: dose changes little with age
- Onset often very fast in young children (high cardiac output)
- Duration varies
    - Shorter: atracurium and mivacurium (organ-independent elimination)
    - Prolonged: rocuronium and vecuronium (organ-dependent elimination is slower)
- Indications, contraindications and side effects as in adults; choice is often personal preference

## Reversal
- Full reversal matters most in neonates and small infants: little respiratory reserve
    - High alveolar ventilation, high closing capacity and high oxygen consumption mean even slight residual block can badly impair oxygenation
- Antagonism generally works normally in infants; recovery after reversal is faster than in adults
- Neostigmine: 35 mcg/kg gives maximal antagonism at all ages; 50 mcg/kg usually given as a safety margin

## Flashcards
- Q: Suxamethonium dose in neonates, infants and older children?
  - A: 3 mg/kg, 2 mg/kg and 1-1.5 mg/kg
- Q: Why do neonates need more suxamethonium per kg?
  - A: Larger extracellular volume, so a larger volume of distribution
- Q: How does neonatal sensitivity to non-depolarising blockers compare with adults?
  - A: About three-fold more sensitive, but similar doses are used because of the larger volume of distribution
- Q: Usual neostigmine reversal dose in children?
  - A: 50 mcg/kg (35 mcg/kg gives maximal antagonism)
