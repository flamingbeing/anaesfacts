# Opioids

Drugs acting at opioid receptors (mainly mu) to give analgesia and sedation; respiratory depression is the most serious adverse effect.

## Definitions
- Opiate: a naturally occurring substance with morphine-like actions
- Opioid: the wider term, including synthetic drugs with affinity for opioid receptors

## Structural classification
- Group 1: naturally occurring (opiates), two chemical classes
  - Phenanthrenes: morphine, codeine, thebaine
  - Benzylisoquinolines: papaverine
- Group 2: semisynthetic (made by modifying natural opioids)
  - Heroin (diamorphine, diacetylmorphine)
  - Hydrocodone (dihydrocodeinone), hydromorphone
  - Oxycodone (dihydrohydroxycodeinone), oxymorphone
  - Dihydromorphine, dihydrocodeine, morphinone
  - Buprenorphine (partial agonist)
  - Naloxone and naltrexone (antagonists)
- Group 3: synthetic
  - Phenylpiperidines: meperidine (pethidine), fentanyl, sufentanil, alfentanil, remifentanil
  - Diphenylpropylamines: methadone
  - Benzomorphans: pentazocine (partial agonist)
  - Morphinans: levorphanol, butorphanol (partial agonist)
  - Others: tramadol, tapentadol

## Opioid allergy and structure
- Rash after morphine is usually from histamine release
- True anaphylaxis to opioids is very rare (< 1%); local reactions from preservatives or histamine are commoner
- With a true allergy, choose a structurally different opioid
  - Groups 1 and 2 are structurally close: avoid all of them if truly allergic to one
  - Group 3 drugs differ enough to be given without cross-reactivity, and also differ from each other

## Functional classification (mu receptor binding)
- Pure agonists: full mu binding; best for acute pain
  - At equianalgesic doses the common parenteral opioids work equally well with similar side effects
- Partial agonists: only partial mu binding, so limited use for pain
- Agonist-antagonists: kappa agonist, mu antagonist
  - Ceiling on respiratory depression, but also a ceiling on analgesia, which limits use in acute pain

## Mechanism of action
- Act on mu, kappa and delta receptors in brain and spinal cord, mimicking endogenous endorphins
- Most potent clinical analgesics are mu agonists
- Opioid receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors

## Routes
- IV: fentanyl, morphine, tramadol, pethidine
- IM: tramadol, pethidine
- SC: fentanyl, morphine
- Transdermal: fentanyl patch
- Oral: morphine, codeine, tramadol

## Pharmacokinetics
- Onset
  - More lipid-soluble opioids act faster (easier transfer to the site of action)
  - Opioids are weak bases; the free-base share depends on pH and pKa, and the free base is more lipid-soluble
  - All are partly bound to plasma proteins (albumin and α1-acid glycoprotein)
  - Only the unionised, unbound fraction diffuses, so onset depends on both lipid solubility and protein binding
- The receptor binds the protonated form, so effect intensity tracks the ionised concentration at the site of action
- Offset is mainly by redistribution and hepatic metabolism, not urinary excretion
- Liver disease
  - The usual degree of perioperative liver impairment has little effect on most opioids (liver transplant excepted)
  - Only severe hepatic failure changes morphine or fentanyl clearance noticeably
- Renal failure matters most for meperidine, morphine, hydromorphone and their prodrugs
- Hepatic enzymes
  - Enzyme induction (e.g. early alcoholism) can raise hepatic metabolic capacity
  - CYP3A4 metabolises many opioids, but the polymorphic CYP2D6 matters more clinically (codeine)
- Accumulation
  - Accumulating opioids show a marked rise in context-sensitive half time with long infusions
  - Remifentanil is broken down as fast as it is given, so it does not
- Dosing scalar: clearance relates best to lean body mass; ideal body weight is a reasonable substitute
- Haemorrhagic shock: central clearance and central and second-compartment volumes fall; reduce the dose substantially
- Age
  - Neonates eliminate nearly all opioids more slowly (immature metabolism including CYP450); this normalises within the first year
  - Elderly need less, mainly from pharmacodynamic sensitivity; kinetic changes (smaller central volume, lower clearance) are minor

## Contraindications
- Few absolute contraindications apart from hypersensitivity to a specific opioid
- Relative contraindications are mostly metabolic, e.g. hydromorphone and morphine half-lives lengthen in renal impairment

## Anaesthetic effects
- Analgesia
  - IV opioids are the mainstay of surgical pain control, and opioids are the commonest drugs for acute and chronic pain
  - No analgesic ceiling; adverse effects are the limit
  - Nociceptive pain usually responds; neuropathic pain may respond poorly and need higher doses
- Sedation
  - High doses alone can cause unconsciousness, but unpredictably: not suitable as sole induction agents and not complete anaesthetics
  - MAC falls with dose, non-linearly, with a ceiling
  - Synergy with hypnotics such as propofol lowers the effect-site concentration needed for loss of consciousness; effect on BIS is inconsistent
  - Dose-dependent EEG changes resemble volatile agents but plateau at high doses
- No reliable amnesia

## Cardiovascular effects
- Individual response (benefit and side effects) varies widely, likely genetic and environmental
- Lower sympathetic tone and raise vagal tone (central vagal nucleus stimulation): bradycardia and hypotension, mainly CNS mediated
  - Hypovolaemic patients, or those reliant on high sympathetic tone or catecholamine infusions, are prone to hypotension
  - Meperidine rarely causes bradycardia and can cause tachycardia (its metabolite normeperidine resembles atropine)
- Little or no direct myocardial depression, much less than many IV and inhaled anaesthetics
- Overall antiarrhythmic (direct action on cardiac ion currents); conduction problems are rare
- High-dose opioids preserve myocardial perfusion and O2 supply-demand as well as or better than volatile techniques
  - No effect on coronary vasomotor tone or myocardial metabolism, no steal, coronary responsiveness intact
  - May mimic ischaemic preconditioning (possible cardioprotection); clinical outcome benefit unproven
- Mild systemic vasodilation, possibly pulmonary vasodilation
  - Histamine release with morphine, codeine and meperidine
  - Fentanyl, alfentanil, sufentanil and remifentanil do not release histamine, so hypotension is less common
- Blunt the stress response: sympathetic (autonomic) and hormonal (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis)
- Useful when haemodynamic instability is expected

## Respiratory effects
- Respiratory depression: the most serious adverse effect
  - Mu-mediated, dose-dependent, acting directly on brainstem respiratory centres
  - Reduced ventilatory response to CO2: higher apnoeic threshold and higher end-tidal CO2
  - Hypoxic drive is less affected than CO2 response, but supplemental oxygen removes the hypoxic stimulus and can deepen depression
  - Respiratory rate falls markedly with longer expiration, so minute ventilation falls; tidal volume changes little (slow deep breaths)
  - Breath interval monitoring is a sensitive measure of fentanyl effect
  - High doses can stop spontaneous breathing without unconsciousness; patients often breathe when told to
  - Untreated pain causes shallow breathing and atelectasis, so good opioid analgesia can improve postoperative respiratory function
  - Naloxone is the standard treatment
- Factors that worsen or prolong respiratory depression
  - Other CNS depressants
  - Older age (more sensitive, and higher plasma levels per weight-based dose)
  - Neonates and infants (immature blood-brain barrier lets opioids into the brain easily)
  - Hypocapnia from hyperventilation; anxious or painful hyperventilating patients can become briefly apnoeic after small IV doses as the apnoeic threshold shifts
- Airway reflexes
  - Potent antitussive; blunt somatic and autonomic responses to airway instrumentation (ETT tolerated without bucking)
  - Can impair protective airway reflexes
  - Remifentanil can suppress cough at extubation
  - IV boluses of fentanyl, sufentanil and alfentanil cause brief cough in up to 50% of patients
- Bronchospasm from histamine release (especially morphine)
- Muscle and chest wall rigidity
  - Raised muscle tone; mild forms such as hoarseness in awake patients
  - Significant rigidity usually starts at or after loss of consciousness
  - Lowers compliance and FRC and impairs ventilation; vocal cord closure is the main cause of difficult bag-mask ventilation
  - Raises O2 consumption and ICP
  - Thought to be central (opioid receptors acting on dopamine and GABA pathways in substantia nigra and striatum), not a direct muscle effect
  - Prevented by muscle relaxants; reversed by naloxone; thiopental induction doses or small doses of diazepam or midazolam can prevent or treat it

## Central nervous system effects
- Cerebral blood flow
  - Little effect in general
  - With vasodilating anaesthetics or N2O, opioids tend to cause cerebral vasoconstriction and lower CBF
  - Alone, or with vasoconstricting anaesthetics: no change or a small rise
- ICP: minimal effect with controlled ventilation; sedation does not change ICP after head injury
- Metabolic rate: neuroexcitation and focal seizure activity can raise regional metabolism
- Seizures: no risk at normal doses without epilepsy, but high doses can cause neuroexcitation
  - Normeperidine (N-demethylated pethidine metabolite) causes myoclonus and seizures; risk higher in renal disease
- No clear neuroprotection

## Gastrointestinal effects
- PONV: intraoperative opioids are a known risk factor
  - Stimulate the chemoreceptor trigger zone via 5-HT3 and dopamine receptors; the vomiting centre itself is depressed
- Slower gastric emptying, longer transit, constipation and ileus (spastic immobility via myenteric plexus receptors)

## Renal and urinary effects
- Mu activation: antidiuresis and less electrolyte excretion; kappa activation: diuresis with little electrolyte change
- Renal function largely preserved; changes are secondary to systemic and renal haemodynamics
- Urinary retention, especially after intrathecal opioids
  - Dose-dependent reduction in detrusor contraction and urge sensation; may tighten the urethral sphincter
- Ureteric tone increases

## Opioid-induced hyperalgesia
- High-dose intraoperative remifentanil gives a small but significant rise in postoperative pain; fentanyl can too
  - All potent opioids can probably cause it; ultrapotent ones like remifentanil may carry more risk
- Abrupt withdrawal may contribute
  - Remifentanil at 2.5 ng/ mL for 30 minutes then stopped abruptly: hyperalgesia common
  - Weaned by 0.6 ng/mL every 5 minutes: no hyperalgesia
- Mechanism: sensitisation of pronociceptive pathways, probably NMDA receptor involvement
- Prevention
  - Ketamine (supports NMDA role); other NMDA-active drugs: methadone, buprenorphine, butorphanol, N2O
  - Parecoxib (COX-2 inhibitor) before remifentanil, suggesting COX-2 involvement
  - Anaesthetic choice: remifentanil hyperalgesia seen with sevoflurane but not with propofol (one study)
- Linked to peripheral and central sensitisation and so to chronic postoperative pain

## Pruritus
- A persistent problem with opioid analgesia; not caused by histamine, as non-histamine-releasing opioids also cause it
  - Usually no rash (morphine a possible exception)
- Worst after intrathecal or epidural use
- Antihistamines may help, possibly through sedation
- Naloxone reverses it but also reverses analgesia
- Mixed or partial agonists (nalbuphine, butorphanol) are increasingly used: partial mu antagonism with kappa analgesia kept

## Obstetrics
- Maternal opioids reduce fetal heart rate variability
  - Fetal acidosis increases transfer across the placenta
  - Limiting opioids to the first stage of labour reduces neonatal effects
- Fentanyl and morphine reach breast milk at milk-to-plasma ratios of 2 to 3:1, but newborn exposure is negligible

## Other effects
- Thermoregulation: thresholds probably lowered about as much as with volatile agents
  - Pethidine uniquely stops or reduces shivering
  - Remifentanil increases postanaesthetic shivering
- Miosis: opioids remove inhibition of the Edinger-Westphal nucleus, constricting the pupil; reversed by atropine
  - Normally light excites the nucleus, and hypercarbia, hypoxia and pain inhibit it
  - Pupillometry may help guide intraoperative opioid dosing
- Endocrine: growth hormone, TSH and prolactin increase; LH, testosterone, oestradiol and oxytocin fall; effects on vasopressin and ACTH conflict
  - Main endocrine problem with misuse: hypogonadism, especially in men
- Biliary: sphincter of Oddi tone and bile duct pressure increase, usually with little clinical consequence
  - No evidence that morphine is contraindicated in acute pancreatitis, despite traditional teaching
- Sleep: can disrupt sleep on the first postoperative night; effects on circadian rhythm unclear
- Mood: euphoria, dysphoria, mood change
- Hallucinations: uncommon but important, possibly from dopamine dysregulation
- Evoked potentials: sensory (posterior tibial, median nerve) and motor evoked potentials largely unaffected, so spinal cord monitoring works under opioid anaesthesia
- Liver: may reduce ischaemia-reperfusion injury, possibly helpful in liver surgery
- Teratogenicity of fentanyl, sufentanil and alfentanil appears minimal in animals
- Neonatal abstinence syndrome in babies of mothers with opioid use disorder or on prescribed opioids
- Fentanyl, sufentanil and alfentanil at induction help prevent a rise in intraocular pressure
- Cancer: epidemiology suggests more recurrence after opioid-based GA than after LA or regional techniques; no direct evidence to change practice

## Overdose
- Recognition
  - a) Oversedation: sedation score 3 or unrousable
  - b) Hypoventilation: respiratory rate 8 breaths per minute or less
- Management
  - Stop the opioid infusion and other sedating drugs (e.g. midazolam, ketamine)
  - Support airway and breathing, with intubation and ventilation if needed
  - IV naloxone 0.4 mg (full dose), up to 2 mg IV for diagnosis, repeated as needed
  - Observe until awake after naloxone has worn off; consider a naloxone infusion until alert without it (up to >48 h with long-acting opioids)
- Renarcotisation: any agonist with a longer half-life than naloxone can cause it
- Secondary peaks in plasma level can occur from release out of muscle, lung, fat and gut

## Tolerance, dependence and withdrawal
- Tolerance: a fixed dose gives less analgesia over time, so doses escalate
- Dependence: a physiological state shown by withdrawal when the drug is stopped abruptly
- Withdrawal: irritability, anxiety, insomnia, sweating, yawning, rhinorrhoea, lacrimation; later fever, chills, myalgia, abdominal cramps, diarrhoea, tachycardia
  - Self-limiting, typically 3–7 days
- Addiction: use causing physical, psychological or social harm, continued despite it
- Mechanisms: not fully known; genetic, molecular, cellular, physiological and functional factors
  - Changes in mu receptor density are not essential
  - Short-term desensitisation probably via receptor phosphorylation by protein kinase C
  - Also implicated: adenylyl cyclase inhibition, reduced protein kinase A, upregulated cAMP pathway, beta-adrenergic receptor kinase (a GRK that rapidly phosphorylates and desensitises GPCRs)
- Differential tolerance: different opioid effects develop tolerance at different rates and degrees
- Acute tolerance can develop quickly (e.g. remifentanil), not only with chronic use
- Chronic high-dose users are probably tolerant, but the size of the effect on analgesia and respiratory depression is hard to predict
  - Their risk of overdose and death rises with dose

## Receptors
- Classical (naloxone-sensitive, IUPHAR): MOP (mu), KOP (kappa), DOP (delta)
  - Names: mu from morphine, kappa from ketocyclazocine, delta from vas deferens
- NOP: non-classical, a non-opioid member of the family
- Clinical opioid analgesia is mainly via MOP

## Endogenous ligands
- Each receptor has selective peptides cleaved from precursor prohormones
- MOP: beta-endorphin (precursor pro-opiomelanocortin, POMC); also endomorphin-1 and endomorphin-2 (precursor unknown)
  - Beta-endorphin acts at all three classical receptors; endorphin means "endogenous morphine"
- DOP: met-enkephalin and leu-enkephalin (precursor pro-enkephalin)
- KOP: dynorphin A and dynorphin B (precursor pro-dynorphin)
- NOP: nociceptin, or orphanin FQ (N/OFQ) (precursor pre-pro-nociceptin)

## Sites and mechanism of analgesia
- MOP receptors are throughout the CNS and in peripheral neural and non-neural tissue
- Supraspinal: dense in the periaqueductal grey (PAG)
  - Opioids block resting GABA inhibition of PAG neurons that project to the rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM)
  - The RVM then releases its brake on descending inhibitory control, increasing antinociceptive output to the dorsal horn
  - MOP also in basal ganglia and cerebral cortex
- Spinal: substantia gelatinosa of the dorsal horn; reduce glutamate and substance P release, cutting transmission from C and Aδ fibres
- Peripheral receptors drive reduced gut motility, urinary retention and pruritus
  - Immune cells at inflamed sites can release opioid-like peptides acting on primary sensory neurons
- Mood and reward: dopaminergic pathways, especially dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens

## Cellular mechanism
- All are Gi-coupled GPCRs
- Inhibit adenylyl cyclase, lowering cAMP
- Close voltage-sensitive calcium channels and open G-protein-gated inwardly rectifying potassium (GIRK) channels, hyperpolarising the cell
- Result: lower neuronal excitability, less impulse transmission and less transmitter release

## Effects by receptor
- MOP: analgesia, euphoria, sedation, respiratory depression, bradycardia, nausea and vomiting, reduced gut motility; inhibits acetylcholine release
- DOP: analgesia
  - Agonists also slow the gut and depress breathing, limiting interest; may affect mood and movement
  - No clinical DOP drugs yet
- KOP: analgesia, sedation, psychotomimetic effects, diuresis
  - Spiradoline and enadoline reached clinical trials but caused diuresis, sedation and dysphoria below analgesic doses
  - Advantage: no respiratory depression; dysphoria limits use
- NOP
  - Spinal N/OFQ: analgesia at high doses, hyperalgesia and allodynia at low doses
  - Supraspinal (intracerebroventricular): pronociceptive anti-analgesia by inhibiting endogenous opioid tone
  - Involved in morphine tolerance (upregulated in morphine-tolerant mice); NOP antagonists give long-lasting analgesia and reduce tolerance, a possible future target

## Other opioids
- Sufentanil: fentanyl analogue, 5–10 times more potent than fentanyl; used for induction and maintenance
- Hydromorphone: long-acting, for postoperative pain

## Flashcards
- Q: Which opioids can be given to a patient with true allergy to morphine?
  - A: Group 3 synthetic opioids (e.g. fentanyl, methadone), as they are structurally distinct from natural and semisynthetic ones
- Q: How do opioids change the pattern of breathing?
  - A: Rate falls markedly with long expiration; tidal volume changes little; higher apnoeic threshold and end-tidal CO2
- Q: Adult naloxone dose for opioid overdose?
  - A: 0.4 mg IV, up to 2 mg for diagnosis, repeated as needed; consider infusion (up to >48 h for long-acting opioids)
- Q: Criteria for opioid overdose?
  - A: Sedation score 3 or unrousable, or respiratory rate 8 per minute or less
- Q: Cellular action of opioid receptors?
  - A: Gi-coupled: inhibit adenylyl cyclase, close voltage-sensitive Ca channels, open GIRK K channels, hyperpolarising neurons
