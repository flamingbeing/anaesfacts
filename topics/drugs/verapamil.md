# Verapamil

Non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker acting mainly on the SA and AV nodes; used for SVT, AF/flutter rate control and angina.

## Presentation

- Tablets 20–240 mg, including film-coated and modified-release forms
- Oral suspension 40 mg/5 ml
- Clear colourless IV solution, 2.5 mg/ml
- Racemic mixture; synthetic derivative of papaverine

## Dosage

- IV 0.1 mg/kg over 2 mins as the first bolus; may repeat after 15 mins

## Mechanism of action

- Competitive Ca2+ channel antagonist
- Blocks Ca2+ entry through voltage-gated slow (L-type) channels in the SA and AV nodes, lowering their automaticity
- Much weaker effect on contractile myocardium, though it does cut Ca2+ entry during plateau phase 2
- Isomers
  - L-isomer: Ca2+ channel block, with particular affinity for SA and AV node channels
  - D-isomer: blocks fast Na+ channels, giving some local anaesthetic action

## Effects

- Cardiovascular
  - Slows conduction at the SA and AV nodes, so heart rate falls
  - Some negative inotropy and peripheral vasodilation (lesser effects)
  - Mild coronary vasodilation
  - Blood pressure falls
- CNS: cerebral vasodilation
- With long-term use, may potentiate both depolarising and non-depolarising muscle relaxants

## Adverse effects

- WPW: treating SVT with verapamil can increase accessory pathway conduction and trigger VT or VF
- Poor LV function: may precipitate heart failure
- Heart block of varying degree
- With other AV-slowing drugs (digoxin, beta-blockers, halothane): severe bradycardia and AV block
- Raises digoxin levels
- Grapefruit juice raises verapamil levels; avoid it
- Hypotension from vascular smooth muscle relaxation, despite relative cardiac selectivity

## Contraindications

- AF complicating WPW syndrome

## Indications

- SVT, AF or atrial flutter: slows the rate or restores sinus rhythm
- Angina prophylaxis
- Hypertension, though negative inotropy limits its value here

## Pharmacokinetics

- Oral and IV use
- Almost 90% absorbed from the gut, but high first-pass metabolism leaves oral bioavailability of about 20% to 25%
- About 90% protein bound
- Volume of distribution 3–5 l/kg
- Hepatic metabolism to at least 12 inactive metabolites, excreted in urine; up to 20% excreted in bile
  - Demethylation gives norverapamil, which keeps significant antiarrhythmic activity
- Elimination half-life 3–7 hours; longer at higher doses as liver enzymes saturate
