# Amiodarone

Benzofuran antiarrhythmic, mainly class III but with class I, II and IV actions; many long-term side effects.

## Presentation

- IV: 150mg/3ml

## Administration

- Dilute in 5% dextrose for infusion
- Irritant: give via a central vein

## Dosage

- Wide complex tachycardia (ACLS)
  - IV 150mg over 10mins, may repeat once
  - Then IV 300mg every 8 hours (Q8H)
- Alternative: 150-300 mg load, then infusion at 1 mg/min
- Alternative: 5 mg/kg over 1 hour, then 15 mg/kg over 24 hours
- Oral: 200 mg TDS for 1 week, then 200 mg bd for 1 week, then 200 mg od

## Mechanism of action

- Classed as class III, but also has class I, II and IV activity
- K+ channel block slows repolarisation, so action potential duration and refractory period lengthen

## Adverse effects

- Most patients get side effects if treated long enough; most reverse on stopping
- Cardiac
  - Large, fast doses: bradycardia and hypotension
  - Hypotension is the commonest (15%): vasodilation from the drug plus its solvent (polysorbate 80)
  - Sinus bradycardia (5%) is expected on long-term use; marked bradycardia or AV block suggests toxicity
  - QT prolongation, but low proarrhythmic potential overall
  - Monitoring: baseline ECG, then annually
- Pulmonary: pneumonitis, fibrosis or pleuritis
  - Incidence 10% at 3 years, with 10% mortality; reversible if stopped early enough
  - High FiO2 may be a risk factor for acute lung toxicity in critically ill patients
  - Monitoring: baseline CXR, then annually
- Thyroid: hyperthyroidism (0.9%) and hypothyroidism (6%), usually reversible
  - Blocks peripheral conversion of T4 to T3
  - Monitoring: baseline TFT, at 3 months, then annually
- Liver: raised enzymes (3%), hepatitis, cirrhosis, jaundice
  - Monitoring: baseline LFT, then annually
- Eyes: corneal microdeposits are common but minor (haloes, mild blur) and reversible
  - Monitoring: yearly eye check on long-term treatment
- Gut: metallic taste while loading; occasional mild upset
- Neurological: peripheral neuropathy; myopathy rarely
- Skin: photosensitivity that can last months after stopping; slate-grey skin, mainly on the face
- Interactions
  - Increases effects of other highly protein-bound drugs (phenytoin, warfarin, digoxin): adjust doses
  - Digoxin level may rise from protein-binding displacement, with toxicity
  - Care with other AV-slowing drugs (beta-blockers, verapamil)
  - Avoid other QT-prolonging drugs (phenothiazines, TCAs, thiazides): risk of torsades de pointes

## Indications

- SVT, VT and WPW syndrome

## Pharmacokinetics

- Poor gut absorption: oral bioavailability 50% to 70%
- Over 95% protein bound (>95%)
- Volume of distribution 2–70 l/kg; accumulates heavily in muscle and fat
- Elimination half-life 20 to 100 days
- Hepatic metabolism to desmethylamiodarone, which has some antiarrhythmic activity
- Excreted via lacrimal glands, skin and biliary tract
