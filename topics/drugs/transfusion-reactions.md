# Transfusion Complications and Reactions

About 3% of transfused patients react, through immune, non-immune or infective mechanisms.

## Overview
- Overall risk: roughly 1:100 (minor), 1:10000 (severe), 1:100,000-million for the rarest
- Non-immune (think massive transfusion)
  - Transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO)
  - Massive transfusion: the "lethal diamond" (hypothermia, dilutional coagulopathy, acidosis, citrate toxicity with hypocalcaemia) plus hyperkalaemia
  - Late: iron overload with chronic transfusion
- Immune (usually recipient sensitised to donor cells)
  - Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI)
  - Acute haemolytic transfusion reaction (AHTR)
  - Febrile non-haemolytic transfusion reaction (FNHTR)
  - Allergic non-haemolytic reaction: urticarial or anaphylactic
  - Late: delayed haemolytic reaction, transfusion-associated graft versus host disease (TA-GVHD), immune sensitisation (Rhesus D antigen)
- Infective
  - Bacterial sepsis
  - Late: viral and other infections

## Recognition
- Fever or chills: AHTR, FNHTR, bacterial contamination
- Prominent respiratory symptoms: TRALI or TACO
  - Fever favours TRALI
  - Fluid overload, known heart failure or end-stage renal failure favour TACO
  - Investigate with CXR, NT-proBNP, ABG
- Urticaria: urticarial reaction, anaphylaxis
- Hypotension: AHTR, bacterial contamination, anaphylaxis

## Initial management
- Stop the transfusion at once; replace the whole giving set
- ABC and vital signs (fever, hypotension, desaturation)
  - Look for dyspnoea, pruritus, signs of haemolysis (flank pain, bleeding, haematuria, jaundice)
  - Support circulation with fluids and vasopressors
  - Fever with hypotension is hard to tell from sepsis: consider broad-spectrum antibiotics
  - Anaphylaxis: adrenaline
- Immediate clerical check: right product, right patient
  - A "correct" match does not exclude haemolysis (the group and crossmatch sample may have been taken from the wrong patient)
- Investigations
  - FBC and peripheral blood film
  - Haemolysis screen: LFT (bilirubin, LDH), haptoglobin, direct Coombs
  - Urea, electrolytes, creatinine; DIC screen (D-dimer, fibrinogen, PT, PTT)
  - Urine dipstick and microscopy for haemoglobinuria
  - Repeat group and crossmatch
  - Blood cultures
- Blood bank
  - Notify; return all units and the giving set (never transfuse the unused units)
  - Complete a transfusion reaction report
- Support complications: renal protection, DIC, hyperkalaemia

## Mild reactions
- Definition: fever only (rise <1C) or urticaria, haemodynamically stable, no haemolysis or haematuria, product correctly matched
- Treat symptoms
  - Febrile non-haemolytic: paracetamol
  - Urticarial: chlorphenamine (Piriton) 4mg and hydrocortisone 100mg
- Restart at a slower rate; observe closely and review soon
  - Fever persisting or symptoms worsening: manage as a serious reaction
  - Symptoms settle: continue; inform blood bank, no lab tests needed
- Future prevention: leucoreduced blood for febrile reactions; premedicate for both

## Massive transfusion: non-immune complications
- Besides the usual transfusion risks, stored blood carries storage lesions
- Main problems: lethal diamond (hypothermia, coagulopathy, acidosis, hypocalcaemia) plus hyperkalaemia

### Dilutional coagulopathy
- Can develop even with normal clotting beforehand, by diluting clotting proteins and platelets
  - Packed red cells contain no clotting factors, fibrinogen, cryoprecipitate or platelets
  - Platelets in stored blood are few and dysfunctional, so dilutional thrombocytopenia follows
  - Stored blood is low in factors V, VIII and XI; coagulopathy is likely once blood volume is replaced more than twice in 24 hours
- Each 10 to 12 units of red cells: platelet count falls by about 50 percent
- Each unit of FFP: expected rise in clotting proteins 10 percent in an adult, but real increments about 2.5 percent (losses in preparation, storage and to the interstitium)
- Each 500 mL of blood loss replaced: clotting proteins fall by about 10 percent

### Transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO)
- Cause: poor cardiac function and/or transfusion that is too fast
- Risk 1 in 700, and rising
- Within 6hrs of transfusion
- Presentation: dyspnoea; hypotension, tachycardia; basal crackles, raised venous pressure
- Management: slow the rate, diuretics, oxygen
- Anticipate it

### Hypothermia
- Rapid infusion of cold blood
- Harms: ventricular arrhythmias (VF at 28°C) and cardiac arrest, less oxygen delivery (Bohr effect), worse citrate toxicity
- Massive transfusion is an absolute indication to warm all blood products and IV fluids

### Citrate toxicity and hypocalcaemia
- Risk when rate exceeds 1 L per 10 minutes, or with exchange transfusion within 2 hours
- Also in liver disease, where citrate is cleared poorly
- Citrate binds Ca2+, causing hypocalcaemia
- Treatment: IV calcium gluconate 10 mL of 10% per 2 units of blood if clinical or ECG signs of hypocalcaemia

### Acidosis
- Stored blood reaches pH 6.5–6.8 after 2 weeks, so massive transfusion can worsen existing acidosis

### Hyperkalaemia
- Potassium leaks from stored red cells; plasma K+ can reach 30 mmol/L after 30 days of storage
- After transfusion it usually moves back into the cells; a problem mainly if already hyperkalaemic, or persistently acidotic and hypotensive
- Risk increases with storage time and irradiation; lower with fresh blood
- Occurs in 5% of massively transfused patients
- Treat as any hyperkalaemia

### Other storage effects
- 2,3-DPG depletion
  - Falls during storage, reducing oxygen offloading
  - Regenerated within 24 hours of transfusion
  - CPD-adenine preservative slows the fall
- Microaggregates
  - Clumps of fibrin, platelets and leucocytes over 20 μm, mainly driven by platelet fibrinogen; peak at 1–2 weeks of storage
  - Lodge in pulmonary vessels and release lysosomal contents, contributing to ARDS

## Iron overload
- From repeated transfusion over a long time (e.g. β-thalassaemia major)
- Can cause secondary haemochromatosis
- Management: iron chelation; phlebotomy if no longer transfusion-dependent and not anaemic

## Immune complications

### TRALI
- New acute lung injury during or within 6 h of the end of transfusion
- Currently the leading cause of transfusion-related death
- Thought to be donor anti-HLA or anti-HNA antibodies activating recipient neutrophils, raising pulmonary capillary permeability; non-antibody forms also exist
- Any plasma-containing product can cause it (rarely IVIg and cryoprecipitate); historically most often products with >60 mL of plasma (plasma, platelets)
- Risk per unit 1:10,000
- Presentation
  - Acute respiratory failure with severe hypoxaemia (PaO2/FiO2 <300 mmHg) within 6hrs
  - Typically 2-4 h after transfusion; resolves in 24-72 h
  - Bilateral pulmonary oedema on CXR
  - Non-cardiogenic: wedge pressure <18 mmHg, no left atrial hypertension
- Management: as for ARDS but resolves in days
  - Supportive, oxygen
  - Inform blood bank; patient and donor testing follow

### Acute haemolytic transfusion reaction (AHTR)
- ABO incompatibility: complement-mediated intravascular haemolysis, immediately after transfusion
  - Usually a clerical (identification) error
  - Worst: group A given to group O
- Risk per unit <1 in 10,000
- Presentation: fever with or without chills (commonest); dizziness, tachycardia, hypotension; dyspnoea, chest pain; back or flank pain, pain at the infusion site, haemoglobinuria, jaundice
- Complications: acute renal failure; DIC with uncontrolled bleeding at the infusion site; often severe even after small volumes
- Treatment: stop transfusion, check for clerical error, notify blood bank, support BP with generous IV fluids with or without inotropes, maintain urine output with fluids and diuretics

### Febrile non-haemolytic transfusion reaction (FNHTR)
- Recipient antibodies to donor white cells and platelets
- Risk per unit 1 in 100 (minor), 1 in 10,000 (severe)
- Fever with or without chills, usually during transfusion but up to 1-2 hours after; no haemolysis
- Treatment: exclude haemolysis and infection; stop, or continue more slowly with antipyretics (paracetamol, ibuprofen, diphenhydramine)
- Prevention: leucodepleted blood or a leucocyte filter

### Allergic non-haemolytic reactions
- IgE antibodies to donor plasma proteins trigger mast cell histamine release; commoner with plasma-rich products
- Urticarial reaction
  - Risk per unit 1 in 100
  - Urticaria, pruritus, occasionally fever
  - Treatment: IV diphenhydramine 25-50mg or oral chlorphenamine 4mg; IV hydrocortisone 100mg if severe
  - If urticaria settles with no breathlessness, hypotension or anaphylaxis, resume more slowly
  - Next time: consider antihistamine premedication; if recurrent despite this, consider washed red cells (discuss with haematology)
- Anaphylactic reaction
  - Rare; typically IgA-deficient patients with anti-IgA antibodies
  - Bronchospasm, laryngeal oedema, hypotension
  - Treatment: stop transfusion, IM adrenaline, IV fluids, IV diphenhydramine and steroids, with or without bronchodilators

### Delayed haemolytic transfusion reaction (DHTR)
- Antibodies to minor antigens (Rh, Kell, Duffy, Kidd, MNS): titre too low at transfusion, then rises on re-exposure and causes extravascular haemolysis
- Risk 1:10000
- A few days to a month after transfusion
- Commonest sign: falling haematocrit; also fever and leucocytosis (mimics hospital infection), mildly raised bilirubin, haemoglobinuria
- No specific treatment; record it for future transfusions

### Transfusion-associated graft versus host disease (TA-GVHD)
- Donor T lymphocytes attack the recipient
- Mostly severely immunocompromised patients (e.g. Hodgkin lymphoma, leukaemia); can occur in immunocompetent patients receiving directed donation from a relative
- 4-30 d after transfusion
- Fever, diarrhoea, abnormal LFTs, pancytopenia
- Prevented by irradiated products (leucocyte filtering alone is not enough)

## Infective complications

### Bacterial sepsis
- Gram positive: S. aureus, S. epidermidis, Bacillus cereus
- Gram negative: Klebsiella, Serratia, Pseudomonas, Yersinia
- Risk 1 in 100,000 for red cells, 1 in 10,000 for platelets
- Prevention
  - 4-hour rule: finish within 4hrs of leaving the blood bank fridge
  - 30-minute rule: units can only go back to the blood bank within 30mins of issue
- High spiking fever, chills and rigors soon after transfusion; hypotension
- Management: stop transfusion, ABC, exclude haemolysis, blood cultures, empirical broad-spectrum IV antibiotics as for sepsis without a clear source, IV fluids

### Transfusion-transmitted infections
- Any virus, parasite or other pathogen carried in donated blood
- Risk: HBV <1 in 153,000; HTLV <1 in 4,300,000; HCV <1 in 2,300,000; HIV <1 in 7,000,000
- Others: EBV, CMV, West Nile virus
- Donations are screened for HIV, hepatitis B and C

## Flashcards
- Q: How much calcium gluconate for citrate toxicity in massive transfusion?
  - A: 10 mL of 10% calcium gluconate IV per 2 units of blood, if clinical or ECG signs of hypocalcaemia
- Q: Timing and oxygenation criterion for TRALI?
  - A: Within 6 h of transfusion (typically 2-4 h), PaO2/FiO2 <300 mmHg, non-cardiogenic bilateral oedema; resolves in 24-72 h
- Q: What prevents TA-GVHD?
  - A: Irradiated blood products; leucocyte filtering alone is not enough
- Q: What is the 4-hour rule?
  - A: Complete transfusion within 4 hours of the unit leaving the blood bank fridge
- Q: How much does each 10 to 12 units of red cells lower the platelet count?
  - A: By about 50 percent
