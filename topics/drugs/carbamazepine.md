# Carbamazepine

Anticonvulsant used for focal seizures, bipolar disorder and trigeminal neuralgia; strong hepatic enzyme inducer.

## Administration

- Oral or rectal
- Before starting: test for HLA-B\*1502 (associated with Stevens-Johnson syndrome)
- Follow up LFTs and FBC during treatment

## Dosage

- As analgesic adjunct: PO 10 mg/kg/day, divided OM or BD

## Mechanism of action

- Acts like phenytoin

## Adverse effects

- CNS: mild neurotoxicity is common and often limits use
  - Headache, diplopia, ataxia, vomiting, drowsiness
- Liver: drug-induced hepatitis
- Kidney: antidiuretic effect, leading to water retention
- Blood: agranulocytosis (rare)
- Stevens-Johnson syndrome (HLA-B\*1502 carriers)
- Teratogenic: facial abnormalities, intrauterine growth restriction, microcephaly, learning disability
  - Risk rises with dose; overall about 1/300–2000 live births
  - Still regarded as one of the safer AEDs in pregnancy: cardiac and facial defect risk 2-5%
  - Safe in breastfeeding
- Interactions (enzyme induction, similar to phenytoin)
  - Phenytoin levels may rise or fall
  - Erythromycin increases carbamazepine levels

## Indications

- Epilepsy: mainly focal seizures
- Psychiatry: bipolar disorder
- Pain: trigeminal neuralgia; analgesic adjunct

## Pharmacokinetics

- Well absorbed orally, high bioavailability
- About 75% protein-bound
- Extensive hepatic metabolism to carbamazepine 10,11-epoxide
  - The epoxide keeps about 30% of the anticonvulsant activity
- Powerful hepatic enzyme inducer, including of its own metabolism (autoinduction)
- Excreted almost entirely in urine as unconjugated metabolites
