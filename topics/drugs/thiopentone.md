# Thiopentone

Sodium thiopental: a thiobarbiturate IV induction agent with fast onset, cerebroprotective and anticonvulsant effects.

## Presentation
- Thiobarbiturate: the sulfur analogue of the oxybarbiturate pentobarbital; formulated as the sodium salt
- Pale yellow powder, 500 mg vial
- Vial contains sodium carbonate (Na2CO3, 6% by weight) and nitrogen instead of air, both to aid solubility
  - Sodium carbonate in water gives a strongly alkaline solution (pH 10.5), which favours the water-soluble enol form
  - Air holds a little CO2; in water this would release bicarbonate and hydrogen ions, lowering pH and shifting more drug to the poorly soluble keto form, so nitrogen replaces it
- 2.5% solution is stable for many days and should be bacteriostatic because of its alkaline pH

## Administration
- Reconstitute 500 mg in 20 ml sterile water to give 25 mg/ml (2.5%)
- Reconstitute in sterile water to avoid precipitation
  - NaCl is acidic (though some texts allow dilution in NaCl)
  - Avoid Hartmann's: its lactate buffer holds the solution at a more acidic pH, so thiopentone precipitates
- Very alkaline: may precipitate when mixed with other IV fluids or drugs
- Now given by continuous infusion only for refractory status epilepticus
- Has been given rectally, but onset is slow by that route

## Dosage
- Induction: 3-5 mg/kg (range 3–7 mg/kg IV), e.g. about 200 mg
- Onset: 30-45 sec
- Duration of action: 5-10 mins (short, but longer than propofol)
  - Repeat doses can delay emergence (high protein binding, low hepatic extraction ratio)

## Mechanism of action
- Like other barbiturates, enhances GABA transmission

## Effects
- Cardiovascular: dose-dependent depression, less than propofol
  - Reduces contractility
  - Some fall in SVR (less than propofol)
  - Marked venodilation
  - May cause a compensatory tachycardia (unlike propofol)
  - Net result: CO falls, MAP falls (less than with propofol)
- Respiratory: dose-dependent depression
  - Lower tidal volume and rate; apnoea
  - Laryngeal reflexes partly preserved at usual doses
  - May cause some laryngospasm and bronchospasm
- CNS
  - Reduces cerebral blood flow and blood volume, so ICP falls; reduces CMRO2
  - Cerebroprotective; used in many neurosurgical cases
  - Potent anticonvulsant
  - Antanalgesic at very low doses: may lower the pain threshold
  - Longer cognitive impairment than propofol

## Adverse effects
- Histamine release
  - Severe anaphylactic reactions in about 1 in 20 000 administrations
- Intra-arterial injection or extravasation: tissue necrosis or gangrene
  - Injecting 2.5% solution at pH 10.5 into arterial blood at pH 7.4 shifts the enol-keto balance towards the keto form, which is less soluble
  - Crystals form and lodge in small arteries and arterioles, causing intense vasoconstriction, thrombosis, ischaemia, pain and even gangrene
  - Does not precipitate in veins because venous blood keeps diluting it
  - Treat at once: consider intra-arterial papaverine or procaine, analgesia, sympathetic block of the limb, anticoagulation
  - Perivascular injection is painful; large extravasation can cause serious necrosis

## Contraindications
- Absolute: inducible porphyrias
  - Induces ALA synthetase (rate-limiting step in porphyrin synthesis), so may trigger an acute porphyric crisis

## Indications
- Induction of anaesthesia; drug of choice for emergency caesarean section and ECT
- Refractory status epilepticus
  - Infusion to a level that gives an isoelectric EEG, i.e. maximal fall in cerebral oxygen demand
  - Inotropes may be needed to keep cerebral perfusion at these doses

## Pharmacokinetics
- 80% bound to plasma proteins
- pKa 7.6, so 60% of free drug is unionised at pH 7.4
  - With only 20% unbound, just 12% is immediately available as unbound, unionised drug
  - Critically ill patients (acidosis, lower protein binding) need much less to induce anaesthesia
  - NSAIDs can displace it from binding sites and increase the free fraction
- Fast onset: high lipid solubility, crosses the blood-brain barrier rapidly
- Large volume of distribution (non-polar at physiological pH, very lipid soluble)
- Rapid waking after a single bolus is due to redistribution, not metabolism
  - Tri-exponential decline: well-perfused organs (brain, liver) first, then muscle and skin
- Slow hepatic metabolism to inactive metabolites (pentobarbital is also a metabolite)
  - Low clearance; with infusion, metabolism may become zero order as hepatic enzymes saturate
  - Induces hepatic cytochrome P450 after a single dose
- Only 0.5-2% excreted unchanged in urine
