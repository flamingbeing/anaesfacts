# Sodium bicarbonate

Alkalinising agent for selected metabolic acidoses, sodium channel blocker toxicity and hyperkalaemia; it generates CO2, so ventilation must keep up.

## Presentation

- **8.4%**: **1 mmol HCO3- per mL**, **very hypertonic (2,000 mOsm/kg)**
- **Isotonic bicarbonate**: 150 mEq (eg three 50-mEq ampoules) in 1 litre of **5% dextrose (D5W)**, giving a **1.3%** (or 1.26%) **solution**, about 300 mOsm
  - Alternative mix: 75 ml of 8.4% in 500 ml D5
  - Hypertonic in the bag, isotonic once the dextrose is metabolised
  - Large volumes needed to raise bicarbonate; lowers potassium
- Bicarbonate can only be given as the sodium salt

## Dosage

- Cardiac arrest: **1-2 mmol/kg** IV bolus
- Correction of metabolic acidosis (formulas vary)
  - HCO3 (mEq) needed = 0.2 x weight (kg) x base deficit
  - Half correction (mmol) = 0.3 x base deficit (mmol/L) x weight (kg)
  - Full correction (mmol) = 0.6 x base deficit (mmol/L) x weight (kg)
  - HCO3 (mEq) needed = 0.5 x weight (kg) x (24 - serum HCO3 in mEq/L)
- **Ventilation must be adequate** to clear the CO2 produced

## Mechanism of action

- Buffers hydrogen ions, aiming to **reverse the harmful cardiovascular effects of acidaemia**

## Adverse effects

- Fluid and sodium
  - **Volume overload**
  - **Hypernatraemia** (1 mmol Na+ per 1 mmol HCO3-)
  - Hyperosmolality, causing arterial vasodilation and hypotension
- Electrolytes
  - **Rebound ("overshoot") alkalosis**
  - **Hypokalaemia**
  - **Low ionised calcium**
- **Hypercapnia** unless ventilation is briefly increased; CO2 enters cells easily and worsens intracellular acidosis
- **Impaired oxygen unloading** (left-shifted oxyhaemoglobin curve): less tissue oxygen delivery and **more lactate**
- **Severe tissue necrosis on extravasation**

## Contraindications

- **Not useful in high anion gap acidosis** (lactic or ketoacidosis): the organic acids are metabolised and regenerate bicarbonate
- Correct hypoxia first: with tissue hypoxia it may worsen things, by releasing acidotic inhibition of glycolysis and by the left shift of the curve
- In general, **treat the cause** and support the patient rather than give bicarbonate

## Indications

- Accepted
  - **Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis from bicarbonate loss** (renal tubular acidosis, fistula losses)
  - **Sodium channel blocker overdose (eg tricyclics)**
  - **Urinary alkalinisation (salicylate poisoning)**
  - **Hyperkalaemia**
- Controversial
  - **Cardiac arrest**: prolonged resuscitation with documented severe metabolic acidosis
  - **Diabetic ketoacidosis**: very rarely, perhaps if shocked with **pH below 6.8**
  - Severe pulmonary hypertension with right ventricular failure, to support the RV
  - Severe ischaemic heart disease where lactic acidosis is thought to be arrhythmogenic

## Flashcards

- Q: Bicarbonate content and osmolality of 8.4% sodium bicarbonate?
  - A: 1 mmol/mL; about 2,000 mOsm/kg
- Q: Dose of bicarbonate in cardiac arrest?
  - A: 1-2 mmol/kg IV bolus (controversial; prolonged arrest with severe metabolic acidosis)
- Q: How is isotonic (1.26%) bicarbonate made?
  - A: 150 mEq sodium bicarbonate in 1 litre of D5W
- Q: Accepted indications for sodium bicarbonate?
  - A: Normal anion gap acidosis from bicarbonate loss, sodium channel blocker overdose, urinary alkalinisation for salicylates, hyperkalaemia
- Q: Why must ventilation be increased when giving bicarbonate?
  - A: It generates CO2, which causes hypercapnia and worsens intracellular acidosis
