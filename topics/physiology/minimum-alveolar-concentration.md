# Minimum alveolar concentration (MAC)

MAC is the standard measure of inhalational agent potency; potency follows lipid solubility.

## Potency

- Potency relates to lipid solubility (Meyer-Overton correlation)
- Lipid solubility is given by the oil:gas (O:G) partition coefficient

## Definition

- Minimum alveolar concentration at equilibrium, at 1 atmosphere, in oxygen, that stops purposeful movement to a standard surgical stimulus in 50% of subjects
- Alveolar concentration is estimated from end-tidal concentration
- A special case of an ED50; also called EC50 for immobility, MAC-movement, minimum alveolar partial pressure (for 50% immobility), median alveolar concentration (for immobility)
- In humans, the stimulus is a skin incision on the forearm; animals use other standards

## How it is measured

- Volunteer induced with the agent, held at a set end-tidal anaesthetic concentration (ETAC) for 15 min so alveolar and arterial partial pressures equilibrate
- Standard noxious stimulus applied; watch for purposeful movement (head or limb)
- Concentration stepped up or down by response
- Plot proportion not moving against concentration to give a dose-response curve; MAC is the point where 50% do not move

## Uses

- Normalises potency, so agents can be compared
- Additive: 0.5 MAC of one agent plus 0.4 MAC of another = 0.9 MAC; helps estimate depth with two agents (usually a volatile plus nitrous oxide)
- Multiples describe depth, but are not linear, as dose-response curves of different agents are not parallel

## MAC variants

- MAC-awake: stops eye opening to verbal command in 50%; about 0.3–0.5 MAC
  - Compares sedative effect between agents
  - Ratio to MAC varies across volatiles, but is similar for isoflurane, sevoflurane and desflurane
  - Usually goes with loss of recall
  - Unresponsive does not always mean unconscious; studies used volunteers without noxious stimuli
- 1.3 MAC: stops movement to incision in 95% (the inhalational equivalent of an ED95)
- MAC for tracheal intubation: stops movement and coughing at intubation; about 1.3 MAC
- MAC-BAR: abolishes the sympathetic response (heart rate, blood pressure or plasma noradrenaline) to surgical stimulus in 50%; about 1.5 MAC (1.7-2.0 MAC in some sources)
- MAC-amnesia: suppresses recall of a noxious stimulus in 50%
  - Less studied but clinically important
  - Lower than MAC-awake (perhaps 0.25 MAC), with more variation between patients
- MAC-unawake: concentration at which consciousness is lost
  - Linked to "neural inertia": an intrinsic resistance to changing state (induction or emergence)
  - Going under and coming out use different neural pathways; the hysteresis is not just pharmacokinetic
  - So MAC-unawake is likely higher than MAC-awake
- MAC-age: MAC at a given age (e.g. MAC40)
- MAC-hr: MAC multiplied by exposure time
  - 1.5 MAC for 30 minutes = 0.75 MAC-hr
  - Used to compare exposure, study adverse effects and set dose limits

## MAC and age

- Full-term neonates need about 25% less than infants aged 1–6 months
- In preterm neonates, MAC falls as gestational age falls
- MAC rises to a peak at age 6 months, then falls by about 6% per decade (Mapleson, 1996), with a slight rise at puberty
- Sevoflurane is the exception: its MAC stays constant through neonates and infants

## Factors that increase MAC (less potent)

- Children, from infancy to adolescence (peak at 6 months)
- Hyperthermia
- Thyrotoxicosis
- Hypernatraemia
- Chronic alcoholism
- Central stimulants, e.g. cocaine or amphetamine intoxication
- Catecholamine reuptake inhibition (MAO inhibitors, TCAs, ephedrine)
- Red hair

## Factors that decrease MAC

- Old age: about 6% per decade (some monitors adjust for this)
- Premature infants
- Pregnancy
- Hypotension (MAP below 40 mmHg)
- Hypercarbia (PaCO2 above 95 mmHg)
- Hypoxia (PO2 below 38 mmHg)
- Anaemia (Hb below 5 g/dL)
- Hypothermia
- Hyponatraemia, lithium
- Acute alcohol intoxication
- Sedative or anaesthetic drugs
- A factor that changes MAC may not change MAC-awake, MAC-unawake, MAC-amnesia or MAC-BAR in the same way

## Limitations

- A population mean; individuals differ
- Needs alveolar gas, which clinical monitors only approximate

## Clinical implications

- SD of MAC is about 10%, so 1.2 MAC (MAC + 2 SD) should stop movement in about 95%
- The steep curve means little variation between people in the dose needed for immobility, which makes volatiles predictable
- Propofol shows wider percentage variation, so immobility is easier to predict with volatile than with TIVA
- But the steep slope also means small drops just below MAC can let many patients move
  - Can happen quickly with insoluble agents such as desflurane: vaporiser turned off and not restarted, vaporiser running empty unnoticed, or vaporiser turned off before reversal of neuromuscular block

## Atmospheric pressure

- Most inhalational anaesthesia is at about 1 atmosphere, so it is easy to forget MAC is tied to pressure and think concentration is what matters
- The real measure is partial pressure
- In kPa, concentration and partial pressure are almost the same, as atmospheric pressure is about 100 kPa
