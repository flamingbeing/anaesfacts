# Acetylcholine and cholinergic receptors

Acetylcholine (ACh) is the transmitter at the neuromuscular junction, autonomic ganglia and parasympathetic endings, acting on nicotinic and muscarinic receptors.

## Acetylcholine
- Neurotransmitter in the brain, autonomic nervous system and neuromuscular junction
- Structure: choline acetylated at the oxygen atom
- No therapeutic use: it acts on both nicotinic and muscarinic receptors and is broken down very fast

## Synthesis and release
- Made in the presynaptic neuron by choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) from choline and acetyl-CoA
  - Choline comes from the diet and from recycled ACh breakdown
- Moved from the axoplasm into small synaptic vesicles for storage
- Action potential at the presynaptic membrane leads to Ca2+ influx, which triggers vesicle release
  - About 200 vesicles per action potential, each holding about 10 000 ACh molecules
- ACh crosses the synaptic cleft and binds postsynaptic ACh receptors

## Breakdown
- Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) in the junctional clefts of the postsynaptic membrane splits ACh into inactive choline and acetate
  - Abundant in the cleft; fast clearance of ACh is essential for normal muscle function
- AChE has two binding sites
  - Anionic site: binds the positively charged quaternary ammonium group
  - Esteratic site: binds the ester group
- Choline is released and the enzyme is left acetylated; this is quickly hydrolysed, releasing acetic acid

## Cholinesterases
- A family of esterases that split choline esters
- Acetylcholinesterase (AChE)
  - Neural synapses, neuromuscular junctions, red cell membranes
  - Hydrolyses acetylcholine faster
- Butyrylcholinesterase (BChE; pseudocholinesterase, plasma cholinesterase, cholinesterase II)
  - Plasma enzyme made in the liver
  - Hydrolyses butyrylcholine faster
  - Metabolises suxamethonium, mivacurium and ester local anaesthetics, including cocaine
  - Absent or abnormal BCHE gives pseudocholinesterase deficiency
  - Butyrylcholine is synthetic (not found in the body); used in the lab to tell the two enzymes apart
- Cholinesterase inhibitors (anticholinesterases) stop breakdown of acetylcholine or butyrylcholine

## Receptor classes
- Two main classes: nicotinic and muscarinic
  - Named for higher sensitivity to nicotine and muscarine respectively
  - Many drugs act as selective agonists or antagonists at each
- Distribution
  - Nicotinic: neuromuscular junction, autonomic ganglia (sympathetic and parasympathetic)
  - Muscarinic: parasympathetic postganglionic nerve endings

## Nicotinic receptors (nAChR)
- Ligand-gated (ionotropic) ion channels, so the response is fast (e.g. skeletal muscle at the neuromuscular junction)
- At the neuromuscular junction
  - Sit on the edges of the junctional folds of the postsynaptic membrane
  - Two α subunits bind ACh; binding of the first raises the affinity of the second
  - Channel is non-specific (Na+, K+, Ca2+), producing miniature end-plate potentials
  - These add up to threshold, opening voltage-gated Na+ channels and firing a muscle action potential
  - Prejunctional receptors give positive feedback to keep transmitter release up during high activity
    - Their block by non-depolarising relaxants may explain fade
- Subtypes (similar but not identical)
  - Muscle type (NM or N1): neuromuscular junction
    - Agonist: suxamethonium
    - Antagonist: tubocurarine
  - Neuronal type (NN or N2): autonomic ganglia (sympathetic and parasympathetic), adrenal medulla
    - In ganglia, preganglionic fibres release ACh onto nicotinic receptors on postganglionic cell bodies
    - Antagonists: ganglion blockers (e.g. hexamethonium, trimethaphan)
- Cellular mechanism
  - Non-selective cation channel, mainly Na+ and K+; some subunit combinations also pass Ca2+
  - Cation flow depolarises the membrane
  - Gives an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) in neurons

## Muscarinic receptors (mAChR)
- G-protein coupled receptors; subtypes M1-M5
- M1 (Gq): autonomic ganglia, CNS
  - Modulates nicotinic transmission in ganglia; mediates the slow EPSP in the postganglionic nerve
  - Increases gastric acid and salivary secretion
- M2 (Gi): heart
  - Negative chronotropy: slower heart rate, slower AV node conduction
  - Also in the CNS
  - Prejunctional M2 receptors give negative feedback in central cholinergic pathways, limiting ACh release
- M3 (Gq): glands and smooth muscle
  - Gut: smooth muscle contraction, more motility, gastric acid secretion
  - Lungs: bronchoconstriction, more secretions
  - More lacrimal and salivary secretion
  - Eye (iris constrictor, ciliary muscle): miosis
  - Also in the CNS
- M4 (Gi): mainly CNS
- M5 (Gq): mainly CNS

### Cellular mechanisms
- M1, M3, M5: Gq, phospholipase C, IP3/DAG, Ca2+
  - Phospholipase C makes inositol triphosphate (IP3), which releases Ca2+ from sarcoplasmic reticulum stores
  - Higher Ca2+ activates myosin light chain kinase, which activates myosin ATPase and starts actin-myosin cross-bridging
  - Diacylglycerol (DAG) is also released and activates protein kinase C
- M2, M4: pertussis toxin-sensitive Gi and Go
  - Inhibit adenylate cyclase, so less cAMP
  - Open K+ channels and close Ca2+ channels
- cGMP may also be involved

## Drugs
- Muscarinic receptors are the main target of drugs acting directly on the parasympathetic system (e.g. atropine)
- Agonists and antagonists generally show little subtype selectivity
