# General Anaesthesia

Reversible drug-induced state of unconsciousness for surgery, built from hypnosis, analgesia, amnesia, immobility and reflex blunting.

## Definition
- Making a patient unconscious so that an operation or procedure can be done
- Implies loss of consciousness and of protective airway reflexes
- Alternative definition: a reversible state, induced by drugs or otherwise, with all of:
  - Loss of awareness
  - No conscious recall
  - No overt muscular response
  - Minimal autonomic response

## Goals
- Hypnosis: unconsciousness
- Analgesia: abolition of pain sensation
- Amnesia: no memory formation
- Immobility: no movement
- Reflex blunting: no exaggerated autonomic response

## Classic triad
- Hypnosis
- Analgesia
- Muscle relaxation

## Drugs used
- Can be induced and maintained with IV drugs or inhaled volatile agents
- Sedative-hypnotics (propofol, etomidate, barbiturates, benzodiazepines)
  - Give sedation, unconsciousness and amnesia
  - Generally poor at analgesia or preventing movement
  - Act on GABA-A receptor chloride channels: chloride influx hyperpolarises the neuron and prevents depolarisation
- Other IV agents: ketamine, opioids
- Volatile agents
  - Unlike sedative-hypnotics, give both unconsciousness and immobility, plus sedation and amnesia
  - Mechanism not fully understood; thought to act on brain and spinal cord
  - Immobility is thought to come from spinal cord action (not the neuromuscular junction, where neuromuscular blockers act)

## Intubation is not always needed
- GA does not require a tracheal tube or controlled ventilation
- Patients without reflux risk who keep adequate oxygenation and ventilation may breathe spontaneously while unconscious

## Volatile agents are not always needed
- GA can be induced and maintained with many drug combinations
- TIVA (total intravenous anaesthesia) uses IV drugs only and is increasingly popular
  - Avoids volatile side effects such as nausea and vomiting
  - Important option in malignant hyperthermia susceptibility, as volatiles are triggers
  - Common infusions: propofol, remifentanil, sufentanil, dexmedetomidine

## Flashcards
- Q: What is the classic triad of general anaesthesia?
  - A: Hypnosis, analgesia, muscle relaxation
- Q: Which receptor do sedative-hypnotics such as propofol act on?
  - A: GABA-A receptor chloride channel
- Q: Where are volatile agents thought to act to prevent movement?
  - A: Spinal cord
- Q: Name two advantages of TIVA over volatile anaesthesia.
  - A: Less nausea and vomiting; safe in malignant hyperthermia susceptibility
