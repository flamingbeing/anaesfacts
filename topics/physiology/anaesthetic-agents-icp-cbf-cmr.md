# Effect of Anaesthetic Agents on ICP, CBF and CMR

Most anaesthetics lower brain activity; IV agents keep flow and metabolism coupled, volatile agents uncouple them.

## General principle
- Almost all anaesthetic agents reduce brain activity
  - Exceptions: ketamine and nitrous oxide when given alone
  - So both are often avoided in intracranial surgery
- With most agents (e.g. propofol), lower brain activity goes with a fall in global cerebral metabolic rate (CMR)

## Volatile agents
- Potent inhaled agents (e.g. isoflurane) dilate the large intracerebral arteries
  - CBF and intracranial volume increase despite lower CMR
  - CMR and CBF become uncoupled: perfusion exceeds need
- Relatively contraindicated with raised ICP, or when brain swelling hampers surgical access

## Intravenous agents
- Propofol and thiopental reduce both CMR and CBF
  - Normal flow-metabolism coupling is preserved
- Infusions can help control raised ICP and improve exposure in a "tight", swollen brain

## Opioids and benzodiazepines
- Little effect on CMR or CBF
- Routinely used as part of a balanced anaesthetic

## Flashcards
- Q: Which agents do not reduce brain activity when used alone?
  - A: Ketamine and nitrous oxide.
- Q: How do volatile agents affect CBF and CMR?
  - A: CMR falls but cerebral vasodilation increases CBF and intracranial volume (uncoupling).
- Q: How do propofol and thiopental affect CBF and CMR?
  - A: Both fall; coupling is preserved.
- Q: When are volatile agents relatively contraindicated in neurosurgery?
  - A: Raised ICP, or a swollen brain limiting surgical access.
