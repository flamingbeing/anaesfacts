# Fluid terminology and chemistry

Basic physical chemistry behind body fluids and IV fluids: solutions, osmolality and tonicity, gas solubility, diffusion, osmosis and equivalents.

## Osmotic and oncotic pressure

- Osmotic pressure: from dissolved salts or small non-ionic solutes
- Oncotic pressure: the part of osmotic pressure exerted by plasma proteins; usually pulls water into the circulation

## Solutions

- Mixture: two or more substances combined
- Solution: a mixture that forms an even dispersion in a single phase
- Solvent: the component present in greater amount (it does the dissolving)
- Solute: the component present in lesser amount (it is dissolved)
- Aqueous solution: one where the solvent is water

## Osmolarity, osmolality and tonicity

- Osmolarity: solute per unit volume of solvent (osmoles/L)
  - Changes with temperature, as volume expands and contracts
  - Used by manufacturers on IV fluid and drug labels (e.g. normal saline, mannitol), as volume is easier to measure than weight in production
  - Not the same as tonicity: counts every solute, not only those that cannot cross the membrane
- Osmolality: solute per unit mass of solvent (osmoles/kg)
  - More accurate; independent of temperature
  - Clinically interchangeable with osmolarity, except in pseudohyponatraemia (a large part of plasma is lipid, not water)
- Tonicity: the osmotic pressure gradient between two solutions
  - Only solutes that cannot cross the membrane count, as only they create the gradient
  - So a solution can be iso-osmolar but not isotonic (e.g. 5% dextrose)
  - Solutes that do not add to tonicity are "ineffective osmoles": mainly dextrose and urea
  - In diabetes, dextrose can act as an effective osmole (e.g. DKA, HHS)
  - Sodium is the main effective osmole: sodium and its anions make up 86% of osmolality and 92% of tonicity

## Solubility

- Henry's law (1803):
  - Dissolved gas is proportional to the partial pressure of the gas in equilibrium with the liquid
  - Exact solubility depends on the particular gas or vapour and solvent
  - Solubility falls as temperature rises (molecules have more energy to leave solution); cold water holds more O2
- Bunsen solubility coefficient: volume of gas, corrected to STP, dissolved per unit volume of solvent
- Ostwald solubility coefficient: volume of gas dissolved per unit volume of solvent at ambient temperature and pressure
  - Anaesthetic tables quote it at body temperature, 37°C
  - Depends only on temperature, not pressure (although the number of molecules dissolved does depend on pressure)
- Partition coefficients: ratio of the amount of gas or vapour in one phase to another (oil/gas, blood/gas, oil/blood)
  - Oil/gas coefficient matters for anaesthetic action: links the gas-phase concentration (MAC), which sets agent activity, to the amount in lipid at the site of action
- Solubility in water at 40°C (ml/100 ml):
  - N2: 1.2
  - O2: 2.3
  - CO2: 53

## Diffusion

- Diffusion: net movement down a concentration gradient from random molecular motion
- Bulk flow: water and solutes moving together down a pressure gradient
- Fick's law: diffusion rate across unit area is proportional to the concentration gradient (for gases, the partial pressure gradient)
- Graham's law: gas diffusion rate is inversely proportional to the square root of molecular weight
  - Liquids flow through an orifice inversely to the square root of density instead
  - So water vapour passes a dry orifice faster than ether vapour, but liquid ether flows faster than water
- Membrane type affects diffusion:
  - N2 crosses dry porous membranes faster than N2O; across fluid barriers N2 is slower, as its low solubility limits the gradient
  - CO2 is larger than O2, so by Graham's law it would cross a dry porous membrane 20% more slowly
  - But O2 is poorly water soluble, so alveolar fluid holds little of it: CO2 elimination is almost always complete, while O2 uptake can suffer in adverse conditions
  - Diffusing capacity for CO2 is 20 times that of O2
- Apnoeic oxygenation: Hb binding of O2 keeps a plasma gradient that draws in more O2 from the alveoli than CO2 returned; the resulting negative pressure pulls gas in from upstream, giving some passive ventilation
- Anaesthetic agents and CO2 pass through plastic circuits fairly easily; N2 does not (poorly soluble in plastic)
- High FiO2 helps shrink pneumothorax or distended bowel: removing inspired N2 sets up a gradient for N2 to leave the cavity into blood

## Osmosis

- Net movement of solvent across a membrane permeable to solvent but not solute, towards the higher solute concentration, tending to equalise concentrations
- Osmotic force depends on the number of particles, not their size or charge
- A particle that cannot cross a barrier (while others can) draws fluid into its compartment
- Molarity: a 1 molar solution has 1 mole per litre of solution
  - 1 molar glucose (180 g/litre) has half the osmotic effect of 1 molar NaCl (58 g/litre), as NaCl splits into two particles
- 0.9% NaCl = 9 g/litre = 9/58 molar = 0.15 molar; same osmotic force as 5% dextrose (50 g/litre = 50/180, about 0.3 molar), i.e. about 300 mosmol/litre
- Blood osmolarity is 290
- Osmotic pressure depends on particle number, so concentrations are usually given as mmol/litre rather than meq/litre
- Iso-osmolar subcutaneous injections hurt less than ones with very different osmolarity
- Capillaries: proteins are few in number but stay in the vessel (except in disease), so they draw fluid back at the distal end, balancing hydrostatic outflow at the proximal end
- Brain: the blood-brain barrier keeps electrolytes out of interstitial fluid (except in disease, the hypothalamus and choroid plexus); being far more numerous than proteins, electrolytes are the main osmotic force for cerebral interstitial fluid movement

## Glomerular filtration pressures

- Resistance to flow in the renal tubules: about 35 mmHg
- Plasma protein oncotic pressure: a further 35 mmHg
- So glomerular hydrostatic pressure must exceed 70 mmHg for filtration

## Milliequivalents (mEq)

- Equivalent: the amount of a substance that reacts with a set amount of another in a given reaction
- Univalent ions: 1 mmol = 1 meq (e.g. Na+)
- Divalent ions: 1 mmol = 2 meq (e.g. Ca++)

## Flashcards

- Q: Why is 5% dextrose iso-osmolar but not isotonic?
  - A: Dextrose is an ineffective osmole: it crosses membranes
- Q: What share of osmolality and tonicity comes from sodium and its anions?
  - A: 86% of osmolality and 92% of tonicity
- Q: What does Graham law state?
  - A: Gas diffusion rate is inversely proportional to the square root of molecular weight
- Q: How many meq is 1 mmol of Ca++?
  - A: 2 meq
- Q: What is the osmolarity of 0.9% NaCl?
  - A: About 300 mosmol/litre (0.15 molar, two particles)
