# Blood Groups and Blood Components

How donated blood is split into components, and the ABO, Rh and other blood group systems that decide compatibility.

## Blood components
- Available: red cells, platelets, coagulation products (frozen plasma, cryoprecipitate, factor concentrates)
- One donated unit (1 U) is 450-500 mL of whole blood, then fractionated
  - Centrifugation gives red cells and platelet-rich plasma
  - Platelet-rich plasma is split into platelets and plasma; several donations are pooled for a therapeutic dose
  - Frozen plasma (FP, formerly FFP): frozen within 24 h of collection
  - Cryoprecipitate: high molecular weight precipitate formed when FP is thawed at low temperature

## ABO groups at a glance
- O: no A or B antigen on red cells; anti-A and anti-B in serum; universal red cell donor
- A: A antigen; anti-B
- B: B antigen; anti-A
- AB: A and B antigens; no anti-A or anti-B; universal plasma donor

## Definitions
- Blood group: classification by inherited antigens on the red cell surface (proteins, carbohydrates, glycoproteins or glycolipids); some also appear on other tissues
- Blood group system: one or more antigens controlled by one gene or a cluster of closely linked genes; the International Society of Blood Transfusion recognises 35 systems and 342 red cell antigens
- Blood group antigen: a red cell surface sugar or protein defined serologically by an antibody; antigens differing by a single nucleotide get superscripts (e.g. Fya and Fyb)
- Phenotype: the set of antigens found by serological testing; genotype: the gene sequence, or antigens predicted by DNA testing
- Null phenotype: no antigens of a given system, often from an inactivating mutation
- ABO and Rh are the two key systems and together give the blood type (e.g. A positive)
- Inherited from both parents; group frequencies vary widely between populations

## ABO system
- Two antigens (A and B) on red cells, two antibodies (anti-A and anti-B) in serum
- 4 groups: A, B, AB, O (neither antigen)
- The most important system for transfusion; anti-A and anti-B are usually IgM
- Landsteiner's 1901 scheme used A/B/C; C later became O, often written 0 (zero) in other languages
- Bombay phenotype
  - No fucosyltransferase, so no H antigen; without H, A and B cannot be made whatever the genotype
  - Red cells lack A, B and H; the patient makes anti-A, anti-B and anti-H
  - Any non-Bombay red cells risk a severe haemolytic reaction
  - Crossmatch shows haemolysis with all group O screening and panel cells, prompting further tests
  - Incidence about 1/10,000 in people from India; about 1/1,000,000 in people of European descent

## Rh system
- Second most important for transfusion; currently 50 antigens
- D is the key antigen: the most immunogenic of the five main Rh antigens
- D-negative people usually have no anti-D, as environmental exposure does not sensitise
- Sensitisation follows fetomaternal haemorrhage in pregnancy or, occasionally, transfusion of D-positive cells, and can lead to Rh disease
- Rh negative: about 0.3% in Asian populations, 15% in European populations
- Written as + or − after the ABO group (A− = group A without D)

## Other systems
- Common and clinically significant: Lewis, P1PK, GLOB, I, MNS, Kell, Duffy, Kidd
- Clinically significant but rare: Lutheran, Vel, Chido-Rodgers, Gerbich, Colton, Diego
- Little or no clinical significance: Cartwright (Yt), Knops

## Compatibility
- Red cells
  - AB+ is the universal recipient
  - O− is the universal donor but can receive only O− blood
- Plasma (contains ABO antibodies): the reverse of red cell compatibility
  - AB plasma has no anti-A or anti-B, so suits any recipient; AB patients can receive only AB plasma
  - Group O patients can receive plasma of any group; O plasma suits only group O recipients
- Platelets
  - Carry ABO antigens (adsorbed from plasma) but not Rh; rarely, very high ABO expression
  - Some services limit the ABO-incompatible plasma given with platelets, or avoid high-titre anti-A or anti-B platelets for group A and B recipients
  - RhD-negative women of childbearing age get RhD-negative platelets, to avoid the few contaminating D-positive red cells

## Other clinical relevance
- Haematopoietic stem cell transplant: any donor group to any recipient; the graft makes a new blood type and matching immune system
- Solid organ transplant
  - Antibodies to graft blood group antigens can cause rejection; organs are usually ABO identical; non-ABO antigens are not matched
  - Non-identical but compatible grafts are sometimes used (e.g. group O organ to A, B or AB recipient)
  - ABO-incompatible grafts in selected cases (living kidney donor, fulminant liver failure) with desensitisation or immunosuppression
  - Passenger lymphocytes from the graft can cause transient haemolysis
  - Cornea, bone and tendon need no ABO matching
- Haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn: maternal antibodies cross the placenta and cause haemolytic anaemia in the fetus or newborn (they persist for several weeks)

## Links with disease
- Some red cell antigens and antibodies are linked to autoimmune haemolytic anaemia
- Duffy-negative people resist some malaria species
- Group O: about 20 to 30 percent lower von Willebrand factor than A, B or AB (faster clearance)
- Kell antigen is sometimes associated with McLeod syndrome
- Gastric cancer commoner in group A; gastric and duodenal ulcers in group O; pancreatic cancer in non-O groups

## Flashcards
- Q: Which group is the universal red cell donor and which the universal plasma donor?
  - A: O negative for red cells; AB for plasma
- Q: What is the Bombay phenotype and why does it matter?
  - A: No H antigen (no fucosyltransferase), so no A, B or H; makes anti-A, anti-B and anti-H and haemolyses any non-Bombay red cells
- Q: How common is Rh negative blood in Asian and European populations?
  - A: About 0.3% in Asian and 15% in European populations
- Q: Why are RhD-negative women of childbearing age given RhD-negative platelets?
  - A: To avoid sensitisation by the small number of D-positive red cells in the platelet product
- Q: How much lower is von Willebrand factor in group O?
  - A: About 20 to 30 percent lower than A, B or AB
