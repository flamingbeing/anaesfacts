# Local Anaesthetic Potency, Onset and Duration

Lipid solubility sets potency, pKa sets onset, and protein binding sets duration, with tissue and site factors on top.

## Key rules

- Lipid solubility: more lipid soluble means more potent (and also faster onset and longer duration)
- pKa: lower pKa means more unionised drug at a given pH, so faster onset
- Protein binding: more binding means longer duration

## Factors affecting onset

- Drug factors
  - pKa (main factor)
    - Only unionised drug crosses membranes; with pKa usually above 7.4, local anaesthetics are mostly ionised at pH 7.4
    - Lower pKa means more unionised drug and faster onset
      - Lignocaine: pKa 7.9, about 25% unionised at pH 7.4
      - Bupivacaine: pKa 8.1, so less unionised at pH 7.4 (about 15%); pKa also quoted as 7.9
    - Sodium bicarbonate (typically 1 ml to 10 ml of local anaesthetic) raises pH and the neutral fraction, speeding onset
  - Lipophilicity: more lipid-soluble drugs diffuse faster, but pKa matters more for onset
  - Particle size: smaller particles diffuse faster (relevant for topical agents)
- Tissue factors
  - Tissue pH: infected or ischaemic tissue is more acidic, further cutting the unionised fraction
  - Transporters speed onset
  - Lipid make-up of the membrane

## Factors affecting duration

- Protein binding (main factor; albumin and α1-acid glycoprotein)
  - Highly bound drugs (e.g. bupivacaine) hold more firmly to their binding sites and act longer
  - Low binding also means lower bioavailability; hypoxia, hypercarbia and acidaemia reduce binding and raise toxicity risk
- Lipophilicity: higher lipid solubility increases membrane affinity and lengthens action
- Blood flow at the injection site: higher flow clears drug faster
  - Peak blood levels for the same dose by site: intravenous > tracheal > intercostal > caudal > epidural > brachial plexus > sciatic
  - Infiltration lasts much less than a nerve block: ropivacaine and bupivacaine 4 to 8 hours, lidocaine only 1 to 2 hours
- Adrenaline and other vasoconstrictors: duration depends on time spent at the axon
  - Greatest effect with short-acting drugs (lidocaine block lengthened by 50% with adrenaline)
  - Bupivacaine and ropivacaine leave nerve tissue so slowly that adrenaline adds little duration, but it lowers their peak blood levels
  - Adrenaline-containing solutions are made at lower pH (4–5), since adrenaline is unstable in alkali
  - Vasodilator activity of the drug itself: procaine is a strong vasodilator, increasing local flow and clearance
- Metabolism
  - Esters (procaine, amethocaine): hydrolysed by plasma esterases to inactive products; shorter half-life than amides
  - Amides: hepatic metabolism; liver dysfunction or low hepatic blood flow slows it and prolongs action

## Factors affecting potency

- Lipophilicity (main factor)
  - The nerve membrane is 90% lipid; more lipid-soluble drugs enter it more easily and block at lower concentrations
- Use-dependent block
  - Repeated depolarisation keeps channels open or inactivated for longer, improving access; frequent firing speeds block
- Fibre size and type
  - "Size principle" underlies differential block: sympathetic fibres block before pain and temperature, which block before motor, pressure and proprioception
    - Myelinated axons are more sensitive, possibly because conduction jumps between nodes of Ranvier and each node must reach threshold for the next; so autonomic B fibres block faster than C fibres
  - Usual order: sympathetic (often with temperature change), then sensory (pain, light touch), then motor (paralysis)
    - Mnemonic: ATP, GTP, MTP
  - Clinically, motor and pressure sensation may persist despite sympathectomy and loss of pain

## Flashcards

- Q: Main determinant of onset, potency and duration?
  - A: pKa (onset), lipid solubility (potency), protein binding (duration)
- Q: pKa and unionised fraction at pH 7.4 of lignocaine and bupivacaine?
  - A: Lignocaine 7.9, about 25%; bupivacaine 8.1, about 15%
- Q: By how much does adrenaline lengthen a lidocaine block?
  - A: About 50%
- Q: Duration of infiltration with bupivacaine or ropivacaine versus lidocaine?
  - A: 4 to 8 hours versus 1 to 2 hours
- Q: Usual order of differential block?
  - A: Sympathetic, then sensory, then motor
