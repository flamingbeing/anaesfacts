# Adrenergic and dopamine receptors

G protein-coupled α, β and dopamine receptors: their signalling (cAMP, IP3), effects, examples, and how receptor numbers change.

## History

- 1913: adrenaline shown to have two separate effects: vasoconstriction in some vascular beds (raising BP) and vasodilatation in others
- 1948: catecholamines proposed to act on two receptors, alpha (α) and beta (β)
  - α: vasoconstriction; potency noradrenaline > adrenaline > isoprenaline
  - β: vasodilatation; potency isoprenaline > adrenaline > noradrenaline
- Selective antagonists confirmed this split; later work found subtypes of both

## Structure and signalling

- All adrenoceptors are G protein-coupled receptors
  - Seven transmembrane segments, three extracellular and three intracellular loops
  - β receptor transmembrane domains are named M1 to M7
  - Antagonists bind at specific sites; agonists attach more diffusely to the hydrophobic membrane-spanning domains
- Each class couples to a different G protein and effector
  - α1: Gq, activates phospholipase C
  - α2: Gi, inhibits adenylyl cyclase
  - β: Gs, stimulates adenylyl cyclase
- cAMP
  - Heart: raises contractility (inotropy), heart rate (chronotropy) and conduction velocity (dromotropy)
  - Smooth muscle: relaxation, by blocking calcium release from intracellular stores and by protein kinase A phosphorylating actin-myosin regulatory proteins
  - Broken down by phosphodiesterase; in heart and smooth muscle this is type 3 (PDE3)
  - PDE3 inhibitors stop cAMP breakdown, so its effects are stronger and longer
  - PDE3 inhibitors also reduce platelet aggregation (more platelet cAMP)

## α1 receptor

- Gq-coupled: phospholipase C, more IP3, so intracellular Ca2+ rises
- At least four subtypes: α1A, α1B, α1C, α1D
- Vascular smooth muscle has many α1 receptors relative to β2
- Effects
  - Vasoconstriction, including pulmonary (major)
  - Gut smooth muscle relaxation, gut sphincter contraction (major)
  - Urethral sphincter contraction (major)
  - Liver glycogenolysis
  - Uterine contraction
  - Salivary secretion
  - Pupil dilation, ejaculation
- Agonist: phenylephrine; antagonist: prazosin

## α2 receptor

- Gi-coupled: inhibits adenylate cyclase, less cAMP; opens K+ channels (hyperpolarisation); closes Ca2+ channels
- Widespread in the nervous system, including the CNS
- Subtypes
  - α2A: sedation, analgesia, sympatholysis
  - α2B: vasoconstriction
  - α2C: CNS actions
- Effects
  - Sedation via α2A in the locus coeruleus; MAC appears to fall
  - Analgesia via α2A in the spinal dorsal horn: more endogenous opioid release and modulation of descending noradrenergic pain pathways
  - Gut smooth muscle relaxation
  - Blood pressure can fall or rise
    - Low doses: central sympatholysis (α2A), giving vasodilation and bradycardia
      - Less firing of the locus coeruleus (key brainstem noradrenergic relay)
      - Less noradrenaline release at the nerve terminal (negative feedback)
      - Noradrenaline acts on presynaptic α2 receptors to limit its own release; postjunctional α-1 receptors act on target tissue, prejunctional α-2 receptors give feedback
    - High doses: α2B on resistance vessel smooth muscle cause peripheral vasoconstriction and hypertension
    - Biphasic response: an IV bolus can first cause hypertension from vasoconstriction; a slow infusion (e.g. over 10 minutes) reduces this
- Agonist: clonidine; antagonist: yohimbine

## β1 receptor

- Gs-coupled: stimulates adenylate cyclase, more cAMP (true of all β receptors)
- Mainly in the heart; also platelets
- Effects
  - Positive inotropy and chronotropy, raising cardiac output (major)
  - Faster AV node conduction and more automaticity
  - Gut smooth muscle relaxation (major)
  - Platelet aggregation
- Agonist: dobutamine; antagonist: atenolol

## β2 receptor

- Gs-coupled: more cAMP
- On vascular and non-vascular smooth muscle
- Effects
  - Bronchodilatation (major): basis of β2 agonist bronchodilators
    - Also reduce release of inflammatory cytokines and bronchoconstrictor mediators, and may increase surfactant
    - Still debated: effects on inflammatory cells and β2 downregulation may harm; mortality concerns with long-acting β2 agonists in some groups
  - Uterine relaxation: basis of β2 agonist tocolytics
  - Bladder relaxation (outweighs alpha-1 contraction) and visceral smooth muscle relaxation generally
  - Vasodilatation in muscle, gut and kidney (major)
  - Small rise in cardiac output (less than β1)
  - Glycogenolysis in liver and skeletal muscle
  - Muscle tremor
  - More aqueous humour from the ciliary process
- Agonist binding site lies in the hydrophobic core within the lipid bilayer
  - Lipophilic drugs build a depot in the membrane and keep re-binding, so they act much longer than hydrophilic drugs
- Agonist: salbutamol; antagonist: butoxamine (not used clinically)

## β3 receptor

- Gs-coupled: more cAMP
- Mainly in adipose tissue
- Effects: lipolysis; non-shivering (catecholamine-driven) thermogenesis in brown fat

## D1 receptor

- Gs-coupled: more cAMP
- CNS and peripheral; postsynaptic
- Effects
  - CNS: modulates extrapyramidal activity
  - Periphery: vasodilation of renal (strongest), mesenteric, splenic and coronary vessels, redirecting blood flow to the kidneys

## D2 receptor

- Gi-coupled: less cAMP (like α2)
- CNS and peripheral; presynaptic
- Effects
  - CNS: may mediate nausea and vomiting, which may explain droperidol's antiemetic action
  - Periphery: inhibits noradrenaline release (perhaps acetylcholine too)

## Receptor regulation

- β receptor numbers change quickly with the noradrenaline level in the synapse or plasma
- Up-regulation
  - Receptor numbers rise within 30 minutes of denervation or β blockade
  - May explain rebound tachycardia, and more MI and ischaemia, when β blockers are stopped suddenly
  - Long-term states such as varicose veins and ageing reduce receptor number or response
- Desensitisation (rapid loss of response despite ongoing agonist)
  - Mechanisms: uncoupling (e.g. phosphorylation), sequestration, down-regulation
  - Rapid β desensitisation needs no internalisation: the receptor is uncoupled from Gs
  - Phosphorylation by two classes of serine-threonine kinase
    - One gives receptor-specific (homologous) desensitisation
    - The other, via second messenger-dependent kinases, gives general (heterologous) hyporesponsiveness
  - An arrestin protein then binds the phosphorylated receptor and blocks signalling; arrestins may also help internalise receptors
  - Phosphorylation only happens to active receptors, so brief β blockade has been used for a "receptor holiday" (e.g. CHF, cardiopulmonary bypass)
  - Recovery needs sequestration, dephosphorylation and probable recycling; no new protein synthesis, so numbers can shift fast
- Down-regulation
  - After hours of agonist exposure (chronic stress, CHF); receptors are destroyed and must be resynthesised
  - CHF is the best-studied example
    - Cardiac β receptor density falls markedly in end-stage heart failure with high catecholamines, so exogenous β agonists work poorly
    - β1 and β2 coexist in human ventricles; in CHF β1 density falls while β2 is unchanged
    - So β2 gives 60% of the inotropic response to isoprenaline in the failing heart, against 40% in the non-failing heart
- Other influences on receptor density
  - Hyperthyroidism increases it, hypothyroidism decreases it; β blockade matters in acute hyperthyroidism treatment
  - Corticosteroids may reduce it
  - So responses to sympathetic agonists vary with disease and circumstances
  - The structural likeness of thyroid hormone and tyrosine suggests false transmitters may play a part
