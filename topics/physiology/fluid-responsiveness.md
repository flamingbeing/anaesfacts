# Fluid Responsiveness

A rise in cardiac output of more than 10-15% after 500 ml of fluid; best predicted by dynamic tests, not static pressures.

## Quick reference

- Static measures: CVP, PAOP, IVC and LVEDA size (poor predictors)
- Passive leg raise: responder if SV/CO changes by 10%; MAP 13%
- SVV, PPV, SPV or IVC variability above 12%
  - Needs sinus rhythm, no spontaneous breaths, tidal volume 8 ml/kg

## Definition

- CO increase (delta CO) above 10-15 % after 500 ml
- A dynamic parameter
- Shows the heart can raise CO when filling changes: there is preload reserve (steep, ascending part of the Starling curve)
- In practice, moving from hypovolaemia towards euvolaemia

## Predictors

- Only two methods have proven good accuracy
  - Fluid challenge
  - Passive leg raise

## Static tests (poor predictors)

- Clinical signs: heart rate, blood pressure, collapsed veins, capillary refill time, recent urine output
  - Insensitive; poor agreement between observers
- CVP (and change in CVP after a challenge)
  - Used to guide fluids since 1959; correlates poorly with intravascular volume and not at all with responsiveness
  - Needs a central line; read at end expiration
  - Reflects right atrial pressure, roughly RV preload
    - Hypovolaemia: CVP below 5 mmHg
    - Unlikely to respond to IV fluid if CVP above 10 mmHg
  - Venous return driving pressure: (MCFP − RAP) / RVR
    - MCFP: mean circulatory filling pressure; RAP: right atrial pressure; RVR: resistance to venous return
    - MCFP reflects the stressed venous volume (contributes to preload)
    - Fluid raises driving pressure only in responders, i.e. when RAP does not rise
    - Venodilation shifts blood from stressed to unstressed volume
  - Pressure–volume chain is complex (CVP, RAP, RVEDP, RVEDV) and affected by
    - Venous return
    - Posture, abdominal pressure
    - RV compliance, LV failure, valve disease
    - Lung disease (pulmonary hypertension), mechanical ventilation (PEEP, lung compliance)
  - Use only alongside other data; trends are more useful than single values
- PAOP (PCWP): also fails to predict responsiveness; rarely used
  - Needs a PA catheter; measured with the balloon wedged in a PA branch
  - Reflects LAP, i.e. LV preload
    - Hypovolaemia: PAOP below 8 mmHg
  - Chain PAOP, LAP, LVEDP, LVEDV is affected by
    - LV and LA compliance, valve disease
    - Lung disease
    - Catheter tip position
    - Frequent misreading
- IVC ultrasound size: tracks CVP, so shares its weakness
- Chest X-ray or lung ultrasound for pulmonary oedema: unreliable
- Lactate or SvO2: not useful

## Dynamic tests

- Principle: make a brief change in preload and watch the effect on cardiac output

### Fluid challenge (definitive test)

- Rapid 250-500 ml IV fluid
- Mini-challenge of 100 ml over 2 min predicts the response to 500 ml
- Main risk: fluid overload

### Passive leg raise (PLR)

- Technique
  - Start with head up at 45 degrees
  - Lay the head flat and lift the legs to 45 degrees
  - Measure rise in stroke volume (cardiac output monitor) or a surrogate such as pulse pressure (arterial line)
  - Peak effect at 30-90 seconds
    - Aortic flow rises within seconds in preload-dependent patients and peaks at about 1 min
    - Patients without a sustained PLR response also do not sustain a response to fluid
    - So assess within 30–90 s of starting
- Mechanism: transient rise in venous return, an "autotransfusion"
  - Shifts about 150 ml from the calves (measured with radiolabelled red cells), raising venous return by about 150ml
- Advantages: reversible, non-invasive, well validated; works with spontaneous breathing and arrhythmias
- Disadvantages: unreliable in severe hypovolaemia; repositioning may not be possible

### End-expiratory occlusion (EEO)

- Needs mechanical ventilation
- 15 second expiratory hold
- A rise in pulse pressure or cardiac index predicts responsiveness accurately
- The hold removes the cyclic fall in left heart preload caused by inspiration, acting like a fluid challenge
- Disadvantages: patient must not breathe spontaneously during the 15 second pause; less validated

### Change in ETCO2

- Can be used as a surrogate of CO change

### Respiratory variation tests

- Systolic pressure variation (SPV), pulse pressure variation (PPV), stroke volume variation (SVV)
- PPV = (PPmax − PPmin) / ((PPmax + PPmin) / 2)
  - PPV above 12%: fluid responder
  - PPV 9-12%: grey zone
- PPV is more reliable than SPV
  - SPV is more affected by pleural pressure swings
  - Pleural pressure affects both SBP and DBP, so it cancels out in PPV
- SVV = (SVmax − SVmin) / ((SVmax + SVmin) / 2)
  - SVV above 12%: fluid responsive
- Devices
  - Pulse contour analysis
    - FloTrac/Vigileo (Edwards Lifesciences): arterial line
    - VolumeView/EV 1000 (Edwards Lifesciences) and PiCCO (Pulsion Medical Systems): central line and arterial line; thermodilution
    - LiDCO Plus: arterial line; lithium dilution with pulse contour CO
  - Pleth Variability Index (Masimo): SpO2 probe; analyses peak and amplitude changes in the oximeter waveform
  - Oesophageal Doppler: placement can be difficult; flow–time analysis gives CO and SV
  - Bioimpedance vector analysis: Ohm's law applied to impedance change with volume; chest electrodes; correlates poorly with measured CO
  - Eyeballing respiratory swing on the arterial trace or SpO2 trace
- Accuracy is limited by
  - AF (needs regular R-R interval; applies to SVV and PPV)
  - Spontaneous breathing (needs 100% controlled ventilation at a fixed rate)
  - Small tidal volumes, e.g. ARDS (needs at least 8 mL/kg)
  - High PEEP (may raise SVV)
  - Open chest
  - Noradrenaline (may lower SVV)
  - Vasodilators (may raise SVV)
- Other causes of falsely high SVV
  - Obstructive shock: tamponade, massive PE, pneumothorax
  - High airway pressure, dynamic hyperinflation or PEEP, raised intra-abdominal pressure

## Focused echocardiography

- Right-sided measures
  - Static: IVC size
  - Dynamic: change in IVC and SVC diameter
- Left-sided measures
  - Static: LVEDA, LVESA
  - Dynamic: SVV, change in aortic VTI, change in aortic peak velocity
- Others: E/Ea, PLR or EEO combined with echo, IVO, STE
- Helps with differential diagnosis
- Still an indirect measure of fluid status and need; combine with lung ultrasound to detect overload
- IVC and SVC
  - IVC size (TTE)
  - IVC distensibility, collapsibility, variability (TTE)
  - SVC variability (TTE or TEE)

## Focused lung ultrasound

- Look for B-lines (fluid overload)

## Flashcards

- Q: Definition of fluid responsiveness?
  - A: CO rises by more than 10-15% after 500 ml
- Q: PPV or SVV threshold for a fluid responder?
  - A: Above 12% (PPV 9-12% is a grey zone)
- Q: Conditions needed for respiratory variation tests to be valid?
  - A: Sinus rhythm, fully controlled ventilation, tidal volume at least 8 ml/kg, closed chest
- Q: When is the peak effect of a passive leg raise?
  - A: 30-90 seconds; it shifts about 150 ml
- Q: How long is the end-expiratory occlusion hold?
  - A: 15 seconds
