# Gas Kinetics of Inhaled Anaesthetics

How inhaled agents reach the brain and leave it: speed depends on inspired concentration, ventilation, FRC, solubility, cardiac output and brain uptake.

## Induction: steps to effect

1. Inspired into the lungs from the breathing circuit
2. Absorbed from alveoli into blood
3. Carried in blood to the target tissue
4. Absorbed from blood into the target tissue (the brain)

## Emergence and elimination

- Eliminated by the lungs: the induction sequence runs in reverse
- Induction and recovery are not mirror images
  - At induction all tissue partial pressures are zero
  - At recovery tissues hold different partial pressures, so recovery is less controllable
- Long anaesthetics in obese patients: highly fat-soluble agents build up in fat and slow recovery (longer context-sensitive half-time)
- Only a small fraction is metabolised

## Partial pressure

- At steady state alveolar (PA), arterial (Pa) and brain (PB) partial pressures are in equilibrium
- So PA is an indirect measure of PB
- Steady state is seldom reached clinically; it can take many hours
- Patient and agent factors set how fast equilibrium is approached

## Factors affecting speed of induction and emergence

- Alveolar concentration
  - Inspired concentration: fresh gas flow, breathing system volume, circuit absorption
  - Alveolar ventilation
  - Functional residual capacity
  - Second gas effect
- Uptake from the lungs
  - Solubility (blood–gas partition coefficient)
  - Pulmonary blood flow (cardiac output)
- Uptake by the brain
  - Brain–blood partition coefficient

## Alveolar concentration

### Inspired concentration

- High inspired concentration: large alveolar–blood gradient, arterial level rises faster, faster induction
- On emergence an inspired concentration of zero drives agent out of blood into the alveoli
- Fresh gas flow
  - High flow replaces exhaled gas (lower in agent) quickly, keeping inspired concentration high
  - On emergence high agent-free flow flushes the circuit and speeds elimination

### Minute ventilation

- More alveolar ventilation: PA rises faster, so PB rises faster and onset is quicker
- High minute ventilation (rate x tidal volume) speeds both induction and emergence
  - Relevant to gas inductions and to most emergences
  - e.g. an infant (high minute ventilation) induces and wakes faster than an elderly patient
- Ventilation matters more for soluble agents (e.g. diethyl ether) than insoluble ones (e.g. nitrous oxide)
  - Common modern agents have low to intermediate solubility, so the effect is moderate

### Functional residual capacity

- Large FRC dilutes the inspired agent, so onset is slow

### Second gas effect

- Nitrous oxide (N2O) increases uptake of a companion agent (the "second gas")

## Uptake from the lungs

### Blood solubility (blood–gas partition coefficient)

- Also called the Ostwald solubility coefficient
- Anaesthesia depends on partial pressure in blood and brain, not the total amount
- Soluble agents have a larger blood reservoir to fill before tissue partial pressure is adequate, so induction is slower
- The same large reservoir must empty on emergence, so emergence is slower too
- Definition: ratio of agent amount in blood to gas, with equal volumes, at equal pressure, in equilibrium at 37°C
- Lower coefficient (less soluble): faster induction and washout
- Main determinants: serum proteins and red cells
- Lower coefficients
  - Haemodilution
  - Obesity
  - Hypoalbuminaemia and starvation
- Higher coefficients
  - Adults (compared with children)
  - Hypothermia
  - After meals
- e.g. other factors equal, isoflurane induction and emergence are slower than desflurane

### Cardiac output

- Low cardiac output reduces uptake, so PA rises faster and onset is quicker
- High cardiac output keeps the alveolar–blood gradient up, so PA rises slowly
- Modern agents are fairly insoluble, so cardiac output affects them much less than soluble agents

## Uptake by the brain

### Brain–blood partition coefficient

- Ratio of agent concentration in brain to blood at equal partial pressures
- Sets how fast the brain equilibrates
- Lower coefficient: less agent needed to saturate the brain, faster equilibration, faster induction and emergence
- Possibly similar to the oil:blood coefficient

## Context-sensitive half-times

- 50% decrement time (context-sensitive half-time) matters if waking and adequate breathing return after a 50% fall in concentration
- If an 80% fall is needed, the 80% decrement time is more relevant
- Decrement time differences roughly follow blood–gas solubility and widen with longer anaesthetics
- Exception: the 50% decrement time is almost the same for all agents
  - The first phase of elimination (about 50% of the fall) depends mainly on ventilation
  - Elimination rate is proportional to alveolar concentration times minute ventilation
  - As alveolar concentration falls, delivery to the lungs limits elimination, and cardiac output and solubility dominate

## Flashcards

- Q: What does the blood–gas partition coefficient predict?
  - A: Speed of induction and emergence: a lower coefficient gives faster induction and washout
- Q: Effect of low cardiac output on inhalational induction?
  - A: Uptake falls, PA rises faster, so onset is quicker
- Q: Effect of a large FRC on inhalational induction?
  - A: Dilutes the inspired agent, so onset is slow
- Q: Factors that raise the blood–gas coefficient?
  - A: Adults versus children, hypothermia, after meals
- Q: Why is the 50% decrement time similar for all volatiles?
  - A: The first phase of elimination depends mainly on ventilation
