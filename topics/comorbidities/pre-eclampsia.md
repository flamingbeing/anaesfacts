# Pre-eclampsia, Eclampsia and HELLP

Multisystem hypertensive disorder of pregnancy (BP 140/90 or 160/110 after 20 weeks): control BP, give magnesium, restrict fluids, plan neuraxial early and prepare for a difficult airway.

## Key points

- BP 140/90 (or severe 160/110) after 20 weeks, with proteinuria
- Organ involvement: renal, neurological (seizures), pulmonary oedema, airway oedema, liver, low platelets and haemolysis (HELLP)
- Early epidural unless thrombocytopenic (airway oedema makes GA difficult)
- Labetalol for BP
- MgSO4 4 g load, then 1 g/hr (target 2-4 mmol/L)
- Fluids under 1 ml/kg/hr
- Delivery: spinal if not thrombocytopenic
- After delivery: uterotonics (avoid ergometrine); avoid nephrotoxins (e.g. NSAIDs)

## Definitions

- Hypertension: sustained SBP >140 or DBP >90
  - Severe hypertension: SBP >160 or DBP >110
- Chronic hypertension: present before pregnancy (before 20 weeks gestation)
  - Superimposed pre-eclampsia if any of:
    - 1) Sudden rise in BP or rapid escalation of antihypertensives
    - 2) New proteinuria
    - 3) Significant new end-organ dysfunction
- Gestational hypertension: arises after 20 weeks, without other signs of pre-eclampsia
- Pre-eclampsia: hypertension (>140/90) starting after 20 weeks gestation (resolving within 6 weeks of delivery) plus either:
  - 1) Significant proteinuria: >300 mg/24 hrs
    - Alternatives: uPCR >0.3 mg/dL or 30 mg/ml, or dipstick 1+ or more suggests 0.3 g/day or more (ideally 2 samples 4 hrs apart)
  - 2) Significant end-organ dysfunction (added in the 2013 ACOG criteria): classed as severe
- Severe pre-eclampsia (now "pre-eclampsia with severe features"), either:
  - 1) Severe hypertension: SBP >160 or DBP >100
    - Severe proteinuria no longer a criterion (poor link with outcome)
  - 2) Significant end-organ dysfunction:
    - Renal: raised creatinine, oliguria (urine output <400 ml/24 hr)
    - Neurological or visual: seizures (eclampsia), visual disturbance, papilloedema, clonus
    - Respiratory: pulmonary oedema (and airway oedema)
    - Liver: severe right upper quadrant pain or tenderness, raised transaminases
    - Haematological: thrombocytopenia (platelets <100), haemolysis, DIC
  - Fetal growth restriction no longer a criterion (managed the same either way)
- Eclampsia: seizure in pregnancy or the puerperium in a woman with pre-eclampsia, with no other cause
  - May have no signs of pre-eclampsia before the 1st seizure
- Pre-eclampsia is a multisystem disorder of hypertension with organ derangement
  - Cause not fully known: immune, genetic, endothelial, abnormal placental implantation, fatty acid metabolism, coagulation and platelet factors
  - Earlier onset in pregnancy means more severe disease
- HELLP: likely a form of severe pre-eclampsia; rarely occurs without hypertension or proteinuria
  - 1) Haemolysis: falling Hb without bleeding, haemoglobinuria, raised bilirubin, raised LDH
  - 2) Elevated liver enzymes: right upper quadrant pain from liver bleeding
  - 3) Low platelets: <100 (<50 is severe)
  - Partial HELLP: two of the three features
- Postpartum pre-eclampsia
  - Usually within 7 days of delivery
  - Delayed-onset (late postpartum): onset >2 days but <6 weeks after birth

## Pathophysiology

- Two-stage model
- Stage 1: defective placentation (no symptoms)
  - Cytotrophoblast invasion stays in the decidua and misses the myometrial spiral arteries
  - Vessels stay narrow, high-resistance and over-responsive to vasoconstrictors
  - Leads to chronic placental ischaemia, hypoxia and risk of IUGR
- Stage 2: systemic endothelial syndrome (symptoms)
  - Fetoplacental demand outgrows the limited supply, so ischaemia worsens
  - Ischaemic placenta releases anti-angiogenic factors (sFlt-1, sEng) and cytokines, causing widespread endothelial dysfunction

## Clinical features and complications

- Neurological: cerebral oedema
  - Signs: headache, visual disturbance, papilloedema, generalised hyperreflexia or clonus, focal deficits, altered mental state
    - Headache marks end-organ involvement and is a severe-features criterion
  - Seizures (eclampsia): impaired cerebral autoregulation leads to hyperperfusion and vasogenic oedema (older theory: vasoconstriction)
  - Generalised cerebral oedema: raised ICP, herniation; posterior reversible leukoencephalopathy
  - Intracerebral haemorrhage
- Cardiovascular
  - Hypertension mainly from raised afterload
  - Greater sensitivity to endogenous and exogenous catecholamines and vasopressors
  - Reduced circulating volume: preload falls, poor tolerance of blood loss
  - Leaky capillaries cause peripheral and leg oedema
- Renal
  - GFR falls but permeability to large molecules rises, giving proteinuria (endothelial damage); frothy urine
  - Uric acid rises (2nd to reduced urate clearance)
  - Oliguria in severe disease
- Respiratory: capillary leak leads to pulmonary oedema; breathlessness
- Gastrointestinal: transaminitis (HELLP); upper abdominal or right upper quadrant pain, nausea and vomiting, jaundice
- Haematological
  - Platelet consumption, hypercoagulability and high fibrin turnover: thrombocytopenia (HELLP), DIC
  - Haemolysis
  - Haematocrit rises from fluid leak
- Fetal
  - Poor placental function, very sensitive to maternal BP changes
  - IUGR and oligohydramnios; CTG abnormalities
  - Placental abruption
- Anaesthetic
  - Airway: pharyngolaryngeal and facial oedema narrowing or obstructing the upper airway; stridor, hoarseness, voice change
  - Back oedema
  - Increased sensitivity to vasopressors e.g. phenylephrine
- Magnesium effects: toxicity, uterine atony, prolonged neuromuscular block

## Investigations

- Pre-eclampsia bloods: FBC, renal panel, LFT, DIC screen, group and crossmatch
- 24 hr urine protein, urine dipstick
- Fetal assessment: ultrasound

## Prevention

- Aspirin for high-risk patients

## Management on delivery suite

- Control BP (mainly to prevent intracranial haemorrhage)
  - Consider arterial line
  - Keep BP <160/110 (SBP >180 is a medical emergency)
  - Oral options: labetalol; methyldopa 0.5-3 g/day; nifedipine 10-20 mg; beta-blockers (metoprolol, propranolol, esmolol)
  - Avoid ACE inhibitors
  - Rapid control (lower by 10-20 mmHg every 20 min):
    - Labetalol IV 5-10 mg slowly
    - Hydralazine IV 5 mg aliquots up to 20 mg; onset 30 min, so titrate very slowly
    - Nifedipine sublingual 5-10 mg (avoid with magnesium)
    - GTN IV 0.1-0.8 mcg/kg/min
    - Sodium nitroprusside IV 1-4 mcg/kg/min
  - Epidurals are not a primary BP treatment: modern high-volume low-concentration regimens do little to BP, but easing contraction pain lowers BP indirectly
- Careful fluids
  - Balance rehydration of a depleted circulation against high risk of pulmonary oedema
    - Severe pre-eclampsia: plasma volume 30-40% lower, so poor tolerance of obstetric haemorrhage
    - Volume expansion lowers SVR and SBP
  - Usually limit intake to 1 ml/kg/hr (up to 80 ml/hr)
  - Pulmonary oedema: oxygen, fluid restriction, furosemide boluses 20-60 mg
- Severe pre-eclampsia: MgSO4 infusion to prevent eclampsia
  - NNT 91 to prevent one seizure (Magpie trial)
  - Indication: any sign of severe pre-eclampsia
  - Magnesium prevents seizures; it is not a BP treatment
- Delivery of baby and placenta
  - Severe pre-eclampsia or eclampsia: deliver within 24 hours
  - Mild: at 37 weeks
  - Severe: at 34 weeks, with steroids for fetal lungs
    - Dexamethasone 2 doses 12 hrs apart; ideally deliver 24 hrs after the 2nd dose, but often not possible
  - Under 32 weeks: try to prolong the pregnancy
- Supportive care
  - Airway: be ready for difficult intubation
  - Breathing: oxygen, positive pressure ventilation if needed
  - Circulation: 2 large-bore IV lines, left lateral tilt, monitor urine output
  - Neurological: MgSO4 as above; midazolam for resistant seizures
  - Gastrointestinal: nil by mouth; acid prophylaxis with sodium citrate 0.3M 30 ml 6-hourly and IV ranitidine 150 mg 12-hourly
  - Fetal: CTG monitoring
  - Disposition: delivery suite or high dependency

## Labour analgesia

- Early epidural is good if no contraindication

## Preoperative assessment

- Multidisciplinary input: obstetrician, neonatologist, midwife
- Platelets and clotting within 6 hrs in all cases, or within 2 hrs if severe (BJA 2016)
  - Platelets >75 with normal clotting: neuraxial likely fine (SOAP 2021)
  - Platelets <50: absolute contraindication to neuraxial
- Assess oedema
  - Pharyngolaryngeal: facial oedema, hoarseness or voice change, stridor
  - Back oedema: difficult spinal
- Continuous CTG; left lateral tilt
- Careful fluids: max 1 ml/kg/hr or 80 ml/hr

## Intraoperative management

- Neuraxial (single-shot spinal, CSE, epidural) suits most patients
  - Check for thrombocytopenia and clotting problems
  - Increased sensitivity to vasoconstrictors: phenylephrine infusion usable with care
  - Early epidural if no contraindication: less sympathetic drive, better BP control and uteroplacental flow, eases operative delivery, may avoid GA
- GA when regional is contraindicated
  - Problems: difficult airway, pressor response to laryngoscopy
  - Benefit: seizures may be easier to manage under GA
  - Intubation
    - Upper airway oedema makes intubation harder: difficult airway trolley, skilled assistant, videolaryngoscope, range of ETTs (size 5.5, 6.0, 6.5), bougie and stylet
    - Marked pressor surges at laryngoscopy, intubation and extubation risk intracranial haemorrhage: blunt with short-acting agents (esmolol, opioids) and adequate depth
  - Maintenance: extra short-acting opioid may be needed (e.g. alfentanil 10 mcg/kg boluses); tell the neonatologist
  - Extubation
    - Airway oedema may worsen during surgery: cuff leak test before extubation, consider IV dexamethasone, may need delayed extubation
    - After magnesium, non-depolarising relaxants last longer: check train of four
- Monitoring: consider arterial line and 5-lead ECG
  - Awake arterial line if severe disease needing IV antihypertensives or MgSO4 infusion
- Ventilation: higher airway pressures, PEEP and FiO2 may be needed with pulmonary oedema; may need postoperative ventilation
- Fluids: limit to 1 ml/kg/hr (up to 80 ml/hr) unless replacing losses such as haemorrhage; use smaller fluid challenges
- Multimodal analgesia to blunt the hypertensive response to pain
  - Avoid NSAIDs until proteinuria has resolved (likely >48 hrs)
  - Regional blocks (e.g. bilateral TAP) if no neuraxial
- Obstetric issues
  - Avoid ergometrine and syntometrine: acute hypertension
  - If magnesium given: prolonged paralysis (check train of four); oxytocin infusion for uterine tone; prepare for PPH (poorly tolerated; ask the obstetrician to consider early surgical measures)

## Postoperative management

- Continue antihypertensives, MgSO4 and fluid restriction
- Delivery suite for BP, oxygen and seizure monitoring; high dependency with invasive BP if severe
- If an epidural catheter is left in, check platelets before removing it

## Eclampsia

- Incidence 1:3500
  - 40% antepartum, 20% intrapartum, 40% postpartum
  - Most common in the 3rd trimester or within 12 hrs of delivery
- Life-threatening, but the seizure is usually brief and self-limiting
- Clinical features
  - Usually a generalised tonic-clonic seizure
  - Fetal bradycardia for at least 3-5 minutes is common during and just after the seizure
  - After the seizure: fetal tachycardia and reduced variability, sometimes decelerations; should improve with treatment
  - Persistently non-reassuring CTG suggests abruption
- May come before pre-eclampsia is recognised; warning signs of impending eclampsia:
  - Headache
  - Visual disturbance (including cortical blindness)
  - Abdominal pain (liver capsule stretch)
  - Hyperreflexia
  - 25% have no symptoms
- Most seizures in pregnancy are not eclampsia: epilepsy, hypoglycaemia in diabetes, LAST, intracranial causes (meningitis, tumour, haemorrhage, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, stroke)
- Differential of a seizure in pre-eclampsia
  - Eclamptic seizure
  - Intracranial event (subarachnoid haemorrhage, stroke)
  - Hypotension and syncope (e.g. rapid BP fall from treatment)
  - Hypoglycaemia
  - Pre-existing epilepsy
- Treatment
  - Stop the seizure with an MgSO4 2 g bolus (even if already on an infusion); magnesium is first line, not benzodiazepines
  - Refractory seizures: IV diazepam 10 mg
  - Eclampsia alone is not an indication for caesarean if the fetus is well; vaginal delivery or caesarean both acceptable
- Prophylaxis: MgSO4 is the anticonvulsant of choice

## Magnesium infusion

- Dose
  - Load 4 g over 10 min
  - Maintenance 1 g/hour
  - Continue for 24 hours after delivery or after the last seizure (some protocols shorter)
  - Seizure on MgSO4: 2 g bolus over 5 min
- Toxicity risk: renal impairment with low urine output; adjust rate
- Monitor reflexes 4-hourly, alertness and vital signs including RR
- Signs of toxicity
  - Loss of patellar reflexes, weakness: stop infusion and check level
  - Respiratory depression (RR <10, SpO2 <92%)
  - Drowsiness, loss of consciousness
  - Treatment: 10 ml of 10% calcium gluconate (1 g)
- Check level if patellar reflexes are lost or if oliguric (<100 ml in 4 hours)
  - Target 2-4 mmol/L (4-8 mEq/L)
  - 2.5-5: ECG changes (long PR, wide QRS)
  - >5 mmol/L: loss of deep tendon reflexes
  - 6-7: respiratory depression
  - >7.5: respiratory arrest, CNS depression, SA or AV block
  - >12: cardiac arrest
- If level <4 mmol/L or reflexes return, restart at 0.5 g/hr
- Crosses the placenta: newborn may be hypotonic with respiratory depression; treat with calcium
- ECG changes of hypermagnesaemia resemble hyperkalaemia; antidote is calcium

## HELLP syndrome

- Likely a form of pre-eclampsia with severe features
  - Hypertension in 85% of cases
  - Rarely before 20 weeks; may present postpartum
- Management as for pre-eclampsia, plus delivery of baby and placenta
  - Without severe features: vaginal delivery may be acceptable
  - Severe HELLP: urgent caesarean
- Prepare for complications (major haemorrhage, ARDS, renal failure, DIC): invasive monitoring, high dependency
- Recovery 24-48 hrs after delivery
- Complications
  - Major haemorrhage or abruption (55%)
  - DIC (21%)
  - AKI (8%)
  - Pulmonary oedema (6%)
  - Subcapsular liver haematoma or rupture (1%)
  - Intracerebral haemorrhage, retinal detachment
- Severe HELLP: 5% mortality

## Flashcards

- Q: Magnesium sulphate regimen for severe pre-eclampsia?
  - A: 4 g over 10 min, then 1 g/hour for 24 hours after delivery or last seizure; 2 g over 5 min for a seizure on the infusion
- Q: Therapeutic magnesium range and treatment of toxicity?
  - A: 2-4 mmol/L; 10 ml of 10% calcium gluconate (1 g)
- Q: Platelet thresholds for neuraxial in pre-eclampsia?
  - A: >75 with normal clotting likely fine; <50 is an absolute contraindication
- Q: Maximum fluid rate in pre-eclampsia?
  - A: 1 ml/kg/hr, up to 80 ml/hr
- Q: Which uterotonic must be avoided?
  - A: Ergometrine (and syntometrine): acute hypertension
