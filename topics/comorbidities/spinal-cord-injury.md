# Spinal Cord Injury

Anaesthesia after spinal cord injury: phase-dependent risks (spinal shock, autonomic dysreflexia, suxamethonium hyperkalaemia) plus the long-term effects of immobility.

## Key issues

- Compression leads to ischaemia: maintain cord perfusion
- Unstable spine: precautions (e.g. cervical collar); airway may be difficult
- Trauma or transection: autonomic dysfunction
- Level of injury
  - Spinal shock in the first weeks to months
  - C3-5: diaphragm
  - T1 onwards: upper limbs affected
  - T7: respiratory insufficiency starts
  - T6: autonomic dysreflexia
- Acute: ATLS, unstable spine, cord perfusion, neurogenic shock, multisystem injury
- Subacute: suxamethonium risk, autonomic dysreflexia
- Chronic: problems of long-term immobility
  - Pressure sores and ulcers
  - Contractures, scoliosis
  - Recurrent pneumonia
  - Poor dental hygiene
  - Gastroparesis and feeding problems leading to malnutrition
  - Faecal impaction, constipation
  - DVT risk
  - Difficult venous access

## Three phases

- Initial (minutes)
  - Extreme hypertension and arrhythmias, risking MI, LV failure and pulmonary oedema
- Spinal shock (immediately, lasting 3 days to 8 weeks)
  - Hypotension, bradycardia, loss of sympathetic tone
  - Parasympathetic tone persists, so severe bradycardia or asystole can occur, especially at intubation or tracheal suction
  - Common with lesions above T7
  - Loss of muscle tone and reflexes below the lesion
  - Paralytic ileus common
- Reflex phase (up to 9 months)
  - Neuronal rewiring: sympathetic tone, muscle tone and reflexes return

## Complications by system

- Cardiovascular: blood volume falls (20%); postural hypotension
- Respiratory
  - Initial level may rise with cord oedema before improving
  - Above C3: apnoea
  - C3-C5: diaphragm may be spared
  - Below C5: phrenic nerve spared; intercostals paralysed; accessory muscles recruited over 6 months; weaker cough and lower FEV1
  - FVC better supine (better diaphragmatic excursion)
  - Bronchial hypersecretion; recurrent pneumonia
- Metabolic: poor thermoregulation, no shivering, poor control of peripheral vascular tone
- Musculoskeletal
  - Spasms and spasticity (baclofen, diazepam)
  - Low bone density leading to fractures; heterotopic calcification
- Skin: pressure sores; difficult IV access
- Haematological: anaemia; higher VTE risk (some anticoagulated with warfarin after day 5)
- GI: delayed gastric emptying
- Genitourinary: recurrent UTIs
- Pain: chronic pain common

## Preoperative assessment

### History

- Injury: level, mechanism, timing
- Ongoing symptoms and complications
- Autonomic dysreflexia
- Stabilisation surgery (neuraxial block harder; intubation may be difficult)
- Complete or incomplete lesion (complete lesions have worse autonomic dysreflexia)
- Function and support at home
- Medications; allergies (latex)
- Previous anaesthetic records

### Examination

- A tracheostomy suggests a high lesion
- Formal neurological examination: lower motor neurone signs at the level, upper motor neurone signs and sensory loss below it
- Airway and precordium

### Investigations

- Bloods: FBC (anaemia), U+E (renal impairment), LFTs
- Pulmonary function tests; CXR
- Spine X-rays, CT and MRI
- ECG for high thoracic lesions
- Urine microscopy and culture

### Management

- Avoid suxamethonium from 2 days to 9 months (some say 2 years)
  - Denervation spreads acetylcholine receptors across the whole muscle; depolarisation releases a large amount of potassium, which can cause asystole
- Supportive care until haemodynamics settle
- Multidisciplinary input
- Gabapentin for neuropathic pain
- Screen for depression
- For minor surgery, decide whether anaesthesia is needed at all

### Deciding on anaesthesia

- If a normal patient would need GA for the operation, so does the cord-injured patient (even with a complete lesion)
- Minor peripheral surgery below a complete lesion is probably fine with local anaesthesia, but muscle spasm can still occur
- Urological surgery with a lesion above T5: high risk of autonomic dysreflexia

## Intraoperative management

- Spinal shock phase
  - Life-threatening surgery only
  - Before intubation: atropine 300 mcg or glycopyrrolate 200 mcg
  - Cervical spine precautions
  - Fluid preload, with or without a central line
- Reflex phase
  - History as above is key
  - Standard monitoring; fluid load
  - High cervical lesions: IPPV
  - No clear increase in aspiration risk despite reflux
  - Vasopressors as needed
  - Neuromuscular block not needed after intubation unless there is muscle spasm
  - Temperature and positioning care

### Neuraxial block

- Advantages: prevents autonomic dysreflexia; little cardiovascular instability (sympathetic tone already low); avoids GA; intrathecal opioid adds nothing
- Disadvantages: may be technically difficult; block success and level are hard to test (loss of hypertonicity, loss of lower-limb reflexes, loss of sweating)

## Postoperative management

- Nurse supine with slight head up (better ventilation)
- Monitor temperature
- Watch for autonomic dysreflexia

## Autonomic dysreflexia

- A massive, disordered autonomic response to stimulation below the lesion
- Cause: loss of descending inhibition of regenerating presynaptic fibres
- Injuries at T6 and above (as for neurogenic shock); rare below T7; more common the higher the lesion
- Starts around 2-3 weeks after injury; less likely after 9 months

### Triggers

- Genitourinary: bladder distension, UTI
- Obstetric: labour, cervical dilatation
- GI: constipation, bowel obstruction, acute abdomen
- Musculoskeletal: fractures
- Skin: minor trauma, skin infection

### Clinical features

- Hypertension (most common)
- Headache, flushing, pallor, sweating
- Nausea, anxiety
- Bradycardia
- Penile erection
- Seizures, pulmonary oedema, coma

### Treatment

- Treat the cause
  - Hidden trauma or infection
  - Catheterise the bladder
  - Check for faecal impaction
- Drugs
  - Phentolamine 2-10mg IV
  - GTN: transdermal, then sublingual, then IV
  - Clonidine 150-300mcg IV (if hypertension and spasticity)
  - Esmolol 10mg IV (only if also tachycardic)

## Pregnancy with spinal cord injury

### Issues

- Cardiovascular: exaggerated postural hypotension and response to aortocaval compression
- Respiratory: lower reserve, higher risk of respiratory failure, higher oxygen demand
- Haematological: more anaemia and VTE
- Sepsis: more infection (pressure areas, urinary)
- Labour: higher risk of preterm labour; autonomic dysreflexia with lesions above T5 (may be the first sign of labour)

### Management

- Early review, multidisciplinary plan
- Discuss neuraxial techniques
  - Epidural: usually possible; site early (the most effective way to prevent autonomic dysreflexia); fluid load and have vasopressors ready
  - Leave the epidural catheter in for 48 hours after delivery (dysreflexia risk persists for 48 hr postpartum)
  - If an epidural fails: prophylactic drug treatment against dysreflexia
  - Spinal: usually possible even after spinal instrumentation
- GA for caesarean section: risks as for GA above

## Flashcards

- Q: When should suxamethonium be avoided after spinal cord injury?
  - A: From 2 days to 9 months (some say 2 years), because of hyperkalaemia and asystole risk
- Q: Which lesion levels cause autonomic dysreflexia, and when does it start?
  - A: T6 and above; from around 2-3 weeks after injury, less likely after 9 months
- Q: Drug options for autonomic dysreflexia?
  - A: Phentolamine 2-10mg IV, GTN, clonidine 150-300mcg IV, esmolol 10mg IV if tachycardic
- Q: How long does spinal shock last?
  - A: From immediately to 3 days to 8 weeks
- Q: How long should a labour epidural stay in after delivery in a woman with SCI?
  - A: 48 hours, as dysreflexia risk persists for 48 hr postpartum
