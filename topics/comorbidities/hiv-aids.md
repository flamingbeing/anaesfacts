# HIV and AIDS

Multisystem disease in an immunocompromised patient: universal precautions, never stop ART, watch drug interactions, avoid transfusion where possible.

## Key points
- **Universal precautions; disposable** airway equipment
- **Do not stop ART**
- **Multisystem** problems
  - Respiratory: opportunistic infections
  - Cardiovascular: cardiomyopathy
  - Neurological: peripheral neuropathies
  - Renal impairment
  - Liver: ART-induced hepatotoxicity
- **Immunocompromised**: strict asepsis

## Surgery in HIV
- About 20% of people with HIV have surgery at some point; most for HIV-related problems, some unrelated (e.g. trauma)
  - Adults: lymph node biopsy, splenectomy, partial colectomy are common
  - Children: central lines, gastrostomy tubes, diagnostic procedures
- HIV alone is no reason to withhold surgery: no increase in death or complications up to 30 days after surgery

## Main considerations
- Infection control
- Multisystem effects of complications, opportunistic infections and ART
- Continue ART; beware interactions, especially hepatic
- Specific: GA versus regional, avoid transfusion
- Comorbidities: IV drug use, hepatitis B

## Preoperative assessment
- Organ effects depend on:
  - **Stage of disease** (WHO staging)
  - Coexisting opportunistic **infections** and **malignancies**
  - **ART**, anti-opportunistic drugs and interactions
- Involve an **infectious disease specialist**

### Respiratory
- **Opportunistic infection dominates**, especially when CD4 count is below 200; **make the diagnosis, as some are treatable**
- **Pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP) is the commonest**
  - Pneumatoceles can rupture, causing pneumothorax and prolonged ventilation
  - CXR may be normal; CT shows bilateral hazy infiltrates
- Tuberculosis, bacterial pneumonia and cavitating disease (e.g. nocardiosis)
- Kaposi sarcoma and lymphoma can involve lungs, airway or mediastinum
- Assess lung function carefully: **CXR** for airspace shadowing; consider arterial blood gas and spirometry
- If GA planned, consider availability of postoperative ventilation

### Cardiovascular
- Long-standing infection often causes **myocarditis**; up to 30% progress to **dilated cardiomyopathy**
  - Causes: Cryptococcus, coxsackie B, CMV, Aspergillus, lymphoma, HIV itself
- Opportunistic bacteria can cause endocarditis or heart failure; vegetations especially in IV drug users
- History, examination, **ECG and possibly echocardiogram**: dysfunction may be clinically silent

### Neurological
- About 90% develop neurological complications
- Early: **peripheral neuropathy** fairly common
- Late: **central demyelination** with **dementia**
- Opportunistic encephalitis or meningitis

### Gastrointestinal
- Diarrhoea, vomiting and anorexia from disease or treatment
- Oral candida makes eating and swallowing painful; CMV and cryptosporidium cause severe diarrhoea
- So **fluid and electrolyte imbalance** is common

### Hepatic
- Check **LFTs**: **raised enzymes are common** on antiretrovirals (especially nevirapine); albumin often low

### Renal
- Acute kidney injury often drug-induced
- End-stage disease from several mechanisms: HIV-associated nephropathy
- Check **creatinine and eGFR**

### Endocrine
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal disturbance is common: high cortisol early, adrenal insufficiency in advanced disease; frank insufficiency is fairly rare

### Haematological
- **FBC**: **pancytopenia**; anaemia, neutropenia or thrombocytopenia common
- **PT and APTT**: some are **hypercoagulable** or have idiopathic thrombotic thrombocytopenia
- Antiretrovirals such as AZT suppress marrow, leading to coagulopathy

### Metabolic
- Blood gas for lactic acidosis if suspected

## Antiretroviral drugs and interactions
- **CD4 count and viral load** from the **previous three months** matter
  - Consider delaying elective surgery if CD4 <200: more postoperative infections
  - CD4 <50: higher 6-month mortality after surgery
- Nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs)
  - e.g. zidovudine (Retrovir, AZT), didanosine (Videx, ddI), stavudine (Zerit, d4T), zalcitabine (Hivid, ddC), lamivudine (Epivir, 3TC), abacavir (Ziagen, ABC)
  - Mostly mild self-limiting effects: fatigue, headache, GI upset
  - **Mitochondrial toxicity**: **pancreatitis**, hepatic steatosis, **lactic acidosis**
  - **Peripheral neuropathy** and **myopathy**
  - Zidovudine: pancytopenia
  - Mainly renally eliminated
- Non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs)
  - e.g. nevirapine (Viramune, NVP), efavirenz (Sustiva, EFV), delavirdine (Rescriptor, DLV)
  - **Rashes**, including Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis
  - **Raised LFTs**
  - **Nevirapine**: used to prevent mother-to-child transmission; strong **cytochrome p450 inducer**
    - Induces its own metabolism; may reduce the effect of midazolam, rifampicin and oral contraceptives
    - Much faster **opioid** metabolism: **synthetic opioids need higher doses**
- Protease inhibitors (PIs)
  - e.g. saquinavir (Invirase, Fortovase, SQV), indinavir (Crixivan, IDV), ritonavir (Norvir, RTV), nelfinavir (Viracept, NFV), amprenavir (Agenerase, APV)
  - **Metabolic effects**: **dyslipidaemia, insulin resistance** with hyperglycaemia, lipodystrophy ("protease paunch"), early atherosclerosis
  - Increase **cardiac toxicity** of amiodarone, quinidine and disopyramide; may worsen digoxin toxicity
  - **Raised LFTs**
  - **Inhibit CYP3A (p450)**
    - **Midazolam** and diazepam effects are **potentiated**: reduce dose; lorazepam probably safer
    - **Alfentanil and fentanyl metabolism much reduced**, with lower clearance
- Others: fusion inhibitors (e.g. enfuvirtide), CCR5 antagonists, integrase inhibitors
- **Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS)**
  - Recovering immunity unmasks a hidden opportunistic infection, which then presents with **unusually aggressive inflammation**
  - **Usually in the first three months of ART**, in advanced HIV
- Other drugs: nephrotoxic (amphotericin B, aciclovir, pentamidine) or causing thrombocytopenia (co-trimoxazole, ketoconazole)
- Comorbidities: IV drug use, hepatitis B

## Preoperative preparation
- **Do not stop HIV medicines while fasting**
  - Interruption raises mortality and adverse events; viral load rises and resistance can emerge and persist
  - **Discuss with ID specialist if oral intake is not possible after surgery**
  - Can be given via gastric or jejunal tubes
- Premedication: **beware interactions with midazolam**

## Intraoperative management
### Preventing transmission
- Staff to patient and back
  - **Universal precautions**: gloves, eye-splash protection
  - **Safe sharps handling**
  - Avoid unnecessary invasive procedures
  - Have an occupational exposure and post-exposure prophylaxis protocol
- **Patient to patient**
  - HIV is killed by heat and chemicals (glutaraldehyde, sodium hypochlorite)
  - **Disposable airway devices, HME filters** and circuits
  - **Clean and decontaminate** other equipment routinely
- Patient is **immunocompromised**: **strict asepsis** throughout

### GA versus regional
- **GA causes immunosuppression** (less natural killer cell, T-cell, monocyte and neutrophil activity) from within 15 minutes of induction, lasting three to eleven days; regional techniques are thought not to
  - The changes are transient, do not increase postoperative infection and are unlikely to matter; not enough evidence to prefer regional on immune grounds alone
- Regional may be chosen to **avoid interactions with antiretroviral or anti-opportunistic drugs**
- **Neurological deficits** are common; pre-existing focal deficit has been suggested as a relative contraindication to regional
- Sepsis and clotting problems are common and are contraindications
- Neuraxial blocks may carry a higher risk of **CNS infection** in these patients

### Drugs
- Induction agents: no change; short, predictable effect set by redistribution, not metabolism
- Volatiles: no change; no known significant ART interactions given lung elimination and little metabolism; less-metabolised agents (e.g. desflurane) may be preferable in theory given possible liver effects
- **Opioids**: complex variable interactions depending on ART; **titrate to effect**
  - **Remifentanil is the opioid of choice**
  - **Morphine is generally safe**
- Suxamethonium: risk of hyperkalaemia if myopathy present
- Atracurium (organ-independent elimination) theoretically safest relaxant

### Transfusion (relative contraindication)
- Allogeneic blood is immunomodulatory; in advanced HIV it may raise viral load (transfusion-related immunomodulation, TRIM)
- **Transfuse only when unavoidable** for safety
- Autotransfusion is contraindicated
- Consider preoperative EPO if heavy blood loss expected
- Epidural blood patch for post-dural puncture headache is not contraindicated

## Postoperative care
- **Restart ART** without interruption

## Children
- Often complex social backgrounds; may not be with biological parents
- Establish (sensitively) who is the legal guardian for consent

## Pain
- **Common in advanced disease**: opportunistic infection (e.g. herpes simplex), HIV arthralgia, peripheral neuropathy, drug-related pain
- Manage **multidisciplinary**, on the same principles as **cancer pain**

## Flashcards
- Q: Below what CD4 count should elective surgery be delayed in HIV, and what does <50 signal?
  - A: CD4 <200 (more infective complications); <50 means higher 6-month mortality
- Q: Should ART be stopped for fasting before surgery?
  - A: No; continue it, via feeding tube if needed; interruption raises mortality and resistance
- Q: How do protease inhibitors affect midazolam and fentanyl?
  - A: They inhibit CYP3A, potentiating midazolam and reducing fentanyl and alfentanil clearance
- Q: Which opioid is of choice in HIV patients on ART?
  - A: Remifentanil (morphine generally safe)
- Q: What proportion of people with HIV develop neurological complications?
  - A: About 90%
