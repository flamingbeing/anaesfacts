# Anaemia

Preoperative anaemia: define it, find the cause, treat before elective surgery (iron, B12, folate, ESA), and plan blood management.

## Summary
- Anaemia: Hb less than 13 (males), less than 11.5 (females)
- Rule out symptomatic anaemia
- Ferritin less than 300 and TSAT less than 20%: IV iron (Ferinject) if surgery is 4 wk or less away, moderate anaemia or bleeding, or absorption problems
- Valid group and crossmatch
- Patient blood management: know the allowable blood loss, minimise blood loss, maintain cardiac output

## Definition
- Male: Hb less than 13
- Female: Hb less than 11.5
- Pregnancy: Hb less than 11

## Preoperative assessment
- Measure Hb before all major surgery, and in anyone with significant history, especially cardiovascular or respiratory disease
- Suspect from history (menorrhagia) or pallor
- Critical or symptomatic: fatigue, breathlessness, angina, palpitations, reduced exercise capacity
  - Admit under the surgeon if a surgical cause is suspected; send to the Emergency Department if not
- Investigations
  - Anaemia panel: iron studies, B12 and folate, reticulocyte count, creatinine
  - Direct Coombs test: detects antibodies on red cells that cause their destruction

## Preoperative optimisation
- Delay elective surgery where possible
- Treat the cause with haematinics: IV or oral iron, B12 injections, folate
- Erythropoietin: useful in dialysis patients
- Transfuse only if indicated: practice is now more conservative, given transfusion morbidity and better tolerance of low Hb than once thought

## Classification
- By cause
  - Reduced production: renal failure, folate or B12 deficiency, marrow infiltration or suppression, anaemia of chronic disease, hypothyroidism
  - Bleeding: acute or chronic
  - Increased consumption or haemolysis
- By red cell size: microcytic, normocytic, macrocytic

## Iron deficiency anaemia
### Diagnosis
- Iron deficiency: TSAT less than 20% and ferritin less than 300 mcg/L
- Ferritin (iron stores)
  - Less than 30 mcg/L: no stores, absolute iron deficiency
  - Less than 100 mcg/L with inflammation: absolute deficiency likely; stores will run out after 500ml blood loss
  - Less than 200 mcg/L in renal failure with inflammation: absolute deficiency likely
  - No defined upper limit, but do not give IV iron if ferritin is above 500 mcg/L
- TSAT (circulating iron) less than 20%: absolute deficiency, or functional deficiency (iron locked in macrophages)
  - Serum iron divided by TIBC (or transferrin): the percentage of transferrin binding sites carrying iron
- Ret-He (reticulocyte haemoglobin equivalent) less than 30pg: iron-restricted red cell production
  - Thalassaemia also lowers it, so it cannot be read in thalassaemia
- Functional iron deficiency: ferritin 100-300 mcg/L with TSAT less than 20%

### Oral iron
- Poor compliance; causes constipation
- Slow: needs 4 weeks to refill stores
- Limited by intolerance or poor absorption

### IV iron (e.g. Ferinject, ferric carboxymaltose)
- Hb rises in 2 weeks
  - If less than 2 weeks to surgery, discuss postponing with the surgeon
  - Never too late to start: correcting before surgery still lowers postoperative transfusion
- Preferred when
  - Moderate to severe anaemia (Hb less than 10 g/dL)
  - Expected moderate (more than 500ml) to high (more than 1000ml) blood loss
  - Surgery 4 weeks or less away, or moderate to severe anaemia after surgery
  - Patient is on erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESA)
  - Inflammation or malignancy (oral iron poorly absorbed)
- Side effects
  - Common: headache, dizziness, nausea and vomiting, metallic taste, muscle and joint aches, permanent brown skin stain if extravasated
  - Uncommon: hypersensitivity, e.g. flushing, itch, rash (less than 1%)
  - Rare: anaphylaxis (less than 0.1%)
- Caution (discuss with haematology)
  - Asthma, atopy, multiple drug allergies
  - Systemic inflammatory disease (e.g. SLE)
  - Systemic infection or bacteraemia
  - Significant liver dysfunction
  - First trimester of pregnancy
  - Children under 14y

## Megaloblastic anaemia (B12 and folate)
- B12 deficiency: B12 less than 200 pmol/L
  - Oral mecobalamin 500 mcg each morning for 1 month
  - IM 1 mg once if less than 150 pmol/L
- Folate deficiency: folate less than 7 nmol/L
  - Oral folic acid 5 mg each morning for 1 month
  - If both are low, start folate after B12 to avoid subacute combined degeneration of the cord

## Anaemia of renal failure
- GFR less than 60: refer to nephrology
- Target Hb 9-10g/dL
- Optimise erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESA)

## Thalassaemia
- Absent or reduced synthesis of alpha or beta globin chains; severity follows the degree of the defect
- Major (transfusion dependent, major iron overload problems), intermediate, minor
- History: anaemia symptoms; Mediterranean, African or Asian origin; organ damage from iron overload; high-output cardiac failure
- Examination: heart failure; marrow overgrowth deforms bone and face, so the airway may be difficult
- Investigations: mild to severe anaemia; Hb electrophoresis with or without globin chain analysis
- Management: severe forms need transfusion; crossmatch early to find antibodies; careful airway assessment

## G6PD deficiency
- Enzyme makes NADPH, which protects cells from oxidative stress (infection, drug oxidation)
- Also needed to reduce methaemoglobin, so sodium nitroprusside and prilocaine are contraindicated
- X-linked
- Haemolysis 2-5d after exposure to a trigger
- History: Black American or Mediterranean origin; fava (broad) beans cause haemolysis; abdominal pain 2 days later
- Examination: anaemia, jaundice
- Investigations: haemoglobinaemia, haemoglobinuria, raised unconjugated bilirubin, Heinz bodies, red cell G6PD assay (may read falsely high during a crisis)
- Management: stop the trigger; transfuse
- Triggers: high-dose aspirin; antibiotics (quinolones, chloramphenicol, isoniazid, sulphonamides); quinine; methylene blue; fava beans
- Perioperative: avoid triggers, including NSAIDs

## Sickle cell disease
- Inherited haemoglobinopathy
  - HbSS (homozygous): sickle cell anaemia
  - HbSA (heterozygous): trait
  - HbS with another beta chain defect: HbSC, HbSD, sickle beta thalassaemia
- Sickling rises with HbS concentration; rare in trait (HbS less than 50%); HbF inhibits sickling
- Endemic in parts of Africa, the Mediterranean, Middle East and India
- Sickled cells block small vessels, causing haemolysis and infarction
- Triggers: hypoxia, hypothermia or fever, acidosis, hypovolaemia, infection
- Highly variable course

### Crises
- Vaso-occlusive (most common): acute abdomen, acute chest syndrome (pneumonia-like), stroke, priapism, proliferative retinopathy
  - Most are functionally asplenic by their teens, so sepsis risk increases
- Aplastic: temporary marrow shutdown; Hb falls steeply with no reticulocytes; triggered by parvovirus with B12 or folate deficiency
- Sequestration: mainly children; sudden pooling of red cells in the spleen causes hypotension and a profound Hb fall; fatal without transfusion
- Haemolytic: Hb falls with raised reticulocytes and bilirubin; usually with vaso-occlusive crises; chronic haemolysis causes gallstones

### Investigations
- Screening test deoxygenates HbS: positive in HbSS and HbSA
- Hb electrophoresis identifies the type
- Hb 60-90, raised reticulocytes
- Film: sickle and target cells; Howell-Jolly bodies if the spleen is atrophic
- All may be normal in trait

### Management
- Supportive: folic acid, vaccinations, penicillin prophylaxis
- Crisis: rest, IV fluids, antibiotics, oxygen, warming, good analgesia
- Transfusion (discuss with haematology first): some vaso-occlusive crises; target HbS less than 30%

### Perioperative management
- Discuss every operation with haematology beforehand
- Avoid hypoxia, dehydration, infection, acidosis, hypothermia and pain
- Regional anaesthesia is not contraindicated
- Tourniquets can be used if the limb is carefully exsanguinated first

## Flashcards
- Q: Hb thresholds defining anaemia?
  - A: Less than 13 in males, less than 11.5 in females, less than 11 in pregnancy
- Q: Ferritin level meaning absent iron stores, and above which IV iron should not be given?
  - A: Less than 30 mcg/L; above 500 mcg/L
- Q: How soon does Hb rise after IV iron?
  - A: In 2 weeks; if less than 2 weeks to surgery, discuss postponing
- Q: B12 replacement doses?
  - A: Oral mecobalamin 500 mcg daily for 1 month; IM 1 mg once if B12 less than 150 pmol/L
- Q: Transfusion target HbS in sickle cell disease?
  - A: HbS less than 30%
