# Atrial fibrillation

Proceed with AF if rate is under 110, asymptomatic and no heart failure; continue rate control, plan anticoagulation, keep K 4-5 and Mg above 1.

## Key points
- Cause: may need treating (e.g. electrolytes, sepsis)
- Criticality: unstable AF; HR above 110 or symptoms
- Complications: myocardial ischaemia, more perioperative cardiac events; embolism and stroke
- Drugs: rate control; anticoagulation (consider bridging); electrolytes

## Preoperative assessment
- Stable or unstable
  - Check vital signs for haemodynamic compromise needing DC cardioversion
  - ACLS approach: cardiovert if unstable
- Fitness for surgery
  - New AF: find the cause and control the rate
    - Delay elective surgery for work-up; exclude acute causes, e.g. thyrotoxicosis, chest infection
    - Refer to cardiology
  - HR > 110, or symptoms (e.g. heart failure, or a change in cardiovascular status or effort tolerance)
    - Optimise further (titrate beta blockers, correct electrolytes)
    - Refer to cardiology
  - Proceed if HR < 110, asymptomatic, no heart failure

## Preoperative optimisation
- Continue rate control perioperatively
- Anticoagulation
  - Assess thrombotic risk with CHA2DS2-VASc
  - Bridge with LMWH if high risk; refer to haematology
- Keep K 4-5 and Mg > 1 (check recent renal panel and calcium, magnesium, phosphate)

## Intraoperative management
- NIBP may be inaccurate: consider an arterial line
- Slow, titrated induction
- Avoid sympathetic stimulation; keep HR < 110 (remifentanil, good analgesia)
- Maintain preload, afterload and coronary perfusion
- Regular ABG; replace electrolytes
- AF during surgery
  - Magnesium; replace K
  - Beta blocker or amiodarone
  - Correct triggers (e.g. fluids); check ECG

## Postoperative management
- Start or restart anticoagulation if high-risk AF (CHA2DS2-VASc)

## New postoperative AF
- Up to 45% after cardiac surgery, up to 30% after non-cardiac thoracic surgery, up to 8% after other major surgery
- Usually within the first 5 postoperative days
- Linked to longer stays and higher mortality
- Most resolve within a few months

### Causes
- Postoperative state: high adrenergic tone, magnesium depletion, oxidative stress
- Cardiovascular: hypovolaemia (e.g. bleeding), sepsis, ischaemic heart disease, valve disease, cardiomyopathy, pre-excitation (e.g. Wolff-Parkinson-White), sinus node disease
  - Tests: fluid status, 12 lead ECG, echo, stress testing, Holter (72 hr ECG)
- Respiratory: hypoxia, pneumonia, effusion, thromboembolism
  - Tests: pulse oximetry, ABG
- Electrolytes: low or high K+, low Mg 2+, low Ca2+
  - Tests: ABG, formal serum electrolytes
- Metabolic: acidosis, alcohol excess, thyrotoxicosis, diabetes
  - Tests: ABG, thyroid function, liver function, glucose, toxicology screen

### Acute management
- ABC, call for help if unstable, supplemental O2; DC cardioversion if haemodynamically unstable
- Assess: chest tightness, breathlessness, syncope; cardiorespiratory examination; drains for blood loss
- Investigations: ABG; FBC, renal panel, Ca, Mg, PO4; 12 lead ECG and cardiac enzymes; septic work-up
- Treat the cause
  - Electrolytes: correct
  - Hypovolaemia: fluids or blood products
  - Hypoxia: oxygen
  - Sepsis: antibiotics
  - Myocardial infarction: cardiology review
  - Pain: analgesia
- Lines: invasive monitoring if unstable; central access for vasopressors or inotropes
- Rate control first if stable: magnesium and K replacement, amiodarone, beta blockers, calcium channel blockers (e.g. verapamil), digoxin
- Disposition: HDU or ICU; cardiology review; consider anticoagulation, weighing surgical bleeding against embolic risk

### Prevention
- Magnesium reduces post-surgical AF in RCTs (odds ratio 0.55)
- Beta blockers
- N-acetylcysteine (antioxidant, glutathione precursor) is effective prophylaxis after cardiac surgery
