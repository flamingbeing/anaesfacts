# Epidermolysis Bullosa

Group of inherited conditions where skin and mucosa blister and shear with minimal trauma; anaesthesia aims to avoid all friction, pressure and adhesives.

## Types
- Three main types, each with subtypes
- Epidermolysis bullosa simplex
  - Most common; autosomal dominant
  - Non-scarring intra-epidermal blisters on arms, legs, hands, feet, scalp, palms and soles
  - May have mild mucosal blistering (nose, mouth, throat)
  - Tends to improve with age
- Epidermolysis bullosa letalis
  - Severe; autosomal recessive
  - Blisters in the lamina lucida
  - Whole skin affected, plus respiratory and gastrointestinal tract blistering
  - Rarely survive infancy; often die of septicaemia
- Dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa
  - Two types, both from COL7A1 mutations (type VII collagen, which anchors the lamina densa to the superficial dermis)
  - Autosomal dominant form milder than recessive
  - Recessive form (from birth or infancy):
    - Widespread atrophic scarring blisters: limb contractures, fused digits, mouth contractures
    - Oesophageal blistering: strictures, feeding problems, malnutrition, iron deficiency anaemia
    - Strictures raise risk of reflux, regurgitation and aspiration
    - Dystrophic nails, malformed teeth, sparse hair, corneal ulcers
    - More squamous cell carcinoma, porphyria and amyloidosis

## Common operations
- Plastics: syndactyly release, dressing changes
- Squamous cell carcinoma excision with split skin graft
- Dental surgery
- Oesophageal dilatation

## Anaesthetic management
- Minimise any pressure or shearing
- Airway
  - Pad face mask and chin-support points with Vaseline gauze to prevent facial bullae
  - Lubricated laryngoscope blade and a smaller ETT than usual
  - Cricoid pressure not contraindicated; apply evenly
  - Oropharyngeal suction can cause life-threatening bullae
  - Badly placed oropharyngeal airways commonly cause mouth bullae
  - LMA used successfully (one case report of a lingual bulla): smaller size, shaft wrapped in gauze to protect lips, remove under deep anaesthesia
  - Atropine or glycopyrronium helps dry secretions
- Venous access
  - Often difficult from scarring
  - Secure cannulae with Vaseline gauze and a crepe bandage
  - Central line may be needed; suture it in
- Positioning
  - Let the patient position themselves on the table where possible
  - Avoid friction from sliding on sheets
- Monitoring
  - Pulse oximeter on finger or ear lobe
  - No adhesive ECG pads: place petroleum jelly gauze between leads and skin
  - Extra gauze under the BP cuff
- No adhesive tape
  - Tie ETT or LMA in place
  - Lubricating eye ointment instead of tape to protect the cornea
- Antacid prophylaxis if reflux or oesophageal stricture
- Long-term steroids: may need perioperative hydrocortisone cover
- Regional anaesthesia avoids many problems
  - Brachial plexus block for hand surgery
  - Neuraxial blocks if indicated
  - Topical anaesthesia to skin graft donor sites

## Postoperative analgesia
- IV, rectal and regional all acceptable
- Oral preferred when possible, as liquid or effervescent forms (swallowing difficulty)

## Flashcards
- Q: How is an ETT or LMA secured in epidermolysis bullosa?
  - A: Tied in, never taped.
- Q: Which airway manoeuvre can cause life-threatening bullae in epidermolysis bullosa?
  - A: Oropharyngeal suctioning.
- Q: Is cricoid pressure contraindicated in epidermolysis bullosa?
  - A: No; apply it evenly.
- Q: How should an LMA be used in epidermolysis bullosa?
  - A: Smaller size, shaft wrapped in gauze, removed under deep anaesthesia.
- Q: Which gene is mutated in dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa?
  - A: COL7A1 (type VII collagen).
