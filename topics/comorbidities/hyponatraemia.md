# Hyponatraemia and Hypertonic Saline

Serum sodium below 135 mEq/L; usually a hypotonic state of excess free water relative to sodium; correct carefully to avoid osmotic demyelination.

## Definition and epidemiology
- Serum [Na+] <135 mEq/L
- Seen in 40–50% of ICU patients; especially common in neurosurgical patients
- Usually hypotonic, but can be:
  - Isotonic: pseudohyponatraemia
  - Hypertonic: hyperglycaemia (e.g. non-ketotic hyperglycaemia)
- In hyperglycaemia, correct the measured sodium for the dilutional effect of glucose

## Clinical features
- Depend on severity and, more importantly, on speed of onset
  - Acute (<24-48 h): more likely symptomatic
  - Chronic (>24-48 h): less likely symptomatic because the brain adapts
    - Adaptation: brain volume normalises by losing electrolytes (within hours) and organic osmolytes (within days)
    - This adaptation is what makes overly rapid correction dangerous
- Most patients with sodium greater than 125 mmol/l, or with chronic hyponatraemia, have no neurological symptoms
- Neurological symptoms dominate (cerebral oedema): headache, nausea, malaise, lethargy, weakness, cramps, anorexia, somnolence, disorientation, personality change, reduced reflexes, falling conscious level

## Complications
- Encephalopathy: seizures, coma, respiratory arrest, permanent brain damage, brainstem herniation, death
  - Risk and severity greater with acute (<48 hrs) hyponatraemia
- Treatment complication: osmotic demyelination syndrome from rapid correction (pontine and extrapontine)

## Causes by tonicity
- Isotonic: pseudohyponatraemia, a lab artefact with severe hyperlipidaemia or paraproteinaemia (e.g. multiple myeloma)
- Hypertonic: hyperglycaemia
- Hypotonic: excess free water relative to sodium; classify by clinical extracellular volume

## Causes of hypotonic hyponatraemia
- Hypervolaemic (fluid overload): sodium and water retained, water more than sodium
  - Congestive cardiac failure
  - Renal impairment: AKI, CKD
  - Cirrhosis and ascites
  - Pregnancy
- Euvolaemic (water overload or ADH-related): excess water intake, or water retention from non-osmotic ADH release
  - Urine osmolality >100
    - SIADH (normal urine sodium): the leading euvolaemic cause
    - Adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism (may also be hypovolaemic)
  - Urine osmolality <100
    - Psychogenic polydipsia
    - Low solute intake ("tea and toast")
- Hypovolaemic (salt loss): sodium loss lowers extracellular volume; baroreceptor-driven (non-osmotic) ADH release plus free water intake lowers the sodium
  - Urine sodium >20 (renal losses)
    - Cerebral salt wasting
    - Diuretics (especially thiazides; mannitol in its early phase)
    - Nephropathies
    - Aldosterone or mineralocorticoid deficiency
  - Urine sodium <10 and FeNa <1% (extrarenal losses)
    - Diarrhoea, vomiting
    - Excessive sweating
    - Third spacing (e.g. peritonitis, pancreatitis, burns)

## Investigations
- Renal panel with glucose (bedside glucose) to confirm the diagnosis
  - Source of sodium loss is usually clinically obvious
- Spot urine sodium separates renal from extrarenal loss in hypovolaemia
  - High (>20 mEq/L): renal loss
  - Low (<20 mEq/L): extrarenal loss
- Euvolaemic patients
  - Paired serum and urine osmolality
    - ADH disorders: urine osmolality >100 mosm/kg H2O; excess water intake: <100 mosm/kg H2O
  - Thyroid function, 8am cortisol
  - Look for causes of SIADH
    - CXR, possibly CT chest if a pulmonary cause is suspected (e.g. small cell lung cancer)
    - CT head if a CNS cause is suspected

## Specific causes
- SIADH
  - Non-osmotic ADH release with various malignancies, infections and drugs
  - Hallmarks: euvolaemia, hypotonic plasma, inappropriately concentrated urine (osmolality >100 mosm/kg H2O), high urine sodium (>20 mEq/L)
- Adrenal insufficiency
  - Primary: mineralocorticoid deficiency causes renal sodium loss and hypovolaemia
  - Secondary (hypothalamic): mainly glucocorticoid deficiency; no renal sodium loss
- Cerebral salt wasting
  - Follows traumatic brain injury, subarachnoid haemorrhage and neurosurgery; mechanism of renal sodium loss unclear

## Pseudohyponatraemia
- Flame photometry measures sodium across the whole plasma, but sodium sits only in the water phase
  - Plasma is about 93% water, so normally the error is small (about 7%)
- Large rises in lipid or protein expand the non-water phase and lower the measured sodium; true (water-phase) sodium is unchanged
  - Usually needs lipids above 1,500 mg/dL or protein above 12–15 g/dL
- Diagnosis
  - Plasma osmolality: normal in pseudohyponatraemia, low in true hypotonic hyponatraemia
  - Ion-selective electrode measures water-phase sodium: normal in pseudohyponatraemia

## Approach to severe hyponatraemia
- Exclude a sampling error (especially a well patient with a very low sodium)
- Exclude pseudohyponatraemia and hyperglycaemia (also hyperproteinaemia, hyperlipidaemia)
- Decide whether symptoms are due to the hyponatraemia
- Decide whether it is acute or chronic: this sets the safe correction rate
- At the bedside
  - Urgent capillary glucose
  - Conscious level and neurological status
  - History of fluid intake and losses
  - Extracellular volume status
  - Drugs that cause hyponatraemia (e.g. diuretics)
- Emergency orders
  - Hypertonic or normal saline over a set time
  - Repeat urea, electrolytes and creatinine at a set time
  - Conscious level charting
  - Plasma osmolality, urine osmolality, urine sodium
  - Thyroid function and assessment for hypocortisolism
  - Treat the underlying cause

## Management by extracellular volume
- Always check volume status: do not give NaCl if already overloaded; furosemide is almost never right if volume-depleted
- Low extracellular volume
  - Symptomatic: 3% NaCl using the rapid correction rates below
  - Asymptomatic: isotonic saline until signs of hypovolaemia resolve
- Normal extracellular volume
  - Symptomatic: 3% NaCl; add IV furosemide (20–40 mg) if volume overload is a concern (e.g. heart failure)
  - Asymptomatic: fluid restrict to 500 mL below daily urine output; if ineffective or not tolerated, consider SIADH drug therapy
- High extracellular volume
  - No guidelines
  - Severe symptoms: hypertonic saline combined with furosemide diuresis
  - Asymptomatic: fluid restriction and furosemide diuresis
- Treat the cause (restore volume, stop offending drugs, treat pain and nausea)

## Treatment options
- Increase sodium intake
  - Oral NaCl tablets
  - IV 0.9% NaCl for asymptomatic patients; starting at 60mls/hr (about 1.5L/day) may be appropriate
  - IV 3% NaCl for symptomatic patients
- Predicting the effect of 1 L of any infusate (Adrogue-Madias formula)
  - Change in serum [Na+] = (infusate [Na+] minus serum [Na+]) / (TBW + 1 L)
  - TBW = 0.6 x weight (kg) in men, 0.5 x weight (kg) in women
  - Assumes no water or electrolyte losses
- Restrict free water and promote water loss
  - Fluid restrict to <1 L/d
  - Furosemide
  - V2 receptor antagonists (e.g. tolvaptan)
  - Oral urea (osmotic aquaresis)
- Monitor closely so correction is not too fast
  - Serum sodium hourly or every 2hrs, then every 4 to 6 hours (ABG may be needed)
  - Urine output and urine tonicity: a high output of dilute urine is the first sign of dangerously rapid correction

## Rates of correction
- Symptomatic (seizures or reduced conscious level)
  - 1-2 mEq/L per hr for 4-6 h initially, but total rise no more than 8 mEq/L in 24 h
  - Aim only to stop symptoms (e.g. seizures) or reach >120mmol/l to avoid cerebral oedema
  - This is the indication for 3% NaCl
  - Stop the rapid phase when sodium reaches 120 mEq/L
- Definitely acute (<48hrs), asymptomatic: treatment depends on severity
  - Acute means developed over <48 h
  - A marked fall in sodium is treated as symptomatic
  - Rapid correction is less risky because adaptation is incomplete
- Chronic (>48hrs) or unknown, asymptomatic: 0.5 mEq/L per hr or less (e.g. 8mmol in 24hrs)
  - Limits: rise less than 12 mmol/l in the first 24 hours and less than 18 mmol/l in the first 48 hours
- Overly rapid correction
  - Give water (IV D5W)
  - Give desmopressin (DDAVP) 1-2 mcg IV to stop the water diuresis

## Osmotic demyelination syndrome
- Also called central pontine myelinolysis
- Caused by rapid correction (>10–12 mEq/L in 24 hrs)
- Features: dysarthria, quadriparesis, loss of consciousness
- Risk factors
  - Rapid rise in sodium
  - Chronic hyponatraemia (>48hrs)
  - Coexisting hypokalaemia
  - Large volumes of isotonic fluid in hypovolaemic hyponatraemia: correcting hypovolaemia suddenly removes the ADH drive, causing a brisk water diuresis and a fast rise in sodium
  - Inaccurate assessment of extracellular volume status
- Prevention
  - Symptomatic: stop rapid correction at 120 mEq/L
  - Acute: may raise by 4–6 mEq/L in the first 1–2 hrs, but final sodium should not exceed 120 mEq/L
  - Chronic: no faster than 0.5 mEq/L per hour (or 10–12 mEq/L in 24 hours)

## Hypertonic saline (3% NaCl)
- Ideally use a central line if multiple doses are needed
- Rate: 1-2 ml/kg/h
- In practice: boluses of 100 to 150 mls of 3% saline, repeated as needed
- Alternative: initial rate (mL/hr) = weight (kg) x desired rise in sodium per hour
  - E.g. 70 kg, target 0.5 mEq/L per hour: 70 x 0.5 = 35 mL/hr
  - Check sodium periodically until the target (120 mEq/L) is reached
- Alternative: 1ml/min (60ml/hr) raises sodium by 1mmol/hr (or possibly 1ml/kg/hr)
- Alternative: 1ml/kg raises sodium by about 1mmol/L (one source says 0.5mmol/L in children)
- Furosemide may be needed for volume overload

## Flashcards
- Q: Maximum correction for chronic hyponatraemia (per hour and per 24 and 48 hours)?
  - A: 0.5 mEq/L per hr or less; less than 12 mmol/l in 24 hours and less than 18 mmol/l in 48 hours
- Q: Initial correction for symptomatic hyponatraemia?
  - A: 3% NaCl, 1-2 mEq/L per hr for 4-6 h; stop rapid phase at 120 mEq/L; total no more than 8 mEq/L in 24 h
- Q: Effect of 1 ml/kg of 3% NaCl on serum sodium?
  - A: Raises it by about 1 mmol/L
- Q: How to treat overly rapid correction?
  - A: IV D5W and DDAVP 1-2 mcg IV
- Q: Urine sodium cut-off separating renal from extrarenal loss?
  - A: Above 20 mEq/L renal; below 20 mEq/L extrarenal
