# Burns

Treat as trauma: secure the airway early if inhalational injury, resuscitate without over-filling, and expect altered drug responses (no suxamethonium after 24 hours, relaxant resistance, opioid tolerance).

## Roles of the anaesthetist
- Initial resuscitation and airway management
- GA for debridement, dressings, skin grafting and escharotomy
- Pain management
- Burns ICU

## Initial resuscitation summary
- Stabilise ABC, then assess severity
  - %BSA (rule of 9), depth (colour, blanching, blisters, sensation), Baux score (age plus %BSA), need for burns unit
  - Inhalational injury risk: history, ABG, nasendoscopy
- Airway (inhalational injury)
  - Possible difficult airway from swelling; may need awake fibreoptic intubation
  - Intubate early
  - Smaller tube; do not cut it
  - RSI; suxamethonium is not contraindicated in the first 24 hours
- Breathing (inhalational injury)
  - Bronchospasm, secretions
  - Lung-protective ventilation
- Circulation: burn shock from capillary leak
  - Fluid resuscitation: Parkland or modified Brooke (ABLS 2018)
  - Consider albumin for capillary leak
- Disability: toxic gases
  - Altered mental state or hypoxia: COHb >10% needs 100% O2; consider cyanide toxicity
- Exposure: rhabdomyolysis with hyperkalaemia and AKI
- Monitor placement and IV access can be hard
- Secondary survey for other injuries; C-spine control
- Surgery: may need emergency operation; keep intubated afterwards

## ICU summary
- Airway: early intubation for inhalational injury; steroids to reduce oedema
- Breathing: ARDS strategy, chest physiotherapy and pulmonary toilet, bronchodilators, mucolytics
- Circulation: Parkland or modified Brooke (ABLS 2018)
  - Urine output 0.5-1ml/kg/hr; lactate
  - Avoid fluid creep
  - Burn shock first, then hyperdynamic phase
- Pain control
- Nutrition: hypermetabolic state
- Infection: antibiotics
- Stress ulcer and DVT prophylaxis

## Pain management summary
- Opioid tolerance; PCA
- Ketamine

## Repeat debridement summary
- Patient
  - Transfer of a critically ill patient
  - Airway: scarring
  - Breathing: ventilation needs; lung-protective
  - Circulation: confirm resuscitated; sepsis or SIRS
  - Monitoring: ECG on burnt chest, BP cuff and SpO2 probe on burnt limbs may be hard; consider arterial line
  - Difficult IV access
  - Drugs: suxamethonium contraindicated after the first 24hrs; relaxant resistance; altered pharmacokinetics
- Surgery
  - Fluid shifts; blood loss (debrided tissue bleeds freely)
  - Heat loss: warm theatre
  - Pain: opioid tolerance, add ketamine
  - Positioning, possibly prone; airway access with facial burns
  - Burns theatre may be remote
- Counts as trauma: some use RSI
- Antibiotics: some consultants prefer co-amoxiclav (Augmentin), others cefazolin

## Pathophysiology
- Manage as for any trauma patient (ATLS); do not let the burn distract from a full sequential survey
- Pay special attention to inhalational injury
- Two systemic phases
  - Burn shock (ebb) phase
    - Intravascular fluid lost into inflamed burns and oedema in unburnt tissue
    - Raised SVR (catecholamines, ADH, haemoconcentration) and depressed cardiac output
  - Hypermetabolic (flow) phase, developing 48 to 72 h after injury
    - Higher oxygen use, CO2 production and protein wasting
    - Cardiac output often two to three times normal; low SVR
    - Sepsis raises output further and lowers SVR

## History and burn assessment
- Mechanism
- Depth (may evolve)
  - Epidermal: erythema
  - Superficial dermal: blisters, pale pink
  - Mid dermal: dark pink, sluggish capillary refill
  - Deep dermal: blotchy red and white, sluggish or absent refill
  - Full thickness: white, brown or black; no capillary refill, no sensation
- Extent (%TBSA); first-degree burns excluded
  - Wallace rule of nines
  - Lund and Browder chart
  - Palmar method: patient's hand (palm and fingers)
- Likelihood of inhalational injury
- Likelihood of other injuries

## Airway management
- Inhalational injury risk
  - Airway swelling: intubate before oedema develops
    - Difficult airway set-up: trolley, videolaryngoscope and aids, trained team, range of smaller tubes, landmarks marked, ENT scrubbed for surgical airway
    - Do not cut the tube: swelling can make it too short; the extra dead space matters little
  - Head up 30-45 degrees for drainage
  - Pulmonary oedema and ARDS: lung-protective ventilation
  - Limit IV fluid before the airway is secured (swelling, ARDS)
- Higher risk of failed intubation: expect laryngeal oedema, smaller tubes, possible surgical airway
  - Awake intubation is ideal
  - RSI if the patient cannot cooperate
  - Trauma history: manual in-line stabilisation of the C-spine
- Avoid suxamethonium >24hrs after the burn
  - Relevant for late presenters and transfers from other centres

## Inhalational injury
- Three types
  - Direct thermal injury to the upper airway
  - Chemical irritation of the subglottic airway and alveoli
  - Systemic poisoning from combustion products
- Usually with face, neck and chest burns and large %TBSA, but can occur without skin burns
- Suggestive history
  - Fire or explosion in an enclosed space, especially with delayed escape
  - Direct facial injury from steam, chemicals or fire
  - Singed nasal or facial hair
  - Soot in mouth or nose

### Upper airway injury
- Swelling may take hours to develop, faster with fluid resuscitation
- Thermal injury usually stays in the supraglottis: dry air holds little heat and the upper airway dissipates it well
  - Causes upper airway oedema and obstruction
  - Epiglottic injury can mimic epiglottitis
- Signs: oropharyngeal oedema, facial swelling, hoarseness, stridor, obstruction, drooling
- Later scarring can limit mouth opening and neck extension

### Lower airway injury
- Below the glottis, injury is mostly chemical (direct and inflammatory), causing pulmonary oedema
  - Exception: steam has a high heat capacity and can burn the lower airway and lung
- Main changes
  - ARDS with non-cardiogenic oedema from capillary leak
  - Bronchospasm from airway hyperreactivity
  - Bronchorrhoea and poor sputum clearance
  - Pneumonia from ciliary dysfunction
  - Bronchial obstruction and atelectasis (surfactant loss, soot)
- Signs: dyspnoea, cough, wheeze, copious secretions
- Investigation: CXR
- Later: lung fibrosis and restrictive disease

### Poisoning
- Carbon monoxide
  - Suspect in any unconscious patient
  - Nausea, vomiting, headache, hypotension, seizures, coma
  - Diagnose by ABG co-oximetry
  - High-flow 100% oxygen via non-rebreather mask for all patients
    - 100% O2 cuts CO elimination half-life from 4 h to under 1 h
    - Hyperbaric O2 at 3 atm cuts it to under 30 min, but rarely practical
- Cyanide: released when nitrogen-containing materials burn

### Early intubation
- Intubate early, before fluid resuscitation worsens oedema (which may take hours)
- Early airway compromise is nearly always from low GCS, not the burn
- Consider early intubation for
  - Hypoxaemia, hypercapnia, GCS <8
  - Deep facial or full-thickness neck burns
  - Singed facial hair, soot deposits
  - Oropharyngeal oedema, stridor

### Airway assessment and extubation
- Fibreoptic nasolaryngoscopy under LA: soot, erythema or swelling of pharynx, aryepiglottic folds and cords
- Fibreoptic bronchoscopy confirms lower airway injury: soot, oedema, erythema, haemorrhage, ulceration
  - Lavage with 1.4% bicarbonate has been used to neutralise acid deposits and clear soot
- Extubation
  - Use a positive cuff leak test to plan timing
  - Short dexamethasone course may reduce swelling
  - Airway exchange catheter adds safety

## Breathing
- Inhalational injury, including systemic effects
- ARDS from major skin burns
- Circumferential deep dermal or full-thickness chest burns restrict chest movement
  - Escharotomy may be needed; rarely before reaching a burns service
- Blast injury
  - Penetrating injury: tension pneumothorax
  - Blast lung: contusion and alveolar injury, leading to ARDS

## Circulation
- Cannulate through unburnt skin; usual sites may be unavailable, so use unusual peripheral or central veins
  - Groins usually spared: femoral access often possible
- Burns do not cause immediate hypovolaemia: shock early means bleeding elsewhere

## Fluid resuscitation
- Major burns cause hypovolaemia through capillary leak
- If in shock: 20ml/kg IV crystalloid
- Starting rate
  - Parkland: 4 ml/kg/(%burn)
  - American Burn Association now advises starting at 2 ml x weight in kg x %TBSA for second- and third-degree burns (less over-resuscitation)
- Formulas give a starting point only: catheterise and titrate to urine output
  - 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults; 1 mL/kg/hr in children under 30 kg (ATLS)
- Hartmann's solution is the fluid of choice
- Target urine output 0.5-1ml/kg
- Avoid fluid creep (over-resuscitation), which causes
  - Excess burn oedema
  - Abdominal compartment syndrome
  - Fasciotomies on unburnt limbs
  - Pulmonary oedema
  - Longer ventilation

## Electrolytes
- Low potassium, phosphate, calcium and magnesium are common: treat

## Exposure and temperature
- Examine fully, including the back (log roll), to estimate %TBSA and depth
- Patients cool quickly: cover and warm early

## Inflammation and hypermetabolism
- TBSA >20%: systemic response and hypermetabolic state
- Metabolic rate rises with burn size and infection, peaking at 7–10 days; can last 1-2 yr
- SIRS

## Infection
- Sepsis sources: eyes (infected corneal burns), pneumonia, burn wound, urinary tract (catheters, perineal burns), sinuses and middle ear (nasogastric tubes), endocarditis

## Other complications
- Muscle breakdown, myoglobinuria, AKI
- DIC
- DVT and PE
- Stress ulcers: early enteral feeding or PPI
- Nutrition: early enteral feeding improves survival; high-protein, high-carbohydrate feeds prevent protein loss, poor healing, immunosuppression and infection

## Disposition
- Every hospital resuscitates; complex burns go to a burns unit

## Anaesthesia for debridement and grafting
- Trend to earlier excision, with skin substitutes when donor skin is short

### Preoperative assessment
- Many repeat anaesthetics for dressings and grafts
  - Consider anxiolytic premedication in children
  - Review the response to the last anaesthetic; expect pain to escalate

### Intraoperative management
- Plan depends on %TBSA, systemic illness and area to be debrided and grafted
- Airway as above
- Monitoring
  - Access to chest (ECG), arms (arterial pressure) and digits (pulse oximetry) may be limited
  - ECG via skin staples or subcutaneous needles with crocodile clips
  - Pulse oximetry on nose, lips or tongue
  - Arterial line essential for major excisions; cardiac output monitor useful
- Heat loss
  - Large heat loss from major scrub-downs
  - Applying large dressings takes time: plan for temperature control
- Haemodynamics
  - Hypermetabolic, SIRS-like circulation; may be unstable and on vasoactive drugs
  - Blood loss can be large and insidious: plan 50–100 ml per % area excised
  - Adrenaline-soaked swabs reduce bleeding
- Positioning may need to be prone
- Burns theatre may be remote

### Neuromuscular blockers
- Suxamethonium
  - Safe in the first 24 h; contraindicated after 24hrs (exaggerated hyperkalaemia)
  - Risk persists up to 1 year after the burn
  - Cause: more extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors releasing potassium on depolarisation (as after denervation), plus immature receptor isoform
- Non-depolarising relaxants: resistance
  - From about 3 to 7 days after injury (lasting to >60 days), doses may need to be much higher
  - Causes
    - (a) Receptor up-regulation, including fetal and alpha7 (neuronal) receptors
    - (b) More binding to alpha1-acid glycoprotein (AAG); faster renal and hepatic elimination
  - Rocuronium 1.2 to 1.5 mg/kg suggested for RSI in major burns
    - Even at 1.5 mg/kg, onset is about 90 seconds, versus under 60 s with 0.9 mg/kg in unburnt patients
  - Atracurium (organ-independent Hofmann elimination) is also less effective: suggests a pharmacodynamic cause

### Pharmacokinetics
- Volume of distribution increases
- Clearance may fall early and rise in the hyperdynamic phase
- Propofol: higher clearance and volume of distribution in the hyperdynamic phase, plus central changes, reduce hypnotic effect
  - May need larger boluses or faster infusions
- Binding proteins change in opposite directions
  - Albumin (binds acidic and neutral drugs) falls
  - AAG (acute-phase protein) rises twofold or more; binds cationic drugs (lidocaine, propranolol, muscle relaxants, some opioids), lowering their free fraction

### Analgesia
- Hard to manage; needs often escalate
- Full-thickness burns are painless, but mixed depth is usual; patient may already be on IV morphine
  - Continue existing sedative and opioid infusions in theatre; increase them or add other drugs
- Opioid tolerance: larger doses
- Ketamine helps acute perioperative pain alongside the usual ladder
  - Bolus ketamine can cause hypotension: chronic catecholamine excess down-regulates beta receptors, unmasking ketamine's direct myocardial depression
- Regional anaesthesia has a role, limited by large areas and infection risk

## Flashcards
- Q: When is suxamethonium contraindicated after a burn, and for how long?
  - A: After the first 24 hours, for up to 1 year.
- Q: What rocuronium dose is suggested for RSI in major burns?
  - A: 1.2 to 1.5 mg/kg (onset still about 90 seconds).
- Q: What are the Parkland and ABA starting fluid rates?
  - A: Parkland 4 ml/kg/%burn; ABA 2 ml/kg/%TBSA, titrated to urine output.
- Q: What urine output targets guide burns resuscitation?
  - A: 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults; 1 mL/kg/hr in children under 30 kg.
- Q: What blood loss should be planned for burn excision?
  - A: 50–100 ml per % area excised.
