# Renal Failure in Children

Acute or chronic loss of renal solute excretion, with fluid, potassium, acid-base, haematological and drug-handling problems that shape the anaesthetic.

## Definitions and causes

- Kidneys cannot excrete the normal solute load: nitrogenous waste builds up, fluid is retained, electrolytes are deranged
- Acute renal failure (ARF)
  - Often presents with oliguria: < 300mL/m2/24 hours, or < 0.5 mL/kg/hour in infants
  - Reflects reduced renal perfusion and GFR
  - Features: oliguria, oedema, hypertension, vomiting, lethargy, electrolyte disturbance, metabolic acidosis
- Causes vary with age
  - Neonates: perinatal asphyxia commonest
  - Infants and older children: haemolytic uraemic syndrome commonest in developed countries; gastroenteritis in developing countries
- Urine tests separate prerenal from renal causes
  - Prerenal: osmolality > 500 mosmol/L, urinary Na < 20 mmol/L
  - Renal: osmolality < 350 mosmol/L, urinary Na > 40 mmol/L
- Chronic renal failure (CRF): develops over months to years
  - Vague presentation: lethargy, fatigue, headache, anorexia, vomiting, poor growth, peripheral oedema, polyuria or enuresis
  - Causes: chronic glomerulonephritis, pyelonephritis, renal tract malformations, inherited disease (Alport syndrome, polycystic kidneys), haemolytic uraemic syndrome

## Clinical features

### Fluids, electrolytes and acid-base
- Sodium may be high, low or normal depending on cause
- Glomerulonephritis: salt and water retention, oedema, hypertension, tendency to heart failure; restrict salt
- Congenital disease and obstructive uropathy: poor concentrating ability, polyuria, salt loss, risk of hypovolaemia; give salt supplements and extra fluid
- Hyperkalaemia is an emergency
  - ECG: peaked T waves, smaller R waves, wide QRS, small or absent P waves
  - Can progress to AV block, VT and cardiac arrest
  - Acidosis worsens it (intracellular potassium swaps with extracellular hydrogen ions)
  - Drugs that raise potassium: ACE inhibitors, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, beta-blockers, aminoglycosides, some anti-neoplastics (ciclosporin)
  - Watch potassium from diet, parenteral nutrition and IV fluids
- CRF: poor hydrogen ion excretion; bicarbonate, bone phosphate and proteins buffer until used up, then metabolic acidosis develops
- Also: low ionised calcium, high phosphate, high magnesium

### Haematological
- Normochromic normocytic anaemia: less erythropoietin and shorter red cell survival (uraemia)
  - Worsened by upper GI blood loss, bleeding tendency, poor iron and folate intake
- Hb often about 8 g/dL; can be allowed to 6 g/dL if asymptomatic
- Symptomatic: recombinant erythropoietin
- Avoid transfusion where possible: volume overload, hypertension, heart failure
- Uraemic platelet dysfunction (poor aggregation and adhesion)
  - Prolonged bleeding time before surgery, or excess bleeding during it
  - Treat intraoperative bleeding with desmopressin 300 nanograms/kg or pooled cryoprecipitate
  - Other clotting tests usually normal

### Cardiovascular
- Hypertension common in CRF: salt and water overload or abnormal renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system
- Fluid overload, hypertension and anaemia can all cause congestive heart failure
- Arteriovenous (AV) fistula for haemodialysis, usually in the arm
  - Pad and wrap in gamgee to protect and mark it
  - No cannulas or arterial lines in that arm; no BP cuff; do not let the patient lie on it
  - Use leg or hand-dorsum veins; save antecubital veins for future fistulas
- Previous dialysis central lines may make those sites hard to use again

### Gastrointestinal
- Uraemia causes anorexia, nausea and vomiting
- Slow gastric emptying, more acid, more peptic ulceration
- Reflux in up to 70%
- ARF is hypercatabolic: give enough calories (mainly carbohydrate and fat such as Intralipid), electrolytes and fluid
- Protein not restricted in children; may be increased on dialysis

### Immunity
- Reduced by uraemia and immunosuppressants; strict asepsis for procedures

### CNS
- Reduced consciousness or seizures from uraemia, electrolyte disturbance (hyponatraemia) or hypertension

## Management of renal failure

- Conservative: diet and drugs to control fluid and electrolytes
- Renal replacement therapy
  - Peritoneal dialysis (CAPD or continuous cycling, CCPD): commonest; done at home; main complication peritonitis
  - Haemodialysis: needs an indwelling venous catheter (infection risk); more complex, costly and disruptive (frequent hospital visits)
  - Renal transplant: best option, better survival than dialysis
    - Immunosuppression with ciclosporin, steroids, azathioprine
    - 1-year graft survival: 80% cadaveric, 90% live donor

## Preoperative assessment

- Renal team optimises fluids and electrolytes, including timing of dialysis around surgery
- After dialysis, check U&E, FBC and coagulation before surgery
- Potassium < 5.0 mmol/L, even for urgent cases
- May be hypovolaemic after dialysis
- Hypertension or risk of heart failure: ECG and CXR
- Medications
  - Continue antihypertensives and heart failure drugs
  - Continue steroids; may need perioperative cover
  - Diuretics can disturb electrolytes
- No pre-emptive top-up transfusion; Hb down to 6 g/dL acceptable for low blood loss surgery; have blood available

## Intraoperative management

- Fluids
  - Know the daily fluid allowance; match surgical losses carefully
  - CVP monitoring if large shifts expected
  - Use saline; avoid potassium-containing fluids
- Lines and access
  - Protect AV shunts and central lines
  - Do not use dialysis lines for routine infusions
  - Site IV access away from current and future fistula sites; no NIBP cuff on the shunt arm
- Suxamethonium
  - Raises potassium transiently by 0.5-1 mmol/L
  - Safe if potassium normal (< 5.5 mmol/L)
  - Usually only for emergency surgery in unfasted patients or with reflux
- Induction agents
  - Thiopental: 75-80% albumin-bound; hypoalbuminaemia and acidosis raise free drug, and uraemic patients are more sensitive; reduce dose
  - Reduce propofol dose too
- Non-depolarising relaxants
  - Pancuronium, gallamine and tubocurarine depend on renal excretion
  - Atracurium: Hofmann elimination, unaffected by renal failure; ideal
  - Vecuronium and rocuronium can be used safely
  - Anticholinesterase elimination is prolonged in step with the relaxants, so recurarisation is unlikely
- Volatiles
  - Enflurane and sevoflurane release fluoride: theoretical nephrotoxicity; prolonged use usually avoided, but not a real clinical problem
- Opioids
  - Renal clearance matters for many opioids and metabolites
  - Morphine and pethidine form active metabolites (morphine-6-glucuronide, norpethidine) that accumulate; norpethidine causes seizures
  - Lengthen PCA lockout (up to 15 min) and monitor for respiratory depression
  - Fentanyl, alfentanil and sufentanil accumulate less and lack active metabolites
- Local anaesthetics: shorter duration in CRF; less protein binding raises toxicity risk
- Neuraxial blocks: weigh risks carefully given platelet dysfunction

## Postoperative management

- No NSAIDs in known renal failure
- Regular renal team input on fluids, electrolytes and dialysis

## Flashcards
- Q: Urine findings in prerenal vs renal ARF?
  - A: Prerenal osmolality > 500 mosmol/L and Na < 20 mmol/L; renal osmolality < 350 mosmol/L and Na > 40 mmol/L
- Q: Commonest cause of renal failure in neonates? In older children in developed countries?
  - A: Perinatal asphyxia; haemolytic uraemic syndrome
- Q: Maximum potassium before surgery, even urgent?
  - A: < 5.0 mmol/L
- Q: Dose of desmopressin for uraemic bleeding?
  - A: 300 nanograms/kg (or pooled cryoprecipitate)
- Q: How much does suxamethonium raise potassium, and when is it safe in renal failure?
  - A: 0.5-1 mmol/L; safe if potassium < 5.5 mmol/L
