# Hypertension

Labile BP with right-shifted autoregulation: keep BP near baseline and blunt swings at induction and intubation.

## Key points
- Control BP below 160/90
- Labile BP; altered autoregulation means higher MAP targets
- LVH, IHD, stroke
- Hypertensive emergency: refer to the emergency department

## Main considerations
- Labile BP: more intraoperative hypotension and hypertension
- Autoregulation shifts right: MAP targets about 20% higher
- Cardiovascular complications
  - LVH leading to diastolic heart failure
  - Arrhythmia
  - Coronary artery disease: myocardial ischaemia or infarction
- Stroke

## Preoperative assessment
- New finding or known history
- Cause: primary or secondary
- End-organ damage (MI, stroke, CKD)
- Degree of control; home readings
- Assessing BP control
  - Severe hypertension: systolic above 180 mmHg or diastolic above 110 mmHg
  - Readings below 180/110 carry no extra postoperative risk: do not postpone elective surgery for them
  - Get community BP readings from the GP; if usually well controlled and the clinic reading reflects stress, there is no gain in delaying surgery
- Exclude hypertensive emergency: BP above 180/110 with end-organ damage
  - Chest pain, arrhythmia, dyspnoea, orthopnoea, peripheral oedema (heart failure)
  - Headache, vomiting, reduced consciousness, seizure
  - Haematuria, proteinuria
  - Numbness, weakness, slurred speech
  - Retinal haemorrhage, papilloedema, blurred vision
- Poor control: weigh urgency
  - Elective: consider postponing in selected patients; refer for better control
  - Emergency: consider an arterial line and perioperative antihypertensives
- Check lying and standing BP: if orthostatic, preoperative fluid helps prevent severe hypotension at induction
- Renal panel, especially if on diuretics
  - Mild hypokalaemia (3-3.5) is common on diuretics and harmless; replace only if symptomatic or on digoxin
- Premedication: reduces anxiety, which helps BP control

## Medications
- Continue antihypertensives in general
- Beta-blockers: continue; sudden withdrawal raises cardiac morbidity and mortality
  - But starting them just before surgery is linked to more strokes and deaths
- ACE inhibitors and ARBs: withhold on the morning of surgery
  - Unless heart failure or poorly controlled hypertension
  - Alternative: withhold for 24 hours before surgery
  - Reason: more intraoperative hypotension
  - Downside: more severe perioperative hypertension needing parenteral treatment
  - Restart as soon as possible: not restarting ARBs within 48 hours is linked to higher 30-day mortality
  - SPACE trial ongoing, planned to end in 2022
- Diuretics: withhold on the morning of surgery
  - Theoretical risk of intraoperative hypotension (unlikely on chronic therapy)
  - Heart failure: decide by volume status, optimised beforehand where possible
    - Well-controlled heart failure with stable volume: may hold the morning dose
    - Historically hard-to-control fluid balance: continue without a break
  - If held and fluid overload develops, give an IV diuretic

## Intraoperative management
- Monitoring: arterial line depending on extent of surgery and end-organ damage
- Induction is often haemodynamically unstable
  - Exaggerated hypotension at induction: avoid preoperative volume depletion
  - Then exaggerated hypertension at intubation: intubate deep, keep laryngoscopy short, add an opioid or lidocaine bolus
  - Ketamine alone can cause marked hypertension; a small dose of another agent blunts this
  - Large pancuronium boluses cause vagal block and catecholamine release, worsening hypertension; small slow increments are unlikely to matter
- Fluids
  - Stiff LVH ventricle may need more IV fluid for higher filling pressures to keep LVEDV and cardiac output
  - But pulmonary pressures may be raised with pulmonary congestion
- Hypertension not responding to deeper anaesthesia (other causes excluded): parenteral antihypertensives
- Other times HR and BP rise: surgical incision, emergence
- BP targets
  - Keep arterial pressure within 20% of preoperative levels
  - Assume CAD: avoid hypertension and tachycardia, which can cause ischaemia or ventricular dysfunction
  - Altered cerebral autoregulation: higher than normal MAP may be needed for cerebral blood flow
  - Marked preoperative hypertension above 180/120: keep BP high-normal, 140-150/80-90

## Postoperative care
- Postoperative hypertension is common in poorly controlled patients: monitor BP closely
- Sustained high BP can cause
  - Myocardial ischaemia and heart failure
  - Wound haematoma
  - Disruption of vascular suture lines
- Correct contributing causes; parenteral antihypertensives if needed

## Flashcards
- Q: Above what BP is hypertension considered severe preoperatively?
  - A: Systolic above 180 mmHg or diastolic above 110 mmHg.
- Q: Should elective surgery be postponed for BP below 180/110?
  - A: No; it carries no extra postoperative risk.
- Q: How long after surgery should ARBs be restarted, and why?
  - A: Within 48 hours; later restart is linked to higher 30-day mortality.
- Q: Intraoperative BP target in a hypertensive patient?
  - A: Within 20% of preoperative levels.
- Q: Target BP if preoperative BP was above 180/120?
  - A: High-normal, 140-150/80-90.
