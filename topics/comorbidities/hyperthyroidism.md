# Hyperthyroidism

Make patients clinically and biochemically euthyroid before surgery and guard against thyroid storm.

## Key points
- Aim for clinical and biochemical euthyroidism
- Thyroid storm risk

## Thyroid function tests
- Overt thyrotoxicosis: TSH suppressed (less than 0.01 mIU/L); free T4 and free T3 raised
- T3 thyrotoxicosis: TSH suppressed; free T3 raised; free T4 normal
- Subclinical hyperthyroidism: TSH low or suppressed; free T3 and free T4 normal
  - Despite the name, typical thyrotoxic features can occur

## Main considerations
- Structural
  - Goitre may make the airway difficult (compression, anterior mediastinal mass)
  - Vocal cord palsy
- End-organ effects of chronic hyperthyroidism
  - Hypermetabolic: VO2 and VCO2 increase
  - Cardiovascular: hypertension, tachycardia, myocardial ischaemia, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias
  - CNS: anxiety, psychiatric disorders
  - Muscle weakness
- Risk of thyroid storm
- Anaesthetic interactions
  - MAC is not increased
  - More anaesthetic may be needed to control BP and heart rate
  - Propofol clearance and volume of distribution increase: raise TIVA infusion rates to reach anaesthetic levels
  - Avoid sympathetic stimulants (ketamine, cocaine, adrenaline etc.)

## Preoperative assessment
- Establish euthyroidism first; antithyroid drugs may need a long wait (3-8 weeks)
  - Beta-blockade (propranolol): effective in 12-24 h
  - Carbimazole: 3-4 weeks; PTU acts faster than carbimazole
  - Iodine: clinical effect in 6-10 weeks
- Examine the upper airway for tracheal compression or deviation from goitre
  - If present, CT or MRI to define site and degree of narrowing
- Look for other autoimmune disease
- Investigations: TSH, free T4, free T3
- Continue beta-blockers perioperatively
- Emergency surgery
  - May need IV beta-blocker, glucocorticoids, iodine and PTU
  - PTU has no IV form: give orally, by nasogastric tube or rectally

## Intraoperative management
- Monitor temperature: risk of hyperthermia during and after surgery
- Proptosis: eye protection is critical (drops, lubricant, pads)
- Induction
  - May be chronically hypovolaemic: exaggerated hypotension at induction
  - Thiopental has antithyroid effects at high doses; the benefit is theoretical and unproven in humans
- Maintenance
  - Sympathetic responses are exaggerated: avoid ketamine, pancuronium, atropine, ephedrine
  - Treat hypotension with a direct-acting vasopressor (phenylephrine)
  - Avoid adrenaline-containing local anaesthetic solutions
  - MAC requirement unchanged

## Thyroid storm
- Medical emergency; see the thyroid storm topic

## Flashcards
- Q: Which vasopressor treats hypotension in a hyperthyroid patient under anaesthesia?
  - A: A direct-acting one, such as phenylephrine.
- Q: How quickly does propranolol work in hyperthyroidism?
  - A: 12-24 h.
- Q: How long does carbimazole take to work?
  - A: 3-4 weeks.
- Q: Is MAC changed in hyperthyroidism?
  - A: No.
- Q: Why must PTU be given orally, by NG tube or rectally?
  - A: There is no IV preparation.
