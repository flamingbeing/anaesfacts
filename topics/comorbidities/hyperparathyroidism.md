# Hyperparathyroidism

Excess PTH; classified as primary, secondary or tertiary. Key anaesthetic issues are calcium control, ECG changes, renal failure and neuromuscular block.

## Classification
- Primary
- Secondary
- Tertiary

## Primary hyperparathyroidism
- Usually an adenoma secreting excess PTH
- Associated with familial multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) type 1

### Preoperative assessment
- History
  - Often asymptomatic
  - Anorexia, dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting, constipation
  - Polydipsia, polyuria
  - Poor memory, drowsiness
  - "Bones, stones, groans and psychic moans"
- Examination
  - Hypertension
  - Gland rarely palpable
- Investigations
  - High serum calcium (Ca2+)
    - Albumin correction: add 0.1mmol/L to calcium for each 5g/L that albumin is below 40g/L
  - Low phosphate
  - ECG: short QTc

### Preoperative management
- Methylene blue 1mg/kg is often needed to locate the glands
- Hydrate
- Calcium-lowering drugs (pamidronate)
- Normalise calcium, then proceed (aim below 3mmol/L)
- Postpone surgery if possible until treated if any of:
  - Calcium above 3
  - Abnormal ECG
  - Cardiovascular or renal impairment

### Intraoperative management
- Monitor response to neuromuscular blockade

### Postoperative care
- Monitor calcium

## Secondary hyperparathyroidism
- Chronic renal failure leads to low calcium and compensatory parathyroid hyperplasia, giving:
  - High PTH
  - Normal or low calcium
  - High phosphate

### Preoperative assessment
- History
  - Chronic renal failure
  - Excess bone resorption (radial aspect of the middle phalanx of the second finger)
  - Calcification of vessels and soft tissues (kidneys, heart, lung, skin)
- Examination
  - Soft tissue calcium deposits
- Investigations
  - High PTH
  - Normal or low calcium
  - High phosphate
  - X-ray changes

### Management
- Medical treatment (fails in 15%):
  - Dietary phosphate restriction
  - Dietary or supplemental calcium and vitamin D
- Parathyroidectomy
- Dialyse before surgery

### Intraoperative management
- Usual renal failure care

### Postoperative care
- Monitor calcium

## Tertiary hyperparathyroidism
- Hyperplasia progresses to autonomous secretion, behaving like an adenoma
- Persists even after renal failure is corrected
- Only a few need surgery

### Preoperative assessment
- Chronic renal failure
- Symptoms of hypercalcaemia (stones, bones, groans and psychic moans)

### Intraoperative management
- Usual renal failure care
- Monitor neuromuscular blockade

### Postoperative care
- Monitor calcium

## Hypercalcaemic crisis
- Most common in elderly patients with undiagnosed hyperparathyroidism and malignancy
- Dehydration leads to anorexia, diarrhoea and vomiting, which worsen it further
- Other features: weakness, lethargy, mental changes, coma
- Calcium above 4.5 is life threatening
- First-line treatment
  - IV fluids: 4-6 litres may be needed
  - Pamidronate 60mg in 500ml saline over 4 hrs
  - Calcitonin 4unit/kg IV, then 4unit/kg subcutaneously twice daily
    - Rapid but temporary fall in calcium; reduces calcium and phosphate release from bone
- Other treatment
  - Hydrocortisone 200-400mg IV daily (in malignancy)
  - IV fluids and diuresis with furosemide 40mg IV (reduces proximal tubular calcium reabsorption)
  - Dialysis, reserved for renal failure

## Flashcards
- Q: How do you correct calcium for albumin?
  - A: Add 0.1mmol/L for each 5g/L that albumin is below 40g/L
- Q: When should parathyroid surgery be postponed for treatment?
  - A: Calcium above 3mmol/L, abnormal ECG, or cardiovascular or renal impairment
- Q: At what calcium level is hypercalcaemia life threatening?
  - A: Above 4.5mmol/L
- Q: Pamidronate dose in hypercalcaemic crisis?
  - A: 60mg in 500ml saline over 4 hrs
- Q: Typical biochemistry of secondary hyperparathyroidism?
  - A: High PTH, normal or low calcium, high phosphate
