# Neonatal anaesthesia

Neonates are high risk: difficult airway, rate-dependent cardiac output, postoperative apnoea, and the need to keep them warm, wet and sweet.

## Summary
- High risk overall: consider booking a high dependency bed
- Avoid sedative premedication; do not proceed with an active URTI; refer a murmur to cardiology
- Difficult airway
- Postoperative apnoea
- Rate-dependent cardiac output
- Warm, wet and sweet
  - Hypothermia
  - Insensible fluid losses
  - Hypoglycaemia
- Prematurity: intraventricular haemorrhage, retinopathy of prematurity
- Birth history and post-conceptual age (PCA)

## Definition
- Neonate: newborn up to 44 weeks post-conceptual age (the first 4 weeks after birth in a full-term 40wk baby)

## Physiology and pharmacology
- Airway
  - Different anatomy (<4YO): large head with prominent occiput, small mandible, large tongue, high anterior larynx, large floppy U-shaped epiglottis; leads to a difficult airway and a different intubation approach
  - Funnel-shaped larynx, narrowest at the cricoid: uncuffed tube if <8y (or microcuff tube if >3kg)
  - Short trachea: endobronchial intubation or accidental extubation even with head movement
  - More laryngospasm
  - Obligate nasal breathers: a nasogastric tube or nasal blockage can worsen respiratory distress
  - Different equipment sizes
- Breathing
  - High metabolic rate and oxygen consumption (needs high minute ventilation) with low FRC: rapid desaturation
  - Closing capacity exceeds FRC, causing atelectasis: use PEEP
  - Little respiratory reserve: few type 1 fibres in the diaphragm, horizontal ribs
  - Prone to diaphragmatic splinting
  - Narrow airways plug easily with mucus
  - Immature respiratory control: postoperative apnoea, breath-holding
  - Lungs vulnerable to barotrauma and volutrauma (interstitial damage, pneumothorax): limit peak pressure
  - High FiO2 also injures lungs: keep inspired oxygen as low as possible
  - Equipment: weight-based tidal volumes, T-piece, apparatus dead space matters
- Cardiovascular
  - Cardiac output is rate dependent (stiff LV)
  - Parasympathetic dominance: bradycardia; give fluids if hypotensive
  - Transitional circulation: rises in PVR can reopen fetal shunts, with risk of paradoxical embolism
  - Possible undiagnosed congenital heart disease
    - Duct-dependent lesions collapse when the duct closes, typically at 1 week of age; treat with prostaglandin infusion until definitive repair
  - Weight-based fluids, small cannulas, attention to dead space volume
- Central nervous system
  - Immature blood-brain barrier: sensitive to anaesthetic drugs
  - Spinal cord ends lower
- Gastrointestinal: shorter fasting times; reflux
- Renal
  - Immature renal clearance (<6-12mo)
  - Immature glomerular filtration and tubules: poor ability to concentrate urine or excrete a water or sodium load
  - Need more fluid per kg; dehydrate quickly with fluid restriction because of large insensible losses (high surface area to weight, respiratory losses)
  - Equally easy to overload: minimal drug dilutions, syringe pumps for fluids and line flushes, accurate fluid balance
- Hepatic
  - Immature hepatic metabolism (<3mo)
  - Risk of neonatal jaundice
- Endocrine and metabolic
  - Small glycogen stores: high hypoglycaemia risk, especially in preterm babies
    - First 24hrs: <2.2mmol/L
    - After 24hrs: <2.8mmol/L
  - Maintenance dextrose infusion often used to prevent it
  - Hyperglycaemia from glucose loading is poorly tolerated
  - Check blood glucose regularly
- Haematology
  - Larger blood volume per kg
  - Physiological anaemia of infancy
  - Haemorrhagic disease of the newborn: vitamin K dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X) are low at birth
    - All newborns should get vitamin K at delivery; check the notes before surgery
    - Give 1mg/kg if surgery is in the first week of life
    - If urgent major surgery and none given: coagulation screen; FFP may be needed
- Thermoregulation
  - Large surface area to weight, thin immature skin, little subcutaneous fat: high heat loss
  - Immature thermoregulation: prone to hypothermia
  - Depend on limited brown fat for non-shivering thermogenesis; GA, volatiles and opioids abolish it
- Drugs
  - Pharmacokinetics
    - Absorption: faster inhalational induction
    - Distribution: more total body water, less protein binding
    - Metabolism and excretion: lower clearance
  - Pharmacodynamics
    - Different receptor numbers and subtypes, so more resistant or sensitive to some effects (e.g. higher MAC)
    - Less reserve to compensate for side effects, so they appear more sensitive
  - Few neonatal drug trials; many drugs unlicensed
  - Weight-based doses in small volumes
- Monitoring: different normal ranges; SpO2 alone is enough in recovery

## Preoperative assessment
- Gestational and post-conceptual age
- Antenatal and birth history: gestation, birth weight, mode of delivery, Apgar score; maternal problems (e.g. gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia); antenatally diagnosed fetal problems
- Surgery: planned procedure and variations, duration, expected blood loss, analgesia options
- Congenital anomalies and syndromes
  - May be the surgical problem or affect anaesthesia (e.g. large tongue in Beckwith-Wiedemann, CHD in Down syndrome)
  - Cardiac: heart failure, duct dependence, shunt direction and flow, pulmonary hypertension, drugs (diuretics, digoxin)
  - Respiratory: guides postoperative monitoring and need for IPPV; prematurity (bronchopulmonary dysplasia, apnoea risk), previous ventilation, baseline oxygenation and oxygen needs
- Meet the parents where possible (may be impossible after an immediate post-birth transfer); history, examination, and discuss anaesthesia, analgesia and likely postoperative course

## Investigations
- Routine: full blood count, U&E, blood glucose
- Others guided by surgery and anomalies
  - Clotting screen: sepsis, jaundice
  - ECG and echo: congenital heart disease
  - Chest X-ray: lung or heart disease, diaphragmatic hernia, oesophageal atresia and tracheo-oesophageal fistula
  - Abdominal X-ray: bowel obstruction, enterocolitis
  - MRI: raised ICP, seizures

## Premedication
- Sedative premedication rarely used: sensitivity, airway obstruction and respiratory depression
- Confirm vitamin K was given (deficiency of factors II, VII, IX and X, bleeding risk)

## Preparation and transfer
- Baby: IV access; NICU can sometimes intubate before transfer
- Theatre
  - Raise ambient temperature to 26-28°C; forced-air warmer
  - Calculate all doses, including emergency drugs, in advance; draw up, flush with or without albumin
  - Monitor set to paediatric mode
  - Check machine, paediatric circuits, masks, airways, blades and ETTs
  - Prepare infusion set
- Transfer in a warmed transport unit

## Intraoperative management
- Monitoring
  - ECG and oximeter on before induction
  - SpO2 ideally pre-ductal (right hand) and post-ductal (other limbs); low post-ductal saturation suggests low pulmonary blood flow, e.g. pulmonary hypertension in a septic neonate
- Warming
  - Theatre at about 26-28°C
  - Warming mattress; forced-air warmer or overhead radiant heater during induction and cannulation
  - Cover head and as much of the body as possible: forced-air blanket, limbs wrapped in gamgee, plastic cover on the head
  - Warmed, humidified gases (heated humidifier or heat and moisture exchanger)
  - Warmed IV and skin preparation fluids
  - Avoid needless exposure; monitor core and peripheral temperature
- Airway
  - Tracheal intubation almost always; small LMAs sit poorly
  - T-piece circuit makes airway handling easier
  - Desaturate quickly when apnoeic: preoxygenate and keep mask ventilation effective until intubation
  - If RSI needed, use a modified RSI with gentle bagging
- Induction
  - IV or inhalational, by situation and preference
  - IV attractive if a cannula is in place; may need smaller doses than older infants (thiopental 2-3 mg/kg, ketamine 1-2 mg/kg)
  - Inhalational common without IV access: avoids cannulating a struggling baby, keeps oxygenation, and the airway can be cleared as the baby relaxes
  - IM ketamine 5 mg/kg occasionally: difficult access, cardiovascular instability, or significant CHD dependent on a left to right shunt
- Neuromuscular blockade
  - Given once anaesthesia and mask ventilation are established; larger volume of distribution in neonates
  - Suxamethonium: larger dose than normal (3 mg/kg)
  - Non-depolarising agents: usual doses (greater muscle sensitivity offsets the larger volume of distribution)
  - Intubation under deep inhalational anaesthesia without relaxant is also possible
- Maintenance
  - Usually volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
    - Neonatal MAC is lower than in infants (MAC peaks at 1-6months), except sevoflurane, where neonatal MAC is higher
    - Neonatal myocardium is very sensitive to volatile negative inotropy: avoid deep volatile anaesthesia; induction concentration not above 6%
    - Less than 1 MAC may be enough if unstable; MAC for unconsciousness in neonates is poorly defined
    - Nitrous oxide reduces volatile requirement; same contraindications as in older children
  - Opioid-based anaesthesia in very sick neonates
  - Titrate FiO2 to target SpO2 (e.g. 92-95%; 88-92% acceptable if ventilation is difficult); avoid FiO2 100% to limit retinopathy and lung injury
  - Almost always ventilated: mechanically if the ventilator is capable, often by hand with a T-piece (cannot be scavenged well)

## Fluids
- Maintenance fluid (continue the ward drip) for all but the shortest cases
  - First few days of life: low sodium needs; 10% dextrose usually recommended
  - After postnatal diuresis at around day 3: 4% or 5% glucose, e.g. 4% glucose with 0.18% saline
  - Monitor blood glucose in longer cases
  - Alternative: 2% dextrose drip to avoid hypoglycaemia, returning to 5% after surgery
    - 20ml of 50% dextrose (D50) in 500ml gives a 2% solution
  - Warm all fluids; give via pump or syringe driver
- Volume replacement
  - Blood volume taken as 90 mL/kg
  - Crystalloid (0.9% saline or Ringer's lactate) or colloid
  - Blood and products by ongoing loss and preoperative Hb; check Hb intraoperatively if access allows

## Analgesia
- If extubating, prefer regional to avoid opioids
  - Infiltration for superficial surgery (inguinal herniotomy, pyloromyotomy)
  - Peripheral blocks for penile or groin surgery
  - Central block, especially caudal epidural, for larger surgery below the umbilicus (e.g. colostomy for imperforate anus)
- Opioids in smaller doses at longer intervals than older children
  - Morphine 25-50 mcg/kg IV
  - Fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg IV
  - Larger doses if ventilating after surgery
- Paracetamol often omitted, or given at reduced dose
- NSAIDs relatively contraindicated: renal immaturity, hypovolaemia, possible sepsis

## Reversal and extubation
- Only when normothermic, normovolaemic and with minimal expired volatile
- Reverse with neostigmine 50 mcg/kg plus atropine 20 mcg/kg or glycopyrronium 10 mcg/kg
- Gentle suction of pharynx and nostrils (nasal breathers)
- Aspirate any nasogastric tube
- Extubate awake, breathing well and moving vigorously
- Face mask CPAP may be needed for laryngospasm

## Postoperative care
- Term neonates <44 weeks PCA and preterm infants <60 weeks PCA: monitor for 24 hours after GA or regional, with apnoea monitor and pulse oximeter (e.g. high dependency)
- Regular blood glucose until feeding
- Maintenance fluids until feeding; replace nasogastric losses, volume and blood as needed
- Morphine infusion 5-10 mcg/kg/hour if needed for analgesia or sedation; occasionally 20 mcg/kg/hour
- Bloods as indicated

- Q: Which babies need 24 hours of apnoea monitoring after anaesthesia?
  - A: Term neonates <44 weeks PCA and preterm infants <60 weeks PCA.
- Q: Suxamethonium dose in a neonate?
  - A: 3 mg/kg (larger than normal).
- Q: Neonatal reversal doses?
  - A: Neostigmine 50 mcg/kg with atropine 20 mcg/kg or glycopyrronium 10 mcg/kg.
