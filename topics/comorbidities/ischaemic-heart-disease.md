# Ischaemic Heart Disease

Stratify risk, keep supply above demand (slow HR, BP near baseline, Hb, normothermia), watch for ischaemia and manage antiplatelets around stents.

## Key points
- Cardiovascular risk assessment; delay elective surgery when needed
- Balance myocardial supply and demand: avoid tachycardia, hypertension, hypotension; Hb >8; normothermia
- 5-lead ECG
- Cardiostable induction with opioids, with or without arterial line, CVP, vasopressors; esmolol on standby
- Plan antiplatelet management

## Physiology
- Above basal needs, myocyte contraction sets myocardial oxygen consumption (MVO2)
- Three determinants of demand: heart rate, contractility, ventricular wall tension
- Wall tension follows Laplace: rises with systolic pressure and ventricular radius, falls with wall thickness
  - Higher afterload raises the systolic pressure generated
  - Higher preload raises radius, so tension and oxygen demand rise
- Greater contractility means stronger and faster contraction (higher dP/dT), which costs more energy

## Anaesthetic relevance
- Raises perioperative MACE risk, rising with severity (Mayo 2021)
- Associated complications, e.g. heart failure
- Patients may be on antiplatelets

## Preoperative assessment
- Goals: risk stratification (unrevascularised IHD, severity, complications), optimisation, urgency of surgery
- History
  - Angina (stable or unstable), breathlessness, orthopnoea, ankle oedema
  - Effort tolerance
  - Risk factors: diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia
  - Previous MI (date, extent, complications), arrhythmias, MR, heart failure
- Canadian Cardiovascular Society angina grades
  - I: ordinary activity does not cause angina (> 4 METS)
  - II: slight limitation of ordinary activity (2-4 METS)
  - III: marked limitation (1-2 METS)
  - IV: unable to do any activity; angina at rest
- Examination: standard CVS exam, often normal; look for heart failure signs

## Preoperative investigations
- ECG
- Biomarkers: troponin, NT-proBNP
  - Baseline hs-cTn identifies chronic elevation and patients at higher perioperative risk
- HbA1c
- Stress testing (stress echo, MIBI) in high-risk patients only if it will change management
  - 2024 ACC: may be considered for elevated-risk surgery with poor or unknown functional capacity and elevated risk on a validated tool
  - 2024 ACC: not routinely for low-risk patients, good functional capacity with stable symptoms, or low-risk surgery (no benefit)
- Coronary angiography
  - Significant stenosis: ≥70% non-left main; ≥50% left main
  - Intermediate 40% to 69%: test physiological significance with FFR or iFR
    - FFR > 0.8: not significant
    - iFR >0.89: not significant

## Preoperative optimisation
- Haemodynamic goals as for intraoperative care
  - Hb > 8g/dL
  - Avoid tachycardia
  - Keep euvolaemic
- Revascularise before surgery only if indicated (e.g. high-risk coronary anatomy)
- Time surgery appropriately after MI or revascularisation
- Premedication to reduce anxiety

## Medications
- Continue all drugs for angina, arrhythmia and heart failure
- Aspirin monotherapy
  - Usually stopped, but continue if cardiac risk outweighs bleeding risk
  - Common practice: stop 5-7d before surgery to reduce bleeding
  - Continue if MI or CABG within 1 year, or previous PCI, if bleeding risk acceptable
  - POISE-2: starting aspirin for CAD without PCI or MI did not change death or non-fatal MI, with slightly more bleeding
- Dual antiplatelet therapy (usually recent stents)
  - Stopping early after PCI carries high risk of acute stent thrombosis (type 4b MI)
  - Postpone elective surgery to 4-6wks after BMS or 6-12 months after DES
  - Bridging with antithrombins, anticoagulants or GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors has not been effective
- Beta-blockers: continue
  - Continue if taken for angina, symptomatic arrhythmia, hypertension or other ACC/AHA Class I indications
  - Start only for a real indication
    - May worsen hypotension and limit cardiac output during bleeding, anaemia or infection
    - 2024 ACC: start early enough to assess tolerance and titrate, optimally >7days before surgery
    - POISE: started on the day of surgery, fewer MIs but more strokes and deaths
    - 2024 ACC: do not start on the day of surgery without an immediate need (higher postoperative mortality)
- Calcium channel blockers: continue
  - No clear protection against perioperative cardiac events
  - Dihydropyridines (especially sublingual nifedipine) may add MI risk (Oxford)
  - Verapamil reduces SVT
- ACE inhibitors and ARBs: usually stop
  - Block the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis, raising postoperative vasoplegia risk
  - Restart at 24hrs if renal function stable and BP allows
- Nitrates: continue (IV or transdermal if needed); prophylactic use does not reduce cardiac events
- Diuretics: consider stopping to avoid hypovolaemia and hypokalaemia, unless high risk of decompensation (Mayo 2021)
- Statins: continue
  - Actions: 1) lipid lowering and plaque stabilisation; 2) anti-inflammatory, less thrombogenesis, better endothelial function
  - Sudden withdrawal can destabilise plaques and worsen outcomes
- Alpha-2 agonists: no role for prophylactic clonidine
  - POISE-2: clonidine gave no benefit, more significant hypotension and non-fatal MI
  - Continue if already taking it (withdrawal risk)

## Preoperative coronary revascularisation
- Only if the patient would need it anyway, regardless of upcoming surgery
- Considered before elective intermediate- or high-risk noncardiac surgery or vascular surgery
- ACC 2024 indications (to reduce MACE)
  - Acute coronary syndromes: STEMI; high-risk unstable angina or NSTEMI (e.g. cardiogenic shock)
  - Chronic coronary disease: significant left main stenosis ≥50%; possibly triple vessel disease with EF < 35%
- Routine revascularisation not recommended
  - CARP trial: prophylactic PCI or CABG before major vascular surgery did not change 30-day or 1-year mortality or MI
    - Excluded e.g. left main disease and severe AS
  - More bleeding complications with PCI
- Choice of method
  - CABG gives better outcomes than PCI, especially if surgery is needed within 1 month (Mayo 2021)
  - If surgery is more urgent, balloon angioplasty allows surgery after only 14 days (Mayo 2021)

## Intraoperative management
- Aims: prevent ischaemia (balance supply and demand), detect and treat it early, manage complications
- GA: cardiostable induction; blunt the pressor response
- Regional: fluid preload to prevent hypotension; vasopressors

## Detecting ischaemia
- Basic haemodynamics (HR, BP, CVP): poor sensitivity and specificity
  - Hypertension and tachycardia are the commonest findings and are almost always the cause, not the result
  - Hypotension is a late, ominous sign of failing ventricular function
- 5-lead ECG: monitor leads II and V5, or CM5
  - Usually T-wave changes or ST depression
  - ST elevation is rare in noncardiac surgery: severe ischaemia, vasospasm or infarction
  - New arrhythmias or conduction defects
  - Most useful leads in decreasing sensitivity: V5, V4, II, V2, V3
  - Monitor at least two leads with 5 electrodes: RA, RL, LA, LL, V1
  - Lead II for inferior ischaemia and arrhythmias; V5 for anterior ischaemia
  - With one channel (3-lead), CM5 is most sensitive for LV ischaemia
    - Bipolar: LA electrode to V5 position (just above the 5th interspace, anterior axillary line), RA electrode to manubrium; select lead I
  - Pros: simple, non-invasive, available; II plus V5 detects 96% of events (V5 most sensitive; II, V4 and V5 together in high-risk patients); changes appear at 50% fall in coronary flow
  - Cons: less sensitive than RWMA; unreadable with BBB or ventricular pacing
- TOE: most sensitive
  - New RWMA is the earliest sign: 4x more sensitive than ECG; appears at 25% fall in coronary flow before ECG change
  - Invasive
- POCUS
- PA catheter
  - Raised pressures, especially diastolic; new V waves
  - Low sensitivity and specificity (40-60%); other causes such as MR, raised SVR, CO or PVR
  - Insertion risks and contraindications
- Others: magnetic resonance spectroscopy, radiolabelled lactate, direct LVEDP measurement

## Monitoring
- Ischaemia: 5-lead ECG, with or without TOE or POCUS
- Perfusion
  - Arterial line reasonable in severe CAD with major or multiple risk factors
  - CVP for long cases with big fluid shifts or blood loss; central line for vasopressors
  - Cardiac output monitoring (TOE, PA catheter) to guide volume and avoid heart failure

## Haemodynamic goals
- Low to normal heart rate, e.g. 60 to 80 bpm
  - Tachycardia worsens both supply and demand
  - 70 to 80 percent of coronary flow occurs in diastole; diastolic time shortens non-linearly with HR
  - Doubling HR more than doubles oxygen demand
  - HR above 80 bpm from pain or light anaesthesia: give opioid or deepen anaesthesia first
  - If HR stays high, give an IV beta-blocker (esmolol, metoprolol, labetalol), with or without signs of ischaemia
- BP within 20 percent of baseline
  - Severe hypotension cuts supply; severe hypertension raises demand
  - Treat hypotension promptly: target MAP 75 to 95 mmHg and/or diastolic 65 to 85 mmHg
  - Hypertension raises systolic wall stress and LVEDP; subendocardium is most vulnerable as LVEDP limits its flow
- Avoid sympathetic surges
  - Good analgesia, anaesthetic depth and paralysis; pain is a potent cause of tachycardia
  - Regional blocks control pain and reduce perioperative hypercoagulability
  - Anticipate hypertension and tachycardia at intubation and extubation; consider esmolol
- Euvolaemia (normal LV end-diastolic volume)
  - Overload distends the LV, raising wall stress and oxygen demand
  - Hypovolaemia causes compensatory tachycardia and poorer perfusion

## Other goals
- Oxygen content
  - Normal to high SpO2 and PaO2; supplemental oxygen to SpO2 ≥95 percent
  - Hb ≥8 g/dL; transfuse at Hb ≤8 g/dL in stable IHD, or ≤9 g/dL after recent MI or unstable angina, especially with bleeding, hypovolaemia or organ ischaemia
- Normothermia: aids oxygen release; shivering raises demand and can cause ischaemia
  - Except deliberate mild hypothermia for organ protection (e.g. high aortic cross-clamp)
- Homeostasis: avoid hypercarbia and acidosis; K 4-4.5; normoglycaemia

## Epidural analgesia
- Pros: good pain control; lower preload and afterload; coronary vasodilation (thoracic); better flow, countering hypercoagulability
- Cons: sympathectomy hypotension; respiratory depression; epidural haematoma or abscess

## Postoperative care
- HDU if haemodynamic monitoring needed
- Troponin surveillance in high-risk patients
- Maintain BP, SpO2 and Hb (check postoperative Hb)
- Restart antiplatelets as soon as possible
- High suspicion for perioperative MI: 12-lead ECG, troponin

## Coronary stents (PCI)
- A stent makes the patient ASA 3
- Stent thrombosis risk
  - Surgery is prothrombotic
  - Stented endothelium is thrombogenic until re-endothelialised; slower with DES
- Timing of surgery
  - Highest risk <6 weeks after implantation (MACE 5–30%)
  - Elective surgery: delay at least 30 days
  - DES
    - DAPT usually 6-12 months: 6mth for chronic coronary syndromes, 12mth for ACS
    - ACS: elective surgery >12mth (AHA 2024); time-sensitive >3mths (AHA 2024) after cardiology MDT
    - Chronic coronary disease: elective >6mth (AHA 2024); time-sensitive >3mths (AHA 2024) after cardiology MDT
    - Never if <1mo
  - BMS: delay 30 days (preferably 90 days)
  - Balloon angioplasty: delay 14 days
  - Alternative: bridge with tirofiban (GP IIb/IIIa; stop 6hr before surgery) plus heparin
- Five factors to consider (four listed)
  - (1) PCI-to-surgery interval
  - (2) continuing DAPT
  - (3) anaesthetic technique
  - (4) perioperative monitoring

## Stents: interval and antiplatelets
- Thrombosis risk is highest in the first month and falls with time; longer waits are better
- BMS: wait at least 30 days (ideally 90 days); DES: at least 1 year before elective surgery
- DAPT at least 6 weeks after BMS and 1 year after DES
- If DAPT must stop, keep aspirin; stop aspirin only when truly necessary
- Stent thrombosis can happen at any time, not only in the vulnerable window
- Monitor closely in the vulnerable period, especially if antiplatelets were stopped
- Bleeding on antiplatelets: platelet transfusion works best once the drug has cleared
  - Clopidogrel: platelets can be given from 4 hours after the last dose, most effective at 24 hours

## Stents: anaesthetic technique and monitoring
- Neuraxial on DAPT is controversial; ASRA and European guidance are conservative and discourage it
- Haematoma risk applies at catheter insertion and removal; use current ASRA timings
- Continuous ECG with ST analysis; any angina needs urgent assessment for MI and a cardiology review

## POISE trial
- 8351 high-risk beta-blocker-naive patients: high-dose extended-release metoprolol versus placebo
- Non-fatal MI fell by 26%
- 30-day all-cause mortality rose by 30% and stroke by 100%, mostly linked to bleeding and hypotension

## Flashcards
- Q: How long should elective surgery be delayed after a DES for ACS versus chronic coronary disease (AHA 2024)?
  - A: >12 months for ACS, >6 months for chronic disease; time-sensitive surgery >3 months after cardiology MDT; never <1 month.
- Q: Minimum delays after BMS and balloon angioplasty?
  - A: BMS 30 days (preferably 90); balloon angioplasty 14 days.
- Q: Haemodynamic targets for a patient with IHD under anaesthesia?
  - A: HR 60 to 80, BP within 20% of baseline (MAP 75 to 95 or diastolic 65 to 85), Hb ≥8 (≥9 after recent MI).
- Q: Best single ECG lead configuration with only 3 leads?
  - A: CM5: LA electrode at V5, RA at the manubrium, lead I selected.
- Q: What did POISE show for day-of-surgery metoprolol?
  - A: Fewer MIs (26%) but 30% higher mortality and 100% more strokes.
