# Diabetes Mellitus

Check control and end-organ damage, adjust medicines and insulin for fasting, keep glucose about 6–10 mmol/L and never stop basal insulin in type 1.

## Key points
- Check control (HbA1c <8.5-10), oral hypoglycaemic and insulin plan, capillary glucose and sliding-scale insulin, ischaemic heart disease risk
- First on the list if insulin dependent or poorly controlled
- Stop oral hypoglycaemic agents (OHGAs) on the morning of surgery
  - SGLT2 inhibitors: stop 72hrs before
  - GLP1 receptor agonists: stop 1 week before
- Insulin: stop prandial insulin, continue basal insulin
- Monitor capillary glucose
- Type 1 diabetes (T1DM): refer to endocrinology; never stop basal insulin

## Effects of diabetes
- End-organ disease: retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, coronary heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, hypertension
  - Higher cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, including silent ischaemia
  - Autonomic neuropathy
- Hyperglycaemia and crises: surgery and stress worsen glucose intolerance
  - Surgery and GA trigger a stress response: adrenaline, glucagon, cortisol, growth hormone, and cytokines such as interleukin-6 and TNF-alpha
  - Result: insulin resistance, less peripheral glucose uptake, impaired insulin secretion, more lipolysis and protein breakdown, so hyperglycaemia and sometimes ketosis
  - Sepsis, missed meals, changed feeding, parenteral nutrition and vomiting add to unstable glucose
- Hypoglycaemia
- More infection and poor wound healing
- Airway: aspiration risk, temporomandibular joint stiffness

## Goals of perioperative management
- Optimise glycaemic control
- Avoid hypoglycaemia
- Avoid marked hyperglycaemia; prevent ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar states
- Assess and optimise end-organ complications

## Preoperative assessment
- Type and cause; type 1 has much higher DKA risk and needs basal insulin at all times
- End-organ complications:
  - Cardiovascular: coronary disease, especially silent myocardial infarction
  - Renal: diabetic nephropathy
  - Neurological: autonomic and peripheral neuropathy
  - Airway: stiff joint syndrome (glycosylation of soft tissues)
  - GI: gastroparesis (reflux risk)
- Degree of control: monitoring frequency, average and range of glucose, HbA1c
- Hypoglycaemia: frequency, timing, awareness, severity
- Full treatment history: insulin type, dose and timing; other drugs and timing
- Associated diseases
- Surgery: fasting start, major or minor, timing and length
- Anaesthetic: regional or epidural techniques have little effect on glucose metabolism and insulin resistance

## Investigations
- FBC, urea, electrolytes and creatinine (including glucose), ECG (old infarct raises risk), CXR
- HbA1c if not done in the last 4-6weeks:
  - 8.5-10%: refer to general physician or GP; can proceed with caution
  - >10%: inadequate control, refer to endocrinology; operate only if urgent
  - Higher HbA1c is linked to more postoperative complications
- Non-invasive cardiac testing case by case
- Capillary glucose trend for inpatients

## When to postpone
- Non-urgent surgery with severe hyper- or hypoglycaemia: postpone and refer to endocrinology
- DKA or hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state: postpone

## Fasting management
- Adjust diabetic medicines (below)
- Dextrose 5% containing infusion while nil by mouth
- Sliding-scale rapid-acting insulin (e.g. NovoRapid)
  - Insulin-dependent patients need basal insulin
  - Non-insulin-dependent patients may need basal insulin if fasting is prolonged
  - Major surgery: consider insulin infusion
- Capillary glucose every 4 hours (q4H)
- Morning list, ideally first case

## Adjusting diabetic medicines
- Diet controlled: no change
- OHGAs (non-insulin-dependent):
  - Stop on the morning of surgery
  - Long-acting OHGAs (e.g. metformin): withhold from 24hrs before
  - Prolonged fasting: start basal insulin on the morning of surgery (isophane insulin, e.g. Insulatard, BD 0.1u/kg)
- Insulin:
  - Continue usual basal dose (glargine, detemir, Insulatard); reduce by 25% if having hypos
  - Premixed insulin (Mixtard, NovoMix): give 70% of the total as basal, as Insulatard BD; reduce by 25-50% if total daily dose is very high (>1u/kg/day)
  - Alternative: half the total daily dose as basal
- Alternative to sliding scale: Alberti (glucose-insulin-potassium) regimen
  - Single premixed bag of glucose, insulin and potassium
  - Problems: complex, slow and error-prone to make up; inflexible; tends to over-treat, especially non-insulin-dependent patients

## Useful numbers
- Correction factor (insulin sensitivity): glucose fall from 1 unit of rapid-acting insulin over 2 to 4 hours when fasting or pre-meal
  - 1U lowers glucose by about 2mmol/L (varies widely)
  - Formula: 100/TDD (TDD = total daily dose)
- D50 raises glucose by about 0.1mmol/L/ml

## Glycaemic targets
- Ideal perioperative target unclear beyond avoiding extremes
- Meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (1403 patients with diabetes): intensive control (<120 or <150 mg/dL, i.e. <6.7 or <8.3 mmol/L) versus conventional did not reduce infection, cardiovascular events or death, but caused more hypoglycaemia
- Guideline target for non-critically ill inpatients: 110 to 180 mg/dL (6.1 to 10 mmol/L)
- A looser target (<200 mg/dL, 11 mmol/L) is reasonable depending on hypoglycaemia risk
- UpToDate suggests 110 to 180 mg/dL (6.1 to 10 mmol/L), given hypoglycaemia risk in unmonitored settings

## Intraoperative management
- Target glucose 6.5-10 mmol/L
  - Levels >11 cause glycosuria (dehydration) and impair wound healing
- Check glucose hourly or half-hourly
- Give:
  - Dextrose-containing infusion (D10 if fluid overload is a risk)
  - Continuous insulin infusion, ideally started >2hrs before surgery
  - Starting rate 0.02u/kg/h, or TDD divided by 24
- Hypoglycaemia <4mmol/L: treat with D50
  - Worse with sepsis and liver failure
  - Signs are masked under anaesthesia; can cause permanent brain damage
- Consider RSI if gastroparesis is suspected
- Autonomic neuropathy can worsen hypotension with spinals and epidurals

## Postoperative management
- Capillary glucose in recovery
- D5 infusion and sliding scale
- Keep reduced-dose (fasting) subcutaneous sliding-scale insulin until eating

## Flashcards
- Q: When should SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP1 receptor agonists be stopped before surgery (per these notes)?
  - A: SGLT2 inhibitors 72hrs; GLP1 agonists 1 week.
- Q: What HbA1c level means inadequate control and urgent-only surgery?
  - A: >10%; refer to endocrinology.
- Q: What intraoperative glucose range is targeted?
  - A: 6.5-10 mmol/L; treat <4mmol/L with D50.
- Q: What is the starting rate of an insulin infusion?
  - A: 0.02u/kg/h, or TDD divided by 24.
- Q: Which insulin must never be stopped in type 1 diabetes?
  - A: Basal insulin.
