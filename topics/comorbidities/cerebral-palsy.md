# Cerebral Palsy

Non-progressive early-onset motor disorder; main issues are aspiration, chest infections, seizures, nutrition, positioning and altered relaxant responses.

## Summary of problems
- Airway: poor dental hygiene, neck contractures, TMJ dislocation, aspiration (reflux, bulbar dysfunction)
- Respiratory: restrictive lung disease from scoliosis, chest infections
- Neurological: seizures; sensitive to anaesthetics
- Musculoskeletal: hypothermia, difficult positioning, difficult IV access
- Cognitive impairment

## Background
- Group of non-progressive disorders of movement and posture appearing early in life
- Due to lesions of the motor pathways in the developing brain
- Severity ranges from mild monoplegia to severe spastic quadriplegia
- Often with intellectual impairment, developmental delay, speech problems and epilepsy
- Incidence 2.5:1000
- Causes:
  - Prenatal: congenital infection (rubella, toxoplasmosis, CMV, herpes), cerebral malformation
  - Perinatal: asphyxia
  - Postnatal: IVH, cerebral ischaemia, trauma, non-accidental injury, meningitis
- Linked to prematurity and low birth weight

## Clinical features
- Classified by main motor type:
  - Spastic: hemiplegia, diplegia or quadriplegia
  - Ataxic: poor balance, tremor, hypotonia, uncoordinated movement
  - Dyskinetic (athetoid): involuntary movements (athetosis, dystonia), poor or fluctuating tone
- Some have normal intelligence despite severe deformity, but struggle to communicate
- Muscle contractures and spasms
  - Multidisciplinary care: physiotherapy, splints and drugs to control spasm and delay contractures
- Antispasmodic drugs:
  - Central: baclofen (oral or intrathecal), diazepam, vigabatrin, tizanidine; mainly sedative side effects
  - Peripheral: botulinum toxin type A, dantrolene
    - Botulinum toxin blocks acetylcholine release at the motor end-plate; effect about 3 months
- Progressive scoliosis may impair breathing or sitting
- Reflux and swallowing problems lead to recurrent chest infections

## Common surgery
- Orthopaedic: muscle releases and transfers, foot or femoral osteotomies; femoral and pelvic osteotomies and scoliosis correction for wheelchair seating
- Feeding gastrostomy, fundoplication for reflux, dental rehabilitation

## Anaesthetic management
- Often many previous admissions; review previous anaesthetic records
- Main problems: recurrent chest infections, poor nutrition, seizure control, higher incidence of latex allergy
- Often on anticonvulsants or antispasmodics
- Position carefully and pad pressure areas
- IV access may be hard (scarring, contractures)
- Reflux common: often needs rapid sequence induction
  - Laparoscopic fundoplication reduces postoperative chest complications
- Increased secretions and drooling: antisialogogue may help a gas induction
- Time fasting around feeding times
- Hypothermia in poorly nourished patients: active warming (mattress, forced air, warmed IV fluids)
- Suxamethonium:
  - Patients may be more sensitive, but it is not contraindicated
  - Extra-junctional ACh receptors found in up to 30% in some studies; others show no extra potassium release
- Non-depolarising relaxants:
  - Less potent, shorter acting due to ACh receptor up-regulation
  - Offset clinically by a smaller total body water (patients often relatively dehydrated)
  - Resistance may also relate to anticonvulsants

## Postoperative care
- Pain and muscle spasm can be hard to control
  - Epidurals are very effective but hard to site with scoliosis
  - Regional blocks, morphine infusion, oral benzodiazepines also work
  - IV midazolam may be needed for distressing spasm
- Restart feeds and medication early

## Flashcards
- Q: Incidence of cerebral palsy?
  - A: 2.5:1000
- Q: Is suxamethonium contraindicated in cerebral palsy?
  - A: No, though patients may be more sensitive
- Q: How do non-depolarising relaxants behave in cerebral palsy?
  - A: Less potent and shorter acting (ACh receptor up-regulation, anticonvulsants)
- Q: How long does botulinum toxin last for spasticity?
  - A: About 3 months
- Q: Why is RSI often needed in cerebral palsy?
  - A: Gastro-oesophageal reflux is common
