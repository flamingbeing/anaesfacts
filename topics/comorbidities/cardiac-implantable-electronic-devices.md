# Cardiac Implantable Electronic Devices

Pacemakers, ICDs and CRT: know the device, dependence and EMI risk, then reprogram or use a magnet, minimise interference and recheck afterwards.

## Key points
- Determine device type, indication, defibrillator function and pacing dependence
- Device check within 6 or 12 months (ICD or PPM)
- EMI risk: surgery above the umbilicus
- Pacing dependent or defibrillator with high EMI risk: magnet or reprogramming; otherwise magnet on standby
- As for arrhythmias: K above 4, Mg above 1
- Defib pads anterior-posterior, 8cm away from the device
- Minimise EMI: bipolar, short intermittent low-power bursts, return pad far from the device

## Preoperative assessment
- Multidisciplinary discussion with electrophysiologist or cardiologist and surgeon

### Device factors
- Indication for the device
- Type (PPM or ICD), programmed mode and brand
  - Manufacturer's card or old notes
  - Otherwise CXR
    - PPM: single or dual chamber, uniformly thin leads
    - ICD: single, dual or biventricular; RV lead has 2 thick radiopaque sections (high-voltage shock coils)
    - Biventricular: 3 leads (RA, RV, coronary sinus towards LV)
    - Radiopaque code identifies the manufacturer
- Magnet behaviour (e.g. programmed magnet rate)
  - Micra leadless pacemaker has no reed switch: a magnet cannot make it asynchronous, so it must be reprogrammed

### Device interrogation
- Most recent check: within 1 year for PPM, 6 months for ICD (BJA 2011); otherwise arrange one
  - Consider 3-6 months for CRT (HRS 2011)
- Voltage < 2.6 V or impedance > 3000: ask the manufacturer whether to replace before surgery
- Battery life should be >3 months

### Clinical assessment of function (if interrogation not possible)
- Giddiness, syncope or heart failure suggest malfunction
- Provocative manoeuvres to slow the rate (Valsalva, carotid massage, low-dose edrophonium, esmolol or adenosine) test sensing, pacing and capture
  - Not recommended; only with caution and a backup pacing plan
- ECG: rhythm, pacing spikes, electrical capture
  - Mostly paced rhythm implies dependence

### Pacing dependence
- ECG pacing spikes
- Interrogation: pacing burden (atrial or ventricular paced) > 40%
- Interrogation: no adequate intrinsic ventricular rhythm at the lowest programmable VVI rate

### Defibrillator status
- Any recent anti-tachycardia therapy or shocks

### Investigations
- ECG within 12 months: rhythm, pacing, capture
- CXR if needed: type and brand, generator position, lead integrity, heart failure
  - Lead fractures: at the generator junction, at subclavian vein entry, or over the 1st rib

### Surgical factors
- Discuss EMI with surgeon
  - Is EMI needed (diathermy, MRI, lithotripsy)?
  - Risk during surgery
    - Above the umbilicus is higher risk than below
    - Critical distance 15cm
    - Laparoscopic instruments can come within 15cm despite low incisions
    - Monopolar rather than bipolar
- Can a magnet be applied intraoperatively? Poor access may mean reprogramming

### Patient factors
- Reduce arrhythmia risk: check and correct electrolytes and acid-base
- Assess cardiac disease and function: active cardiac conditions, clinical risk factors, functional capacity

## Intraoperative management

### Device management
- Not pacing dependent, no defibrillator function, or low EMI risk: no reprogramming needed
- High EMI risk or poor access, plus pacing dependence or defibrillator function: reprogram
  - PPM to asynchronous mode (VOO or DOO)
  - Disable ICD shocks and anti-tachycardia pacing
  - Reprogramming does not protect against EMI damage to the device
- Emergency with no reprogramming available: magnet over the device for the whole case
  - Secure with a large transparent dressing (Tegaderm)
  - PPM goes to VOO; defibrillator function is switched off
  - External defibrillator and pads ready as soon as the magnet is on

### Arrhythmia preparation
- Magnet on standby
- Defib pads on; transcutaneous or transvenous pacing and defibrillation ready
  - Pads as far as possible from the generator (at least 15cm)
  - Perpendicular to the leads; anterior-posterior is ideal (UpToDate)

### Minimising interference
- Diathermy
  - Bipolar or harmonic scalpel rather than monopolar
  - Short bursts <5s at low power
  - Cutting rather than coagulation current
  - Diathermy and return plate at least 15cm from the generator; current path perpendicular to the leads
- Avoid artefacts
  - Avoid suxamethonium: fasciculations can be oversensed and inhibit pacing
    - Reduce with precurarisation, a non-depolarising relaxant or VOO mode
  - Prevent shivering: keep warm

### Monitoring
- Continuous ECG and SpO2
- Turn off the ECG artefact filter (diagnostic mode) to see pacing spikes
- Confirm mechanical capture: pulse, SpO2 trace, arterial waveform; watch for oversensing and loss of capture
- Consider arterial line if contractility is poor
- Central or PA catheters may dislodge leads

### General arrhythmia prevention
- Avoid hypoxaemia, hypercarbia, acidosis, low or high potassium, hypothermia and electrolyte imbalance

### Device malfunction or arrhythmia
- Failure may come from loss of capture or EMI; signs depend on the original indication
- Stop surgery and all EMI sources
- Treat the arrhythmia: transcutaneous pacing, defibrillation
- If a magnet is on, consider removing it to restore pacing and anti-tachycardia therapy
- ALS for cardiac arrest
- May need cardiology for transvenous pacing

## Postoperative care
- Device check, interrogation or reprogramming in recovery if
  - Device was reprogrammed (restore settings)
  - Magnet was used
  - Surgery above the umbilicus
  - Haemodynamically demanding surgery (cardiothoracic, major vascular such as AAA)
  - Cardiac arrest, CPR, temporary pacing or external cardioversion during surgery
- Continuous ECG in recovery or HDU until interrogated
- Pacing and defibrillation kit available; leave pads on in recovery
- HDU or telemetry for
  - Major cardiac or aortic surgery
  - Significant events: arrest needing pacing or CPR, VT or VF needing cardioversion
  - Unstable high-risk patients, or devices not restored overnight

## Electromagnetic interference (EMI)
- Electromagnetic radiation that can affect implanted devices

### Sources
- Radiofrequency 0-10^9 Hz and microwaves 10^9-10^11 Hz (written in the note as 0-109 Hz and 109-1011 Hz)
  - Diathermy (monopolar far worse than bipolar), 300-500 kHz
  - Radiofrequency ablation
  - Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy
  - ECT
  - MRI (30-3000 Hz); many MRI-safe pacemaker systems now exist (generator and leads)
  - Evoked potential monitors
  - Nerve stimulators (twitch monitors)
  - TENS (20-110 Hz)
  - External cardioversion and defibrillation
- Physiological: fasciculations (suxamethonium), shivering, large tidal volumes
- Higher-frequency radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, infrared, UV) rarely interferes
  - Repeated or prolonged exposure can damage circuitry or insulation (short circuits)

### Effects
- Malfunction
  - Pacing inhibited by oversensing
  - Inappropriate ICD shocks from oversensing
  - Mode switch or reset to backup mode (usually VVI or VOO); failure to shock when needed
- Permanent damage to circuitry (short circuit, output failure) or leads (threshold changes)
- Damage at the lead tip
  - Leads can carry diathermy energy to the myocardium: stimulation or burns
  - Later scarring changes resistance and can cause device failure

### Reducing EMI
- Device design
  - Bipolar leads: short cathode-anode distance, less pickup
  - Circuit shielding from the metal case
  - Noise algorithms ignore signals outside cardiac frequencies (BJA 2004)
    - Absolute refractory period: after sensed atrial or ventricular activity
    - Noise sampling period: after it, when the atrial lead ignores retrograde P waves
- Surgical technique
  - Bipolar diathermy or harmonic scalpel
  - Minimal power, cutting current
  - Short, intermittent, irregular bursts
  - Return plate at least 15cm from generator; current path perpendicular to leads

## Pacemaker magnets
- Reed switch originally designed to show battery life and pacing thresholds
  - Closing it switches off demand function
    - PPM: asynchronous pacing
    - ICD: shocks disabled; pacing does not become asynchronous
  - Removing the magnet usually restores function
    - PPM resumes its mode
    - ICD may or may not resume shocks
- Risk: asynchronous pacing can compete with intrinsic rhythm, pacing in the vulnerable period and causing arrhythmias (theoretical VF risk)
  - Modern devices time the switch to the next cardiac cycle
- Magnets protect dependent patients from inhibition by pulsed interference, with quick reversion on removal
- Not every device goes into continuous asynchronous mode: confirm magnet response first
  - No change in rate or rhythm
  - Continuous or transient loss of pacing
  - Brief asynchronous pacing (10-64 beats)
  - Asynchronous pacing without rate response
  - Shock delivery disabled
- No effect on Micra leadless pacemakers

## Flashcards
- Q: How recent should a device check be before surgery?
  - A: Within 1 year for PPM, 6 months for ICD; 3-6 months for CRT.
- Q: What pacing burden suggests pacing dependence?
  - A: More than 40% paced beats.
- Q: What distance should diathermy return plates and defib pads keep from the generator?
  - A: At least 15cm.
- Q: What does a magnet do to a PPM and to an ICD?
  - A: PPM: asynchronous pacing. ICD: disables shocks, no asynchronous pacing.
- Q: Which pacemaker does not respond to a magnet?
  - A: Micra leadless pacemaker (no reed switch); it must be reprogrammed.
