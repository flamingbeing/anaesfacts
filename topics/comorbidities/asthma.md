# Asthma

Reversible airway obstruction: optimise first, keep the airway out of reach of light anaesthesia, avoid triggers and allow long expiration.

## Key points
- Assess control and risk (previous ICU admissions)
- Ask about triggers and NSAID tolerance
- Low threshold to postpone for URTI
- Inhalers on call to theatre; salbutamol on standby
- Instrument the airway only when deep
- LMA if possible
- Ketamine is the best induction agent in this setting
- Prolong expiratory time
- Avoid NSAIDs, atracurium, morphine and desflurane
- Beware the silent chest
- Look for a shark-fin capnograph trace

## Treating bronchospasm (summary)
- Deepen anaesthesia
- Salbutamol (Ventolin) 1st line, via spacer
- IV magnesium
- IV ketamine
- IV adrenaline
- Others: steroids, ipratropium, aminophylline, IV salbutamol

## Checklist
- Assess severity and control
- Inhalers and salbutamol before surgery; salbutamol and spacer on standby
- Adequate depth before airway instrumentation
- LMA; no desflurane, NSAIDs or histamine-releasing drugs
- Long expiratory time; beware silent chest
- Smooth emergence with deep suctioning
- Have a bronchospasm plan

## Background
- Chronic reversible airway obstruction from inflammation and hyper-responsiveness
- Episodes of airway oedema, bronchoconstriction, cough and wheeze
- Nocturnal cough may be the only symptom
- Treatment aims to reduce airway inflammation and irritability
- Pointing to asthma rather than COPD
  - Never smoked
  - Childhood symptoms
  - Diurnal variation, worse in the morning
  - Specific triggers such as allergens
  - Reversible with bronchodilator
- Surgical risk
  - More bronchospasm and other respiratory complications
  - Ventilated patients risk air trapping and barotrauma
  - Bronchospasm or laryngospasm during routine surgery in about 2%
- Goals: prevent and treat bronchospasm

## Preoperative assessment
- Assess severity (risk) and how well current treatment works (need for more)
- Optimise therapy; asthma should be well controlled before elective surgery
  - No wheeze and PEFR above 80% of predicted or personal best
  - Consider delaying elective surgery if not
- Severity and control
  - Symptoms: cough, wheeze, breathlessness at rest or on exertion, sputum, nocturnal cough
  - Exercise tolerance reflects severity
  - Reliever use
  - Hospital or ICU admissions mean severe disease
  - Examination: may show wheeze or distress; usually normal when well
- Adherence, especially to controller therapy
- Oral steroids may suppress the HPA axis: replace steroids to prevent adrenal crisis
- Triggers: URTI, cold, pollen, exercise
- NSAID tolerance
  - NSAID hypersensitivity is much more common with asthma and/or chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps
- Recent URTI: low threshold to postpone
- Smokers: advise stopping

## Investigations
- Blood eosinophils often track airway inflammation; airway hyperreactivity indirectly reflects disease activity
- Spirometry before and after bronchodilator may be useful before major surgery
  - FEV1 or FVC below 70% predicted, or FEV1:FVC below 65% predicted: risk factor for respiratory complications
- ABG rarely needed; only if ventilation or oxygenation is in doubt

## Preoperative orders
- Continue anti-inflammatory and bronchodilator therapy up to induction
- A short preoperative oral steroid course may help selected patients
- High-dose systemic steroids in the past 6 months: give stress-dose hydrocortisone or methylprednisolone
- Inhaled steroids alone very rarely suppress the HPA axis

## Intraoperative management
- Most well-controlled asthmatics need no change to the usual technique
- Changes matter mainly for severe asthma or a symptomatic child needing urgent surgery

## Reducing bronchospasm risk
- Inhaled or nebulised beta agonist 30 minutes before surgery
- Airway
  - Avoid airway instrumentation and intubation if possible
  - LMA causes less bronchoconstriction than an ETT; use if no raised aspiration risk
  - Regional anaesthesia is attractive when suitable; high blocks do not cause bronchospasm via sympathetic block
- Adequate depth and analgesia before airway instrumentation
  - Propofol for the stable patient: relaxes airway smooth muscle and lowers resistance
  - Ketamine preferred if haemodynamically unstable
  - Opioids (fentanyl, not morphine) suppress cough and deepen anaesthesia
  - Ventilate with volatile (sevoflurane, not desflurane) for a while after induction to deepen

## Maintenance
- Keep anaesthesia and analgesia adequate
- Humidify and warm gases, especially in exercise-induced asthma (heat loss from the mucosa)
- Drugs
  - Avoid NSAIDs
  - Use sevoflurane instead of TIVA; avoid desflurane
  - Avoid histamine releasers (atracurium, morphine); use fentanyl or oxycodone (OxyNorm)
  - Rocuronium with sugammadex: neostigmine's muscarinic effects can cause bronchospasm
    - In practice this is unpredictable, probably because the co-given anticholinergic protects
- Pre-emptive measures
  - Salbutamol puffs before induction
  - Hydrocortisone 100mg at induction
  - Ketamine, magnesium
- Ventilator: allow enough expiratory time
- Suspect bronchospasm (beware silent chest), anaphylaxis and laryngospasm
- Ensure good analgesia before extubation
- Severe asthma: short-acting drugs to limit postoperative respiratory depression

## Ventilation in bronchospasm
- Give enough time to exhale to prevent air trapping
- Prolonged I:E ratio, 1:3 - 1:5

## Postoperative care
- Monitor for bronchospasm, sputum retention, atelectasis, infection and respiratory failure
- Severe disease: ICU
- Epidural analgesia if indicated
- Titrated O2
- Review steroid dose daily

## Children
- Asthma is common and probably increasing
- Often atopic, with allergies including latex

## Flashcards
- Q: What PEFR target suggests asthma is optimised before elective surgery?
  - A: Above 80% of predicted or personal best, with no wheeze.
- Q: Which spirometry values signal raised respiratory risk?
  - A: FEV1 or FVC below 70% predicted, or FEV1:FVC below 65% predicted.
- Q: Which steroid history needs stress-dose cover?
  - A: High-dose systemic steroids within the past 6 months.
- Q: What I:E ratio is used to prevent air trapping in bronchospasm?
  - A: 1:3 - 1:5.
- Q: Which drugs to avoid in asthma?
  - A: NSAIDs, atracurium, morphine, desflurane.
