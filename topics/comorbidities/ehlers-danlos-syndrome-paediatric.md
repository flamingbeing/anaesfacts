# Ehlers-Danlos syndrome in children

Collagen disorders with fragile tissues, bleeding and joint laxity; vascular type (IV) carries rupture risk and needs group and crossmatch, gentle airway and vascular handling, and no NSAIDs or IM injections.

## Background
- Heterogeneous genetic group caused by collagen mutations
- Six recognised subtypes (by severity, inheritance and molecular defect)
  - Classical (EDS I, II) and hypermobile (EDS III): 90%
  - Vascular (EDS IV): 3-10%
  - Kyphoscoliosis, arthrochalasis, dermatosparaxis: very rare
- Diagnosis mainly clinical
- Main features: easy bruising, tissue fragility, hyperextensible skin, poor healing with atrophic scars, joint hypermobility (Beighton score 4/9 or more)

## Systemic features
- Cardiovascular: none to arterial aneurysm, arterial rupture without aneurysm, varicose veins, aortic regurgitation, mitral valve prolapse, coronary artery disease (friable endothelium), conduction defects
- Spontaneous pneumothorax
- Bowel or bladder diverticula; megaoesophagus, megatrachea, megacolon
- Diaphragmatic, umbilical and inguinal hernias common

## Subtypes
- Classic (I/II): very soft, velvety skin
- Hypermobile (III): joint hypermobility and recurrent dislocation dominate over skin changes
- Vascular (IV): most severe; only type with increased risk of death
  - Type III collagen mutation
  - Thin translucent skin; joint hypermobility and skin stretch unusual
  - Major vessels and viscera more affected
  - Early signs: premature rupture of membranes in the mother, congenital club foot, congenital hip dislocation
  - Arterial tear, dissection or fistula; mostly medium thoracic and abdominal arteries
  - Hollow viscus rupture (bowel, uterus); high-risk pregnancy, preterm delivery from cervical insufficiency or fragile membranes

## General management
- Baseline echo with aortic diameter before age 10 years; follow up abnormal results
- Avoid drugs that impair platelet function
- Prefer ultrasound or subtraction angiography to invasive arteriography or catheterisation (rupture risk)
- Avoid surgery where possible; if needed, handle vessels and tissues gently
- Counsel women with type IV about uterine rupture, bleeding and other pregnancy complications

## Preoperative assessment
- Establish the subtype (especially type IV vascular)
- FBC, PT and APTT
- Type IV: group and crossmatch; be ready for rapid transfusion
- Assess the cervical spine for atlantoaxial instability (ligament laxity)

## Intraoperative management
- Positioning: pad and protect joints and skin; avoid tourniquets
- Vascular access:
  - Avoid multiple attempts; prophylactic pressure dressings
  - Avoid central and arterial lines if possible; otherwise use ultrasound
  - Stretchy skin can hide extravasation
- Airway:
  - Gentle mask ventilation: high risk of temporomandibular joint dislocation
  - Fragile tissue bleeds with repeated intubation attempts
  - Smaller ETT may reduce tracheal mucosal damage; check cuff pressure carefully
  - Collapse of fibro-elastic tissue and tracheal rings can make intubation hard
  - Consider subclinical cervical spine instability even with normal neck and jaw movement
- General vs regional: no clear recommendation
  - Local anaesthetics (including EMLA) may have reduced effect
- Bleeding: no NSAIDs, no IM injections; treat bleeding aggressively; expect sudden haemorrhage
- Avoid hypertension (arterial dissection risk)
- Keep airway pressures low (pneumothorax risk)
- Good antiemetic prophylaxis: vomiting is linked to spontaneous oesophageal rupture

## Flashcards
- Q: Which EDS type has increased mortality and what collagen is affected?
  - A: Vascular type (IV); type III collagen
- Q: What Beighton score indicates joint hypermobility?
  - A: 4/9 or greater
- Q: Which drugs and injections should be avoided in EDS?
  - A: NSAIDs and other antiplatelet drugs; IM injections
- Q: Why keep airway pressures low in EDS?
  - A: Risk of pneumothorax
- Q: Why give good antiemetic prophylaxis in EDS?
  - A: Vomiting is linked to spontaneous oesophageal rupture
