# The Syndromic Child

Many congenital syndromes bring a difficult airway (often worsening or improving with age) plus cardiac and spinal anomalies to assess.

## Difficult airway by anatomical site

- Nasopharynx: mucopolysaccharidoses
- Oral cavity and oropharynx
  - Macroglossia: trisomy 21, Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, mucopolysaccharidoses
  - Retrognathia: DiGeorge
- Mandible and maxilla: Pierre Robin sequence, Treacher Collins, Goldenhar, Apert
- Pharynx and larynx: trisomy 21
- Trachea: trisomy 21, mucopolysaccharidoses
- Cervical spine: trisomy 21, Klippel-Feil, Goldenhar, mucopolysaccharidoses

## Pierre Robin sequence

- Triad: micrognathia (small mandible), glossoptosis (tongue base falls back and down), airway obstruction
  - Airway obstruction is present in all and is needed for the diagnosis
- Cleft palate is common but not universal
- Usually isolated, but up to 60% have an associated syndrome
  - Commonest: Stickler, velocardiofacial (22q 11.2 deletion), fetal alcohol syndrome, Treacher Collins
  - Murmur (especially with a syndrome): echocardiogram before surgery
- Mild obstruction relieved by prone position or jaw thrust; severe cases may already have an NPA or ETT
- Improves with age as the mandible grows (unlike Treacher Collins)

### Anaesthetic considerations

- Common procedures: DLB, TLA, mandibular distraction osteogenesis, tracheostomy, imaging, gastrostomy, Nissen fundoplication
- Main problems: airway obstruction, difficult intubation, difficult mask ventilation
  - Risk of intraoperative and postoperative respiratory complications
- Gauge the degree of obstruction and look for OSA (predicts respiratory complications)
- Plan as for a known paediatric difficult airway
- Sedative premedication relatively contraindicated
- GA may be induced before securing the airway, but spontaneous ventilation must be kept; this may be very hard or impossible without adjuncts
- Standard maintenance
- Postoperative
  - Main risk is airway obstruction, also from postoperative airway oedema (surgery often involves the airway)
  - With OSA: reduce opioid doses; monitored bed with continuous pulse oximetry (e.g. high dependency)

## Fetal alcohol syndrome

- Features: microcephaly, developmental delay, hyperactivity; maxillary hypoplasia, micrognathia, short neck; VSD
- Anaesthetic concerns
  - May be difficult to ventilate and intubate
  - Preoperative echo may be needed
  - May be uncooperative

## Treacher Collins syndrome (mandibulofacial dysostosis)

- Autosomal dominant; abnormal development of the first branchial arch; symmetrical malformations
- Nager syndrome gives similar anaesthetic problems

### Clinical features

- Hypoplastic mandible and zygomatic bones, down-slanting palpebral fissures, lower eyelid colobomas, missing medial eyelashes
- Abnormal pinnae, ear canals and ossicles: conductive deafness common
- High-arched or cleft palate, macroglossia, dental malocclusion
- Associated: congenital heart disease, intellectual disability, skeletal anomalies
- Chronic upper airway obstruction and apnoea (awake, asleep or under anaesthesia)

### Anaesthetic management

- Mask ventilation harder than appearance suggests
- Unlike Pierre Robin, airway management and especially intubation get harder with age (poor mandibular growth)
- Sedative premedication relatively contraindicated (obstruction risk); antisialogogue helps at induction
- Inhalational induction may be difficult; an assistant may need to pull the mandible and tongue forward
- Expect difficult intubation unless a previous laryngoscopy view is known; fibreoptic intubation often needed
- Awake extubation
- High dependency monitoring after surgery (frequent respiratory complications)

## Velocardiofacial syndrome (DiGeorge, 22q11 deletion, CATCH-22)

- May need irradiated blood products
  - Transfused lymphocytes can engraft and cause transfusion-associated graft versus host disease; irradiation destroys them

## Goldenhar syndrome (oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum)

- Also called hemifacial microsomia or oculoauriculovertebral dysplasia
- Defect of first and second branchial arch structures: asymmetric craniofacial anomalies
- May include cardiac, vertebral and CNS defects
- Usually sporadic; rarely autosomal dominant

### Clinical features

- One-sided underdevelopment of malar bone, mandible and maxilla
- Epibulbar dermoids; congenital heart disease (VSD, TGA, tetralogy of Fallot); vertebral anomalies (40%)
- Ear on the affected side absent or misshapen; ear canal atresia, preauricular tags, pretragal fistulae; one-sided deafness common
- Upper eyelid coloboma common; extraocular muscle defects, cataract, iris atrophy
- Cleft lip and/or palate
- Often, not always, low IQ
- Asymmetry may worsen with age
- Surgery: ear reconstruction, eye surgery, mandibular advancement

### Anaesthetic management

- Airway is the main issue; may get harder with growth as asymmetry increases
- Asymmetry can make a face-mask seal difficult
- Laryngoscopy and intubation may be difficult, especially if the right side is affected
- Options used: fibreoptic intubation, retrograde intubation, tracheostomy
- Assess congenital heart disease and craniovertebral anomalies

## Flashcards

- Q: Pierre Robin triad?
  - A: Micrognathia, glossoptosis, airway obstruction
- Q: How does the airway change with age in Pierre Robin versus Treacher Collins?
  - A: Pierre Robin improves as the mandible grows; Treacher Collins gets harder
- Q: Blood product need in DiGeorge (22q11 deletion)?
  - A: Irradiated products to prevent transfusion-associated graft versus host disease
- Q: Proportion of Pierre Robin with an associated syndrome?
  - A: Up to 60%
- Q: Premedication in syndromic difficult airways?
  - A: Sedative premedication relatively contraindicated
