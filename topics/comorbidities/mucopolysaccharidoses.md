# Mucopolysaccharidoses

Inherited lysosomal enzyme deficiencies causing GAG build-up in connective tissue; the airway is the main anaesthetic problem and gets harder with age.

## Background

- Missing lysosomal enzymes cannot break down glycosaminoglycans (GAGs); part-degraded GAGs build up in connective tissue cells and disrupt their function
- Features depend on which enzyme is missing and where GAGs deposit
- Deposition is lifelong, so features worsen with age (except Sanfilippo)
- Shortened life expectancy and reduced quality of life
- Autosomal recessive, except Hunter (X-linked)
- Classified by enzyme defect, not by clinical picture
- Present on ENT, orthopaedic, neuroimaging, neurosurgical, ophthalmic and general surgical (hernia) lists

## Types and clinical features

### MPS I (iduronidase deficiency)
- Three variants: Hurler (MPS I H, "gargoylism", most severe), Scheie (MPS I S, mildest), Hurler-Scheie (MPS I HS)
- Hurler syndrome (1:100000 live births)
  - Coarse features, short stature, short neck, frontal bossing, hypertelorism, flat nasal bridge, macroglossia, gum hypertrophy
  - Difficult airway (see below)
  - ENT: obstructive sleep apnoea, deafness
  - Cardiac disease very common: cardiomyopathy, thickened septum and mitral and aortic valves, coronary artery disease
  - CNS: progressive intellectual decline, hydrocephalus, corneal clouding
  - Hepatosplenomegaly, protuberant abdomen, frequent inguinal and umbilical hernias
  - Usually die in the first decade from cardiorespiratory failure
- Scheie syndrome (1:600000 live births): much milder
  - Joint stiffness, corneal clouding, normal intelligence
  - May have coarse features, excess body hair, macroglossia, glaucoma, mitral and/or aortic regurgitation when older
  - Present with orthopaedic problems: carpal tunnel, stiff joints, back pain, spondylolisthesis
  - Normal life expectancy
- Hurler-Scheie: extremely rare, intermediate severity, may survive into adulthood

### MPS II: Hunter syndrome
- Rare; X-linked recessive; 1:140000 male births; mostly Caucasian
- Severe form: like a milder Hurler with less skeletal disease; progressive neurodegeneration to a vegetative state and death in the teens
- Mild form: risk of cervical cord compression from thickened dura and ligamentum flavum
  - MRI of the craniocervical junction before airway manipulation to assess compression and stability (reduces risk of quadriplegia)

### MPS III: Sanfilippo syndrome (types A to D)
- Commonest MPS in the UK
- Mild dysmorphism
- Severe intellectual disability with aggressive, hyperactive behaviour
- Death in the teens

### MPS IV: Morquio syndrome (A and B)
- Rare; normal intelligence
- Aortic regurgitation in adults
- Severe bone dysplasia: dwarfism, kyphosis (restrictive lung disease), pigeon chest
- High risk of cervical cord compression with minor trauma: hypoplastic, poorly ossified dens and weak ligaments
  - Frequent MRIs; often prophylactic occipitocervical fusion
  - Lower spine may also be unstable
- Survival depends on neurological and cardiorespiratory (restrictive lung) complications

### MPS VI: Maroteaux-Lamy syndrome
- Rare; very variable severity; normal intelligence
- Severe, rapidly progressive form (most patients, early onset)
  - Coarse facies, atlanto-axial instability, difficult intubation
  - Cardiomyopathy
  - Corneal clouding
  - Joint and skeletal abnormalities
  - Progressive airway narrowing, cor pulmonale, death in the early twenties
- Attenuated, slowly progressive form
  - Short stature, carpal tunnel syndrome, hip dysplasia
  - Corneal clouding typical; facial dysmorphism mild or absent

### MPS VII: beta-glucuronidase deficiency
- Extremely rare

## Airway

- Commonest major anaesthetic problem in MPS
- Hurler, Hunter and Maroteaux-Lamy: often difficult to maintain and to intubate; worsens with age
  - Fibreoptic intubation often needed
- Features
  - Short neck, small high larynx
  - Limited atlanto-axial movement
  - Thickened mouth, tongue and pharyngeal tissues; small hypopharynx
  - Large, fairly fixed epiglottis
  - Copious secretions
- Morquio: atlanto-axial instability
- Recent anaesthetic records help; airway imaging is sometimes available
- Discuss unplanned tracheostomy as a possible outcome
- Guedel airways may make the airway worse; a nasal airway may be better

## Respiratory

- Obstructive sleep apnoea; may improve at first with adenotonsillectomy
- Recurrent upper and lower respiratory infections
- Copious secretions
- Progressive narrowing of the distal airways

## Cardiovascular

- GAG deposits in valves, septum or coronary arteries; hard to detect because patients have poor mobility and communication
- May present with cardiomyopathy, heart failure, arrhythmias, ischaemia or sudden death

## Flashcards
- Q: What is the commonest major anaesthetic problem in MPS?
  - A: The airway; difficult mask ventilation and intubation that worsens with age
- Q: Which MPS is X-linked?
  - A: Hunter syndrome (MPS II)
- Q: Which MPS types have atlanto-axial instability?
  - A: Morquio (MPS IV) and Maroteaux-Lamy (MPS VI)
- Q: Which MPS does not worsen with age and is commonest in the UK?
  - A: Sanfilippo (MPS III)
- Q: Which oral adjunct may worsen the airway in MPS?
  - A: Guedel airway; a nasal airway may be better
