# Chronic Liver Disease and Cirrhosis

Multi-system disease: grade severity (Child-Pugh, MELD), protect hepatic blood flow and oxygen delivery, and adjust drugs for altered handling.

## Key points
- Grade severity with Child-Pugh score; review complications system by system
- Liver functions to remember:
  - Breakdown: bilirubin, ammonia
  - Synthesis: albumin, clotting factors (coagulopathy), glucose
- Portal hypertension: ascites (spontaneous bacterial peritonitis), hypersplenism, varices
- Reduced drug clearance
- Pringle manoeuvre: manual occlusion of the hepatoduodenal ligament (pedicle) to limit hepatic inflow; can also be done with a clamp or a tightened silicone tube
- Varices: TEE contraindicated; pass NG tubes carefully

## Regional anaesthesia
- Advantages:
  - Opioid sparing
  - Fewer respiratory complications
  - Increased hepatic blood flow
- Disadvantages:
  - Coagulopathy may rule it out
    - Postoperative liver dysfunction can cause coagulopathy that affects catheter removal
  - Neuraxial block worsens vasodilation and hypotension
  - LAST risk, especially with long infusions, as local anaesthetics are metabolised by the liver

## Perioperative risk
- Child C or MELD > 14: high mortality (>50%); avoid intervention unless life-saving
- Child B or MELD > 8-14: higher risk; weigh the indication for surgery carefully
- Child A or MELD ≤ 8: low perioperative mortality
- Child-Pugh points are summed:
  - 5 or 6 = class A: low operative mortality (<5%)
  - 7–9 = class B: moderate (25%)
  - 10–15 = class C: high (>50%)
- First used for portosystemic shunt surgery; its variables also predict outcome in all abdominal surgery in liver disease
- Other poor prognostic factors: malnutrition, emergency surgery, sepsis, blood loss

## Gastrointestinal effects
- Portal hypertension (portal pressure >10 mm Hg): collateral shunts, varices, ascites, splenomegaly
  - Treatment: transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS)
- **Gastric and oesophageal varices**
  - Can bleed **catastrophically**
  - Even small GI bleeds can trigger or worsen **encephalopathy**, as digested blood greatly raises the bilirubin load on the liver
  - Treatment: non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol), band ligation or glue, octreotide or terlipressin for acute bleeds
  - **Careful NG tube insertion; TEE may be contraindicated**
- **Splenomegaly**: platelet sequestration causing **thrombocytopaenia**
- **Ascites**
  - Massive ascites raises **intra-abdominal pressure**:
    - **Aspiration risk**
    - Splints the diaphragm, reduces FRC, causes atelectasis
  - Treatment: **sodium and water restriction**, with or without **paracentesis**
    - Large-volume paracentesis (>5L) drops intra-abdominal pressure fast; blood pools in the abdomen, preload falls and hypotension follows
    - **Give 100ml 20% HAS per 2.5L of ascites drained** (same for liver transplant with an abdominal drain)
- Delayed gastric emptying adds to aspiration risk

## Cardiovascular effects
- **Hyperdynamic circulation**: high CO, low SVR, wide pulse pressure
  - From portosystemic shunting and poor clearance of vasodilators (nitric oxide)
- **Cirrhotic cardiomyopathy**: hidden systolic and diastolic dysfunction and long QT, masked by low SVR and unmasked by surgical stress or clamping

## Respiratory effects
- **Hepatopulmonary syndrome**: intrapulmonary arteriovenous shunting
  - Sign: **platypnoea-orthodeoxia** (breathlessness and hypoxaemia worse **sitting up**, better lying flat)
  - Test: echo with **agitated saline bubble study**
- **Portopulmonary hypertension**: true pulmonary hypertension (high PVR, normal PCWP) caused by portal hypertension; high perioperative mortality

## Haematological effects
- Thrombocytopaenia from hypersplenism
- Poor clotting factor synthesis causes **coagulopathy**, mainly the **vitamin K**-dependent factors II, VII, IX and X; PT and APTT prolonged
- **Anaemia**: GI blood loss, hypersplenism, haemolysis, malnutrition

## Renal and electrolyte effects
- Any acute worsening of liver function can cause **hepatorenal syndrome**
  - Causes: renal hypoperfusion, portal hypertension, intra-abdominal hypertension, nephrotoxins
  - May need postoperative haemodialysis; poor prognosis
- GA vasodilation can cause renal hypoperfusion and pre-renal failure
- Secondary hyperaldosteronism: water retention and hyponatraemia, leading to ascites and peripheral oedema
  - Loop diuretics can cause relative hypovolaemia and hypokalaemia
  - Spironolactone can cause hyperkalaemia

## Endocrine and metabolic effects
- Low liver and muscle glycogen: perioperative **hypoglycaemia**
- Muscle wasting from poor protein synthesis and malnutrition

## Neurological effects
- **Hepatic encephalopathy**
  - Major feature of acute liver failure; graded by conscious level
  - Deep coma reflects cerebral oedema and raised ICP; grave prognosis
  - In chronic disease, triggered by infection, GI bleeding, electrolyte or acid-base upset, sedatives, hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, hypotension or high protein intake; or may reflect slow progression

## Underlying cause
- Alcoholic liver disease and haemosiderosis are linked with cardiomyopathy
- Alcoholic patients with thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency risk Wernicke's encephalopathy

## Preoperative assessment
- Focus on **how severe the liver dysfunction is** and **its effects on other organs**
- Establish hepatitis B or C status (risk to theatre staff); treat hepatitis of unknown cause as infectious
- History and examination:
  - Compensated disease may be silent or give vague symptoms (malaise, weight loss, dyspepsia); signs may be absent
  - Look for signs of advanced disease: muscle wasting, spider naevi, pleural effusions, ascites, splenomegaly, encephalopathy
- Investigations:
  - **FBC**: anaemia, thrombocytopaenia, raised white cells with infection
  - **Renal function**; correct severe **hyponatraemia** or **potassium** problems before surgery
    - Severe hyponatraemia may explain altered consciousness
    - Correct slowly, <10 mmol litre−1 in 24 h, to avoid central pontine myelinolysis
  - **PT** is the best test of synthetic function
    - Prognostic in acute liver failure and after surgery in chronic disease
    - Also raised by vitamin K deficiency, DIC and warfarin
    - Give vitamin K for several days before surgery where possible
  - **Albumin** is also useful
  - Raised bilirubin is common; enzyme pattern depends on the cause
  - ECG; echo if risk of LV dysfunction, cardiomyopathy, valve disease or pulmonary vascular disease; exercise ECG or dynamic LV assessment if significant coronary disease suspected
  - **Chest X-ray** or ultrasound for **pleural effusions** needing drainage; lung function tests for restrictive or obstructive disease

## Optimisation and fitness for surgery
- Elective surgery only if chronic liver disease is **well compensated**
- Emergency surgery: **urgent optimisation** of volume status, coagulation, neurological state and infection screen
- **Targeted blood components** guided by **thromboelastography** and clotting tests:
  - FFP if PT >1.5 times control
  - Platelets if count <50 000 mm−3
  - Cryoprecipitate usually only if fibrinogen <1.0 g litre−1
  - Recombinant factor VIIa increasingly used for prevention and treatment of bleeding; also tranexamic acid and desmopressin
- **Avoid sedative premedication** (may precipitate encephalopathy); H2 antagonist (e.g. ranitidine) premedication advisable
- Decision by severity:
  - Child-Pugh A or B: proceed; monitor and treat encephalopathy, coagulopathy, metabolic and electrolyte problems
  - Child-Pugh C: prefer non-surgical options; defer necessary elective surgery until improved
  - Acute hepatic dysfunction: defer elective surgery until improved
- Contraindications to elective surgery:
  - Acute alcoholic hepatitis
  - Acute viral hepatitis
  - Child-Pugh C cirrhosis
  - Fulminant hepatic failure
  - Severe chronic hepatitis
  - Severe coagulopathy (**PT prolonged >3 seconds** despite vitamin K; **platelets <50,000/mm3**)
  - Severe extrahepatic complications
  - Acute renal failure
  - Cardiomyopathy, heart failure
  - Hypoxaemia

## Intraoperative management
- Goal: **maintain hepatic blood flow and oxygen delivery**
  - Hypoperfusion or hypoxaemia can injure hepatocytes and cause decompensation
  - With portal hypertension, **liver supply depends on hepatic arterial flow**
  - **All anaesthetic techniques** can lower MAP and so hepatic blood flow
  - Other factors that reduce hepatic flow: **surgical traction, positive pressure ventilation, hypocapnia, alpha agonists, laparoscopy**
- Monitoring for major surgery:
  - Arterial and central venous pressure; arterial line for gases, lactate, glucose, electrolytes and coagulation
  - Core temperature, neuromuscular monitoring, urine output
  - TEE can help, but **probe placement is contraindicated with oesophageal varices**, as are NG tubes and oesophageal temperature probes; a PA catheter may be needed instead
- Drugs: careful use matters more than choice
  - Suggested: IV induction with propofol and remifentanil; usually modified **RSI** with cricoid pressure and rocuronium 1 mg kg−1; maintain with oxygen, air, desflurane and remifentanil infusion
  - Propofol TCI is an alternative, useful for endoscopy and radiology; propofol clearance is not much affected by liver disease
  - **Atracurium** preferred for maintenance of neuromuscular block
- Fluids and circulation:
  - Large-bore IV access is mandatory; warm all fluids; rapid infuser for major surgery
  - Guide fluids by haemodynamics, blood loss and urine output
  - Use vasopressors (metaraminol, phenylephrine, noradrenaline) for hypotension only once filling is optimised
  - Keep circulating volume and haemodynamics right to maintain urine output; loop diuretics or mannitol occasionally
  - Antioxidants for renal protection remain experimental
  - Colloids may work better than crystalloids with ascites
  - Background 5−10% dextrose at 50–100 ml h−1 prevents hypoglycaemia and guards against rises in plasma sodium

## Pharmacology in liver disease
- IV induction agents:
  - Thiopental: reduce dose (more unbound drug from low plasma proteins; longer distribution half-life and action)
  - Propofol: more sensitive to sedative and cardiorespiratory depression; reduce dose
  - Etomidate: safe, little advantage over thiopental
  - Chronic alcohol use may raise requirements; use all IV agents with care
- Neuromuscular blockers:
  - Suxamethonium: slower breakdown from low pseudocholinesterase, rarely a problem in practice
  - Apparent resistance to non-depolarising blockers (larger volume of distribution or altered protein binding)
  - Vecuronium and rocuronium: prolonged elimination in severe disease
  - Atracurium and cisatracurium: suitable, not reliant on the liver
    - Laudanosine (seizure risk) is lower with cisatracurium because of its higher potency; unlikely to matter clinically
  - Always monitor neuromuscular function
- Opioids:
  - Morphine: slower elimination (lower hepatic flow and extraction); morphine-6-glucuronide accumulates in renal failure; best avoided in decompensated disease (may trigger encephalopathy)
  - Fentanyl: fine in low doses intraoperatively (no active metabolite, renally excreted); accumulates with large or repeated doses
  - Alfentanil: slower elimination, larger volume of distribution, less protein binding (low alpha-1-acid glycoprotein)
  - Remifentanil: ideal; broken down by tissue and red cell esterases, which are preserved in severe liver disease (unlike plasma esterases)
- Volatile agents:
  - All lower CO and MAP, and so liver blood flow
  - Isoflurane, sevoflurane and desflurane have minimal hepatic metabolism and are safe
  - Desflurane is probably best: least metabolised, fastest emergence, relatively preserves hepatic blood flow (little effect on the hepatic arterial buffer response) and CO

## Postoperative analgesia
- IV PCA with fentanyl (sometimes morphine) is well tolerated in compensated disease
  - Fentanyl bolus 10 mcg, lockout 10 min, no background infusion
- Regional analgesia reduces systemic needs; check coagulation first
  - TAP blocks or local infiltration recommended
  - Epidural only with extreme caution, and only if INR is <1.5 and platelets >100 000 mm−3
  - Clotting factors are not routinely given just to allow an epidural
- IM or SC injections risk haematoma
- NSAIDs not recommended (GI bleeding, platelet dysfunction, nephrotoxicity)
- Paracetamol not contraindicated; use with care and monitor liver function

## Postoperative care
- Plan ICU for advanced liver disease
- Postoperative ventilation sometimes appropriate, but usually stop sedation early so neurology can be assessed
- Signs of decompensation: worsening encephalopathy, jaundice, ascites
- Continue invasive monitoring and careful fluids to avoid renal failure
- Keep monitoring coagulation and watch for bleeding
- Remove intravascular catheters early (higher catheter sepsis risk)

## Flashcards
- Q: How much albumin is given during large-volume paracentesis?
  - A: 100ml of 20% HAS per 2.5L of ascites drained
- Q: What Child-Pugh scores define classes A, B and C, and their operative mortality?
  - A: A 5-6 (<5%), B 7–9 (25%), C 10–15 (>50%)
- Q: What INR and platelet count are needed before an epidural in liver disease?
  - A: INR <1.5 and platelets >100 000 mm−3
- Q: What PCA regimen suits compensated liver disease?
  - A: Fentanyl bolus 10 mcg, lockout 10 min, no background infusion
- Q: What is the classic sign of hepatopulmonary syndrome?
  - A: Platypnoea-orthodeoxia: worse sitting up, better lying flat
