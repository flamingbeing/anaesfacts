# Chronic Kidney Disease and Dialysis

Check fluid status, potassium, dialysis timing and drug handling; protect remaining renal function and fistulas.

## Summary
- CKD (not on dialysis): protect renal function, adjust drug doses
- ESRF: fluid status, electrolytes and potassium, creatinine, pharmacokinetics, anaemia, IHD risk
- Haemodialysis: timing and events of last dialysis, fluid and electrolytes after it, fistula care
- Peritoneal dialysis: drain the fluid, check fluid and electrolytes, haemodialysis if needed

## Causes and complications
- Causes: diabetes, hypertension
- Acute complications: AEIOU (acidosis, electrolytes, intoxication, overload, uraemia)
- Chronic complications: IHD, renal bone disease, anaemia

## Main considerations
- Treatment: fluid restriction; renal replacement therapy changes the acute complications
  - Haemodialysis: dialyse the day before; check fluid and electrolytes afterwards; fistula care
  - Peritoneal dialysis: drain, check fluid and electrolytes, haemodialysis if needed
- Anaesthetic: altered drug pharmacokinetics; difficult venous access
- Optimisation: correct fluid status, electrolytes and uraemia; transfuse for anaemia

## CKD not on dialysis: preoperative assessment
- Renal function: KDIGO G1–G5 by eGFR; look at the trend
- Identify those at high risk of perioperative renal failure or needing dialysis
- Complications:
  - Fluid overload: know the fluid restriction target; may instead be dry from over-diuresis
  - Hyperkalaemia
  - Metabolic acidosis
  - Uraemia
- Comorbidities: diabetes, hypertension
- Cardiovascular risk: accelerated atherosclerosis, LVH, autonomic neuropathy
  - Cr >2mg/dL (177umol/L) raises MACE risk and is part of the RCRI (or eGFR <30)
  - Silent ischaemia is common: low threshold for ECG and functional testing

## CKD not on dialysis: intraoperative management
- Protect the kidneys and avoid acute-on-chronic kidney injury:
  - Balance fluids: avoid both dehydration and overload; goal-directed fluid therapy with dynamic measures (stroke volume variation)
  - Maintain MAP for renal perfusion
  - Avoid nephrotoxins: NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, IV contrast
- Aspiration risk: RSI if diabetic gastroparesis or symptomatic uraemia
- Adjust doses of renally cleared drugs
  - Avoid morphine and pethidine
  - Rocuronium: sugammadex use is controversial
  - Suxamethonium acceptable if K <5
- Watch for hyperkalaemia with transfusion

## ESRF and dialysis: preoperative assessment
- Creatinine, eGFR and trend: is dialysis adequate?
- Screen for and correct complications:
  - Fluid overload: breathlessness, basal crackles, raised JVP, ankle oedema, hypertension
    - Fluid restriction target; dialysis regimen, site, dry weight, last session
    - May instead be fluid depleted from dialysis
  - Hyperkalaemia: aim K <5.0-5.5; recheck 4 hours after dialysis if unstable or concerned
  - Metabolic acidosis: check bicarbonate
  - Anaemia: target Hb 9-10g/dL with EPO
  - Renal bone disease: hypocalcaemia, hyperphosphataemia
  - Uraemic platelet dysfunction: consider desmopressin if bleeding risk is high
  - Uraemic neuropathy
- Comorbidities: diabetes, hypertension, IHD or MI (high MACE risk; part of the RCRI)

## ESRF: induction
- Most IV induction agents (propofol, etomidate, thiopental) are safe
- Thiopental: larger volume of distribution and less protein binding, so reduce the dose
- Many patients behave as if hypovolaemic at induction; uraemia and antihypertensives make hypotension more likely
- Induction agents may have exaggerated CNS effects (uraemia disrupts the blood-brain barrier)
- RSI if indicated:
  - Suxamethonium can be given if potassium is less than 5 mmol/L; potassium release is not exaggerated in CKD
  - Or rocuronium for fast onset

## ESRF: maintenance
- Volatile elimination does not depend on the kidneys
  - Some avoid sevoflurane (fluoride, compound A), though there is no evidence of extra renal harm in renal disease; some avoid it for long cases
  - Volatiles can depress cardiac output too much
  - TIVA is an option
- Non-depolarising relaxants: choose by clearance route
  - Reduce the first dose and guide later doses with a nerve stimulator
  - Vecuronium and rocuronium may be excreted more slowly; mivacurium, atracurium and cisatracurium clearance does not depend on the kidneys
  - Laudanosine (from atracurium and cisatracurium) may accumulate; no neuromuscular effect, but high levels can cause seizures
- Blunted sympathetic activity limits compensatory vasoconstriction: small blood loss, starting IPPV, sudden position change or myocardial depression can drop BP sharply

## ESRF: analgesia
- Opioids are useful: no myocardial depression and less volatile needed; choose those without active metabolites or renal excretion
  - Morphine and pethidine (meperidine) form neurotoxic metabolites cleared by the kidney (morphine-3-glucuronide, norpethidine)
  - Morphine-6-glucuronide is more potent than morphine and can accumulate, causing profound respiratory depression
  - Hydromorphone-3-glucuronide can accumulate; hydromorphone usable with monitoring and dose reduction
  - Fentanyl has no active metabolites, but its elimination half-life may be longer
  - Alfentanil, remifentanil and sufentanil have no active metabolites
- Avoid NSAIDs: worsen hypertension and oedema, raise cardiovascular risk

## ESRF: fluids and urine output
- Give fluids carefully: overload risk
  - If still passing urine, aim for at least 0.5 mL/kg/h
- Use Hartmann's (contains 4 mEq/L potassium) and other potassium-containing fluids with caution; normal saline is an option but risks hyperchloraemic acidosis
  - Best maintenance fluid is debated: 0.9% saline has no potassium but can worsen acidosis

## ESRF: acid-base, monitoring and bleeding
- Avoid hypercapnia: respiratory acidosis worsens acidaemia, depresses the circulation and raises potassium
- Consider an arterial line
- CVP often very helpful for fluids, as even mild renal impairment tolerates volume poorly; right atrial or pulmonary artery pressure chosen by cardiopulmonary disease
- TOE or FloTrac are alternatives
- Uraemic platelet dysfunction can increase blood loss

## ESRF: emergence
- With renally excreted relaxants (e.g. rocuronium), monitor and reverse in all
- Kidneys clear about 50% of neostigmine and about 75% of edrophonium and pyridostigmine
  - Their half-lives lengthen more than the relaxants', so recurarisation is less likely

## ESRF: postoperative management
- Check electrolytes, urea, creatinine and haematocrit
- Chest X-ray if pulmonary oedema is a concern
- Consider uraemic coagulopathy if bleeding
- Fentanyl PCA rather than morphine PCA

## Haemodialysis: preoperative assessment
- Dialysis regimen, access site, dry weight, residual urine output
- Optimise dialysis adequacy:
  - Fluid status (may be depleted after dialysis)
  - Hyperkalaemia: aim K <5.0-5.5; recheck 4 hours after dialysis if unstable or concerned
  - Electrolytes
- Last dialysis: best 12-24hr before elective surgery; arrange it in advance
  - Note recent dialysis problems (e.g. hypotension)
  - Wait 4-6h after haemodialysis before surgery, for fluid equilibration and heparin metabolism
  - Recheck electrolytes 4hrs after dialysis
  - Risk of dialysis disequilibrium syndrome

## Haemodialysis: intraoperative management
- Regional anaesthesia:
  - Consider residual heparin
  - Neuraxial: keep intravascular volume adequate to limit hypotension
  - Brachial plexus block suits AV fistula surgery: analgesia, abolishes vasospasm and vasodilates
- Dialysis alters drug pharmacokinetics
- AV fistula:
  - No BP cuff on the fistula arm; cuff and drip then share an arm, so use an anti-reflux valve
  - Save veins for future access: no venepuncture in the non-dominant arm or the upper dominant arm
- Dialysis catheters:
  - Temporary dialysis catheters may be used for IV access if other access fails (discouraged):
    1. Access aseptically, as for dialysis
    2. Aspirate the heparin lock before connecting a line or transducer
    3. Re-lock with heparin and seal aseptically when finished
  - Central access may be hard with a tunnelled or temporary catheter, or many previous ones, due to central vein stenosis
- Venous access:
  - Often difficult; avoid the antecubital fossa if a fistula or graft is planned
  - Avoid femoral lines if renal transplant planned, and subclavian lines if dialysis planned (stenosis risk)
- Fluids: no renal function means a narrow margin between too little and too much
  - Most arrive with a contracted ECF volume: mild hypovolaemia after dialysis worsens hypotension from drugs and autonomic neuropathy
  - Minor surgery: replace insensible losses only (not Holliday-Segar)
  - CVP often useful
  - Consider a small bag (e.g. 100ml normal saline) to avoid accidental boluses
- Positioning: poor nutrition makes skin bruise and slough easily; pad elbows, knees and ankles
- Protect the fistula at all costs: pad well, do not tuck the arm if possible, check the thrill during surgery

## Haemodialysis: postoperative management
- Haemodialysis can restart 1 day after surgery; consider heparin-related bleeding

## Dialysis disequilibrium syndrome
- Neurological symptoms from cerebral oedema
- Risk factors:
  - First dialysis
  - Predialysis urea > 175mg/dL or > 60mmol/L
  - CKD rather than AKI
  - Extremes of age
  - Existing neurological disease (head injury, stroke, epilepsy)
  - Other causes of cerebral oedema (hyponatraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, malignant hypertension)
  - Leaky blood-brain barrier (sepsis, vasculitis, TTP or HUS, encephalitis, meningitis)
- Mechanism: rapid urea removal lowers plasma osmolality; urea in brain cells cannot equilibrate in time (takes hours), so water shifts into the brain
- Features (during or just after haemodialysis, or starting CRRT):
  - Early: headache, disorientation, blurred vision, dizziness, nausea, cramps, anorexia
  - Late: confusion, seizures, coma, death
  - Self-limiting, usually settles within hours
- Prevention: slow urea removal when starting dialysis
- Management: treat headache and nausea; slow blood flow or stop dialysis; support during seizures; raising osmolality gradually with mannitol or hypertonic saline 3% is controversial

## Peritoneal dialysis
- Drain peritoneal fluid before anaesthesia (protects breathing and gastric emptying)
- Can usually omit 24-48h of dialysis
- Restart after surgery; may need haemodialysis after abdominal surgery

## Flashcards
- Q: What creatinine is an RCRI risk factor?
  - A: Cr >2mg/dL (177umol/L).
- Q: Potassium target before surgery in ESRF?
  - A: K <5.0-5.5, rechecked 4 hours after dialysis if concerned.
- Q: Ideal timing of haemodialysis before elective surgery?
  - A: 12-24hr before, and at least 4-6h before surgery.
- Q: Which opioid is preferred for PCA in ESRF and why?
  - A: Fentanyl; no active metabolites (morphine metabolites accumulate).
- Q: Two risk factors for dialysis disequilibrium syndrome?
  - A: First dialysis; predialysis urea > 175mg/dL (> 60mmol/L) (also CKD, extremes of age, neurological disease).
