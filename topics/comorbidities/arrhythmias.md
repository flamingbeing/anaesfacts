# Arrhythmias and Long QT Syndrome

Correct electrolytes, have defibrillator pads on before induction, avoid QT-prolonging drugs and sympathetic surges.

## Arrhythmias in general
- Classification
  - By origin: supraventricular vs ventricular; narrow vs broad complex
  - By rate: tachyarrhythmias vs bradyarrhythmias
- Bradycardias may need temporary pacing
- VT and VF may need cardioversion or defibrillation
- Common goals
  - Correct potassium, magnesium and calcium
  - Defibrillator pads on before induction

## Long QT syndrome: summary
- Electrolytes (especially magnesium), drug choice, avoid sympathetic stimulation, continue beta-blockers, defibrillator pads ready, MgSO4, arterial line
- Defined as QTc > 0.44 seconds: prolonged ventricular repolarisation that predisposes to torsades de pointes

## Causes
- Genetic: cardiac ion channel mutations (Na+, K+)
- Acquired: myocardial disease, electrolyte disturbance, drugs, head injury
  - Myocardial disease: MI, rheumatic fever, 3rd degree heart block, cardiomyopathy
  - Electrolytes: low Ca2+, low K+, low Mg2+
  - Drugs: class I and III antiarrhythmics, phenothiazines, tricyclic antidepressants, SSRIs
  - Hypothermia

## Pathophysiology
- Long QT: myocyte repolarisation is prolonged by faulty ion channels
- Torsades
  - Prolonged repolarisation allows early after-depolarisations (EADs)
  - EADs can show as tall U waves; if they reach threshold they cause premature ventricular contractions (PVCs)
  - TdP starts when a PVC lands on the preceding T wave ("R on T")
  - Often preceded by a short-long-short R-R sequence ("pause dependent" TdP); longer pauses lead to faster TdP runs

## Anaesthetic goals
- Avoid QT-prolonging drugs
- Avoid rises in sympathetic tone; anaesthetic and surgical stress can provoke TdP
- Keep metabolic and electrolyte balance
- Continue long-term beta-blockade where indicated

## Preoperative assessment
- History: syncope, exercise-induced syncope, cardiac arrest, family history
- Examination: may be normal
- Investigations
  - ECG: QT >440ms
  - Echo: structural heart disease

## Preoperative management
- Beta-blockers are the main treatment for congenital LQTS
  - Propranolol preferred: works best, probably by also blocking late sodium current
  - Little evidence for long-term beta-blockade in purely acquired LQTS
- Correct magnesium, potassium and calcium
- Sedative premedication may help as stress can trigger arrhythmias; midazolam is safe
- Treat the cause

## Intraoperative management
- Ready: defibrillator with pads on, IV magnesium sulphate, arterial line
- Avoid QT-prolonging drugs and sympathetic stimulation
- Keep warm: hypothermia can prolong QT

## Drug choice in long QT
- Avoid drugs that further prolong QTc, affect cardiac ion channels, cause tachycardia or act on the autonomic system
- Avoid if possible
  - Volatiles (especially sevoflurane), though their QT effect is probably insignificant
  - Ketamine, pancuronium: may worsen haemodynamic side effects
  - Suxamethonium: prolongs QT
  - Glycopyrrolate, atropine, neostigmine
  - Sympathomimetics: ephedrine, metaraminol
- Acceptable or suggested
  - Propofol or TIVA (good choice)
  - Opioids
  - Non-depolarising neuromuscular blockers
  - Sugammadex
  - Phenylephrine

## Torsades de pointes
- Diagnosis: VT with twisting morphology; check the arterial trace
  - Twisting may not be visible in short runs or a single lead
  - Bigeminy in known long QT may signal imminent TdP
- Treatment: MgSO4 bolus
  - Verapamil and phenytoin have also been used for malignant arrhythmias
- In a stable patient, DC cardioversion is a last resort: TdP is paroxysmal and often recurs after cardioversion
- TdP often stops by itself but can turn into VF, which needs DC defibrillation

## Postoperative management
- Consider HDU/ICU with ECG monitoring for 24hrs

## Brugada syndrome
- Sodium channel abnormality
- ST elevation in V1 and V2 with partial RBBB pattern
- Common in Asian men
- 2 types
  - Type 1: with syncope, needs an ICD
  - Type 2
- Triggers (BJA): vagal stimulation, fever, electrolyte disturbance, drugs
- Drugs to avoid: lignocaine, propofol infusion
- Evidence on anaesthetic drugs is unclear; take a pragmatic approach
  - High-risk patients (multiple previous arrhythmias): avoid propofol; use midazolam, volatile and opioid (BJA)
- Monitor closely for ST elevation and vagal events in relevant surgery (e.g. neck surgery)
- Avoid pneumoperitoneum if possible

## Flashcards
- Q: QTc threshold for long QT?
  - A: QTc > 0.44 seconds (440ms).
- Q: First-line treatment of torsades de pointes?
  - A: MgSO4 bolus; DC cardioversion is a last resort in a stable patient.
- Q: Beta-blocker of choice in congenital long QT?
  - A: Propranolol.
- Q: How long should ECG monitoring continue after surgery in long QT?
  - A: 24hrs, consider HDU/ICU.
- Q: Anaesthetic for a high-risk Brugada patient?
  - A: Avoid propofol; use midazolam, volatile and opioid.
