# Drowning

Main problems are fluid aspiration, severe hypothermia and prolonged tissue hypoxia.

## Respiratory
- ARDS: from alveolar cell injury, lung inflammation and loss of surfactant
  - Aspiration pneumonitis: chemical injury from inhaled gastric contents or acidic fluid
  - Negative pressure pulmonary oedema: from breathing in hard against a closed, spasming glottis
- Secondary bacterial pneumonia: often delayed, from organisms in contaminated water

## Hypothermia
- Core temperature below 32: rewarm gradually, no faster than 0.5 degrees per hour, with active external or internal warming

## Neurological
- Hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy from prolonged oxygen deprivation
  - Cerebral oedema raises ICP, with a risk of herniation
  - Status epilepticus (convulsive or non-convulsive) sharply raises cerebral metabolic demand

## Cardiovascular
- Hypoxic cardiomyopathy: myocardial stunning, low cardiac output, cardiogenic shock
- Malignant arrhythmias: VF or asystole, triggered by core temperature <28°C and severe acidaemia
- Intravascular hypovolaemia: cold diuresis and capillary leak

## Systemic and metabolic
- Severe lactic acidosis from tissue hypoperfusion
- SIRS after resuscitation
- AKI: tubular necrosis from shock, hypoxaemia or rhabdomyolysis
- DIC: consumption of clotting factors, with both bleeding and microvascular clotting
- Occult trauma: spinal cord or brain injury from diving into shallow water or debris

## Flashcards
- Q: Maximum rewarming rate after drowning with core temperature below 32?
  - A: 0.5 degrees per hour.
- Q: Below what core temperature do VF and asystole become likely?
  - A: <28°C (with severe acidaemia).
- Q: Why is a drowning victim often hypovolaemic?
  - A: Cold diuresis and capillary leak.
- Q: What occult injury should be considered after drowning?
  - A: Spinal cord or brain injury from diving into shallow water.
