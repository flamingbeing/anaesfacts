# Anaesthesia after Heart Transplant

The transplanted heart is denervated: no reflex tachycardia, no response to indirect agents, and the patient is immunosuppressed.

## Denervated heart
- Higher resting heart rate (90-100)
- Greater risk of arrhythmia; may have a cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED)
- Use direct-acting beta-agonists (isoproterenol, adrenaline)
  - Ephedrine (indirect) has a blunted effect
  - Atropine and Valsalva have no effect
- No compensatory tachycardia with hypotension or hypovolaemia
  - Preload dependent: keep intravascular volume adequate
  - Blood pressure may fall sharply after a spinal

## Chronic graft rejection
- May cause diastolic dysfunction: give fluids carefully
- Contractility is preserved unless rejection is active

## Other considerations
- Protect the graft: keep coronary perfusion pressure high
- Immunosuppression
  - High infection risk: strict asepsis for invasive procedures (central lines, neuraxial blocks)
  - Stress-dose steroids
  - End-organ effects (e.g. renal impairment)

## Flashcards
- Q: Typical resting heart rate after heart transplant?
  - A: Higher than normal, 90-100.
- Q: Which drugs raise heart rate in a transplanted heart?
  - A: Direct-acting beta-agonists (isoproterenol, adrenaline); ephedrine is blunted and atropine has no effect.
- Q: Why can a spinal cause a sharp fall in blood pressure after heart transplant?
  - A: No compensatory tachycardia; the heart is preload dependent.
- Q: Effect of chronic rejection on the graft?
  - A: Diastolic dysfunction; contractility preserved unless rejection is active.
