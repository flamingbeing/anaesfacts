# Perioperative acute kidney injury and renal protection

Who is at risk of AKI around surgery, how anaesthesia affects the kidney, how to manage a patient with AKI, and what does (and does not) protect the kidney.

## Risk factors
- Patient
  - Older age
  - Pre-existing renal impairment
  - Renovascular disease
  - Dehydration or hypovolaemia
  - Diabetes mellitus
  - Heart failure (cardiorenal syndrome)
  - Liver dysfunction with jaundice (hepatorenal syndrome)
  - Pregnancy-induced hypertension or pre-eclampsia
  - Rhabdomyolysis
  - Sepsis or multiorgan dysfunction syndrome (MODS)
- Surgical
  - Long or extensive surgery with big fluid shifts
  - Type of surgery
  - Cardiac: long CPB time, combined procedures, emergency or redo surgery
  - Aortic: cross-clamp placement and duration, intraoperative contrast
  - Peripheral vascular surgery
  - Liver or kidney transplant
  - Intra-abdominal hypertension
- Anaesthetic
  - Prolonged hypotension or hypovolaemia
  - Nephrotoxic drugs
  - Antibiotics (aminoglycosides, beta-lactams, amphotericin B)
  - ACE inhibitors or ARBs
  - NSAIDs
  - Lithium
  - Chemotherapy (ciclosporin, tacrolimus)
  - Radiographic contrast

## Effects of anaesthesia on renal function
- General anaesthesia
  - Reversible fall in renal function: lower SVR and myocardial depression reduce renal blood flow (RBF)
  - May speed up chronic renal impairment
  - IPPV: higher intrathoracic pressure lowers CO, so RBF, GFR and urine output fall
  - Sympathetic activation: RAAS activated, ADH release increases, ANP release falls; RBF and GFR fall, sodium and water retained
- Regional anaesthesia
  - Less effect on renal function, though the fall in SVR still lowers RBF
  - Higher risk of local anaesthetic toxicity: less protein binding, so a larger free fraction
- Surgical stress response: RAAS activation and more ADH, so sodium and water retention

## Patient with AKI: preoperative
- Assessment
  - Grade severity (RIFLE criteria)
  - Find and treat the cause: pre-renal, renal, post-renal
  - Assess comorbidities
- Optimisation
  - Fluids: optimise volume status, CO and MAP; invasive monitoring to guide fluids and vasoactive drugs
  - Electrolytes: correct metabolic and electrolyte abnormalities
    - Sodium bicarbonate if pH < 7.2
    - Bicarbonate deficit = 0.5 x body weight (kg) x (24 - HCO3)
  - Nutrition: AKI is catabolic, so feed adequately; keep glucose controlled in diabetes
  - Medications: stop nephrotoxins; adjust doses for creatinine clearance; some drugs are contraindicated
  - Dialysis indications
    - Severe pulmonary oedema
    - Severe hypertension from fluid overload not responding to diuretics
    - Hyperkalaemia
    - Severe metabolic acidosis
    - Uraemia with pericarditis or encephalopathy
    - Toxins: methanol, ethylene glycol, salicylates, lithium
  - Dialysis-dependent patients: dialyse before surgery
  - Optimise comorbidities
  - Timing: delay elective surgery until the acute renal dysfunction recovers, if possible

## Patient with AKI: intraoperative
- Goals mirror supportive AKI care
  - Avoid further insults: keep renal perfusion and fluid balance, avoid nephrotoxins
  - Correct electrolyte and acid-base disturbances
- Monitoring
  - Standard ASA monitoring
  - Invasive monitoring as comorbidities and surgery dictate
  - Arterial line also useful for frequent blood gases and electrolytes
- Technique: GA carries risks of fluid shifts and haemodynamic instability
- Maintenance
  - Keep renal perfusion: track fluid balance and replace losses; early and aggressive fluid replacement
  - Maintain MAP
  - Inotropes if CO stays low despite adequate volume
  - Keep normoglycaemia
- Drugs
  - Avoid nephrotoxins: no NSAIDs
  - Avoid renally cleared drugs
    - Neuromuscular blocker: atracurium (organ-independent elimination) rather than rocuronium or pancuronium
    - Opioid: fentanyl rather than morphine
    - Others: tranexamic acid
  - Adjust doses to creatinine clearance

## Patient with AKI: postoperative
- Monitor renal function closely
- Watch for complications of renal dysfunction
- Avoid nephrotoxins such as NSAIDs; adjust doses (e.g. gabapentin)
- Keep normoglycaemia
- Start dialysis, if criteria are met, once haemodynamically stable

## Renal protection: preoperative
- Identify at-risk patients
- Optimise volume status, CO and MAP
  - In critically ill patients, an RCT found no difference in AKI between 0.9% saline and albumin
  - Albumin and gelatin are safe with normal renal function
  - Hydroxyethyl starch: more AKI than Ringer's lactate in critically ill patients with severe sepsis
  - Invasive monitoring to guide fluids and vasoactive drugs
- Correct electrolyte and metabolic disturbances
- Glucose control in diabetes
- Avoid nephrotoxins
- Delay surgery until acute renal dysfunction recovers, if possible
- Dialysis-dependent: dialyse before surgery
- Contrast nephropathy prevention: isotonic IV fluids and N-acetylcysteine
- Monitor urine output and urea, electrolytes and creatinine
- Discuss surgical changes
  - EVAR instead of open AAA repair
  - Shorter aortic cross-clamp time
  - Off-pump rather than on-pump CABG

## Renal protection: intraoperative
- Stable haemodynamics is the mainstay
  - Optimise volume, CO and MAP for renal perfusion
  - Early and aggressive fluid replacement
  - Inotropes if CO stays low despite adequate volume
- Normoglycaemia in all patients
  - RCT in ventilated surgical ICU patients: intensive insulin improved survival and reduced AKI needing RRT
  - Hyperglycaemia during cardiac and vascular surgery linked with more renal morbidity and higher mortality
- Avoid nephrotoxins

## Pharmacological renal protection
- Diuretics
  - High doses may turn oliguric into non-oliguric AKI and reduce dialysis need
  - Giving diuretics to keep urine flowing in non-oliguric patients has not improved renal outcome or survival
  - Furosemide: IV 20-40mg bolus; IV 250mg over 1h in established renal failure
    - Loop diuretic: blocks reabsorption in the loop of Henle; higher tubular flow keeps tubules patent
    - Meta-analysis: no increase in mortality in AKI (no strong evidence of benefit)
    - Prophylactic use may harm the kidney, especially if the patient is dry
  - Mannitol: IV 0.5 g/kg
    - Osmotic diuretic and free radical scavenger
    - Benefit shown only in renal transplant
  - Never use diuretics to push urine output before the circulation is filled: oliguria is most often from low volume, and diuretics then worsen renal function; give only once filling and BP are adequate
- Dopamine agonists
  - Fenoldopam
    - Selective D1 agonist
    - Renal vasodilation: GFR, RBF, sodium excretion and urine output increase
    - No beta effects, so no inotropy
    - Few interactions, no toxic metabolites
    - Meta-analysis: benefit in critically ill patients
  - Dopamine: no renal protective effect
    - Multicentre RCT: low-dose dopamine did not prevent AKI, avoid RRT or lower mortality in ICU patients with early renal dysfunction
    - Systematic reviews: no role for low-dose dopamine
- Other agents
  - Theophylline: adenosine antagonist, reverses adenosine-mediated renal vasoconstriction; does not prevent AKI in CABG
  - ACE inhibitors and calcium channel blockers: no proven protection
  - Sodium nitroprusside: in one single-centre trial, given during CPB rewarming in CABG, reduced postoperative AKI
  - Recombinant ANP: conflicting evidence

## Renal protection: postoperative
- Monitor renal function closely
  - Urine output: fluid challenge of 250-500ml normal saline if output < 0.5ml/kg/h
  - Urea, electrolytes and creatinine
- Avoid nephrotoxins such as NSAIDs
- Keep normoglycaemia
- Watch for and treat complications
  - Treat sepsis aggressively
  - Replace haemorrhage losses
  - Treat heart failure promptly
  - Intra-abdominal hypertension (IAP > 20mmHg): compresses the renal pelvis and lowers renal perfusion, causing ischaemic ATN; treat with decompressive laparotomy
  - Rhabdomyolysis: aggressive volume expansion, diuretics, urinary alkalinisation
  - Organ support for MODS
  - RRT for RIFLE grade F (failure)

## Flashcards
- Q: How is the bicarbonate deficit calculated, and when is sodium bicarbonate given in AKI?
  - A: 0.5 x weight (kg) x (24 - HCO3); give if pH < 7.2
- Q: Which neuromuscular blocker and opioid suit a patient with AKI?
  - A: Atracurium (organ-independent elimination) and fentanyl
- Q: Does low-dose dopamine protect the kidney?
  - A: No: it does not prevent AKI, avoid RRT or reduce mortality
- Q: Postoperative fluid challenge trigger in an at-risk patient?
  - A: Urine output < 0.5 ml/kg/h: give 250-500 ml normal saline
- Q: At what intra-abdominal pressure is intra-abdominal hypertension a renal threat, and the treatment?
  - A: IAP > 20 mmHg; decompressive laparotomy
