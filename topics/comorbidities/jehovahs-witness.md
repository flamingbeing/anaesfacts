# Jehovah's Witness Patients

Clarify exactly what the patient accepts, document it, conserve blood maximally and keep any transfusion confidential.

## Key points
- Ethics, defining acceptable blood fractions and cell saver use, documentation
- Maximal blood conservation
- Confidentiality if blood is given

## Beliefs
- Christian belief that blood must not be taken in
- Blood removed from the body is unclean and must not be returned
  - Refused: red cells, white cells, platelets, FFP, autologous transfusion (pre-donated)
  - Usually accepted: fractions and synthetics, e.g. albumin, PCC, fibrinogen
  - Usually accepted if blood flow stays continuous: cardiopulmonary bypass, cell saver, ANH
  - Accepted: gelatin, recombinant factors
- Singapore: the organisation was deregistered in 1972 under the Societies Act
  - The organisation is illegal but personal belief is not
  - A refusal of blood by a patient with capacity is a valid exercise of autonomy

## Ethical conflict
- Giving blood to a JW patient who refuses it is illegal
- Tension between medical treatment and the patient's beliefs

## Ethics and consent
- Discuss limits of intervention with surgeon, patient and anaesthetist, for ethical and medicolegal reasons
- Emergency: act in best interests
  - Treatment, including transfusion, can be given without consent when there is no time to consult
  - A JW family's child in an emergency can be transfused to save life despite family refusal
  - Gillick competence applies to a competent minor, but can be used to agree to treatment, not to refuse it
- No capacity: faith must be proven
  - Needs proper documentation before assuming JW status and a wish for no transfusion
  - Without documentation, treat in best interests
- Capacity
  - Patient can consult their religious community on what they accept
  - Discuss in private: relatives or church members may coerce
- Meticulous documentation with date and time: discussion, conclusion, options, accepted risks, alternatives
  - Sign a refusal of medical treatment form
  - Record what the patient will accept (e.g. albumin, clotting factors, cell salvage), not just what they refuse
- Seek a court order and ethics committee if time allows
- Conscientious objection
  - If you cannot provide care without blood in a high-risk case, hand over to a colleague electively
  - Never abandon a patient in an emergency

## Preoperative assessment
- Confirm transfusion is truly refused; medicolegal discussion with surgeon, patient and anaesthetist on what refusing blood products means
- Discuss alternatives such as cell salvage
- Check for conscientious objection in the team (elective cases only)
- Full medical and anaesthetic review
  - FBC and coagulation profile; investigate any anaemia
- Involve surgeons and refer to haematology
- Raise Hb
  - Recombinant erythropoietin: allow 4 weeks; peak effect in 7-14 days
  - Give iron with it to avoid functional iron deficiency
  - Iron, folate, vitamin B12
  - Discuss whether autologous donation is acceptable
- Reduce blood loss
  - Look for coagulopathy or platelet dysfunction; stop drugs affecting clotting
  - DDAVP has limited value with normal coagulation
- Stage major surgery to limit loss and let Hb recover; neoadjuvant therapy to shrink tumours; minimally invasive approach; experienced team; non-surgical options

## Intraoperative management
- Surgical
  - Meticulous haemostasis: diathermy, cautery, clips and clamps, haemostatics (fibrin glue, oxidised cellulose, alginate dressings)
  - Tourniquets
  - Fast surgery by surgeons experienced with JW patients
  - Minimally invasive surgery may lose less blood
  - Consider alternatives to surgery
  - If the surgical plan or competence is in doubt, escalate for patient safety
- Anaesthetic
  - Monitoring, induction, maintenance and reversal as usual
  - Regional anaesthesia reduces blood loss; weigh coagulopathy risk if major loss is likely
  - Invasive monitoring
- Blood conservation
  - Preoperative hypervolaemic haemodilution in fit patients at low risk of fluid overload
  - ANH only if blood stays in a continuous circuit
    - Closed circuit that is never disconnected, via central access and 3-way taps
  - Avoid venous congestion at the surgical site: low PEEP, low intrathoracic pressure, avoid hypercarbia
  - Permissive hypotension
  - Avoid the lethal diamond (hypothermia, acidosis, hypocalcaemia, coagulopathy)
  - Antifibrinolytics, e.g. TXA
  - Cell salvage if agreed and the circuit runs continuously from wound to bloodstream
  - Recombinant factors 7,8,9 instead of FFP
  - Maximise oxygen delivery: SpO2 100% and high FiO2, mindful of oxygen toxicity

## Postoperative care
- Minimise blood sampling
- Consider hyperbaric oxygen therapy

## Confidentiality
- A known transfusion can lead to ostracism by family and friends and expulsion from the church
- Update only the patient; full confidentiality; staff aware of the sensitivity
- Give blood in full privacy with no visitors

## Flashcards
- Q: Which blood-related products and techniques do JW patients usually accept?
  - A: Fractions and synthetics (albumin, PCC, fibrinogen, gelatin, recombinant factors), and bypass, cell saver or ANH if blood flow stays continuous.
- Q: How long does recombinant erythropoietin need before surgery?
  - A: Allow 4 weeks; peak effect at 7-14 days; give iron with it.
- Q: How must ANH be set up for a JW patient?
  - A: A continuous closed circuit never disconnected from the patient, via central access and 3-way taps.
- Q: Can blood be given to a JW family's child in an emergency?
  - A: Yes, in the child's best interests despite family refusal.
- Q: What must the refusal documentation specify?
  - A: What the patient will accept, not just what they refuse.
