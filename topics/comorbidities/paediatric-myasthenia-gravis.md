# Myasthenia Gravis in Children

Autoimmune (or congenital) neuromuscular junction disorder: fatigable weakness, resistance to suxamethonium, sensitivity to non-depolarising relaxants.

## Pathophysiology
- IgG antibodies against postsynaptic nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction
- Fewer functional receptors: antibodies block acetylcholine binding, speed receptor breakdown, and cause complement damage
- Clinically: weakness and fatigue after exertion
- Affects 1:10000 of the general population; childhood onset (age <16 years) is about 11% of cases
- Three childhood forms: juvenile, transient neonatal, congenital

## Juvenile myasthenia gravis: features
- Girls affected 4 times more than boys
- Commonest presentation is ocular: strabismus, ptosis, diplopia, ophthalmoplegia
  - If confined to eye muscles, about 30% remit spontaneously
- May also start with bulbar, facial, generalised limb or (rarely) respiratory weakness
- Usually slowly progressive, fluctuating
- Worsened by infection, fever, surgery, emotional stress, and drugs (aminoglycosides, anticonvulsants)
- Thymic hyperplasia involved; thymoma found only in adults
- Often with other autoimmune disease: thyroiditis, SLE, rheumatoid arthritis

## Juvenile myasthenia gravis: diagnosis
- Edrophonium (anticholinesterase) test: rapid improvement in strength for about 5 min; give atropine first
- Acetylcholine receptor antibody radioimmunoassay: highly specific; a negative result does not exclude it
- Nerve studies: decrement with repetitive nerve stimulation

## Juvenile myasthenia gravis: treatment
- Anticholinesterases first line: block acetylcholine breakdown, raising and prolonging its action at the junction
  - Pyridostigmine bromide 1 mg/kg 4-hourly initially; preferred over neostigmine (longer half-life, fewer side effects)
- Steroids short term for exacerbations not controlled by more anticholinesterase; occasionally azathioprine, ciclosporin, cyclophosphamide
- Plasmapheresis: removes antibodies to optimise before surgery; sometimes in myasthenic crisis with respiratory failure
- IV immunoglobulin: before surgery or in crisis
- Thymectomy: marked improvement and remission in 60-90%

## Transient neonatal myasthenia gravis
- 15% of infants of myasthenic mothers; maternal antibodies cross the placenta
- Onset usually within the first few hours, sometimes after several days
- Hypotonia, generalised weakness, poor feeding, apnoeas, respiratory distress, facial weakness, ptosis
- Edrophonium may confirm the diagnosis
- Supportive care; may need ventilation at first; neostigmine used
- Improves over 4-6 weeks; no higher risk of myasthenia later in life

## Congenital myasthenia gravis
- Autosomal recessive neuromuscular junction disorder; not autoimmune
- Often presents at birth, sometimes not until adolescence
- Newborn: severe weakness, hypotonia, recurrent apnoeas, poor feeding, ocular weakness
- Variable response to anticholinesterases; no benefit from immunosuppression or thymectomy
- Usually non-progressive, mild lifelong disease

## Preoperative management
- Multidisciplinary: neurology, surgery, intensive care, anaesthesia
- Identify muscle groups affected; assess respiratory and bulbar involvement; lung function tests
- Optimise on anticholinesterase with the neurologist; IVIG or plasmapheresis before surgery if weakness is severe
- Omit anticholinesterases the night before; first on the morning list
  - Severe weakness: continue up to surgery and give glycopyrronium to reduce salivation and vagal effects
- On steroids: may need an extra intraoperative dose

## Intraoperative management
- Usually intubate and ventilate (most anaesthetic agents depress breathing)
- Avoid suxamethonium
  - Resistance: often needs 3-4 times the normal dose
  - High incidence of phase II (non-depolarising) block
  - Anticholinesterases and plasmapheresis may prolong its action
- Non-depolarising relaxants are safe if titrated with neuromuscular monitoring
  - Monitor at the facial nerve (ocular muscles commonly affected and sensitive)
  - Atracurium or mivacurium preferred, at 10-20% of the normal dose
  - Reverse, and extubate only once airway and respiratory muscle function are normal
  - Assess with 5 sec head lift, vital capacity, inspiratory force
  - Known bulbar weakness: may need airway protection until tone returns
- Intubation and surgery can often be done without relaxants
  - Volatiles increase weakness; opioids such as remifentanil add some relaxation
- Weakness worsens with hypothermia, hypokalaemia, aminoglycosides, anticonvulsants
- Analgesia: opioid infusion or PCA, regional, NSAIDs, paracetamol all suitable

## Postoperative management
- Restart oral anticholinesterase as soon as possible; use NG tube if needed
- With ileus: IV neostigmine, titrated slowly up to the equivalent of the usual oral pyridostigmine dose
  - 1 mg IV neostigmine = 30 mg oral pyridostigmine
- Postoperative weakness: myasthenic or cholinergic crisis; edrophonium helps tell them apart and guide treatment
- Some need immunoglobulin, plasmapheresis or neostigmine infusion
- ICU or HDU for ventilation or observation should be available and discussed with patient and parents; especially after major body cavity surgery
