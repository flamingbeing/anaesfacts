# Prematurity and Ex-Premature Infants

Preterm and ex-preterm infants carry airway, lung, apnoea, neurological, haematological and thermal risks; plan monitoring for postoperative apnoea.

## Newborn problems
- Respiratory distress in the newborn
  - Transient tachypnoea of the newborn
  - Meconium aspiration syndrome
  - Hyaline membrane disease: immature lungs with too little surfactant (surfactant production surges at 36 weeks)
  - Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn
  - Others: pneumothorax, pulmonary hypoplasia, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, tracheo-oesophageal fistula, sepsis, congenital heart disease, anaemia, choanal atresia, intracranial haemorrhage, inborn errors of metabolism
- Apnoea
- Transitional circulation is fragile
  - A perioperative rise in PVR can reopen fetal shunts
  - PVR may stay high: persistent fetal circulation (PFC), also called primary pulmonary hypertension of the newborn
  - Treatment is supportive until it settles on its own: high frequency oscillation, inhaled nitric oxide, inotropes if needed
  - May be met during neonatal surgery such as congenital diaphragmatic hernia repair
- Congenital heart disease: duct-dependent lesions
- Fluids: water and sodium balance is negative over the first few days

## Definitions and epidemiology
- Premature birth (WHO): gestational age <37 weeks (259 days), whatever the birth weight
  - Moderate prematurity: 31-36 weeks
  - Severe prematurity: 24-30 weeks
- Incidence 6-11 % of live births
- Raises neonatal and infant morbidity; 85% of early neonatal deaths not due to congenital conditions in Great Britain
- Risk is higher with low birth weight (< 2500 g) and higher again with very low birth weight (< 1500 g)

## Common operations
- Cardiac: PDA ligation
- Gastrointestinal: laparotomy for perforated viscus or NEC, stoma formation, inguinal hernia repair, fundoplication
- Neurosurgical: CSF drainage or shunts for hydrocephalus after IVH
- Eye: laser photocoagulation, vitrectomy for retinopathy of prematurity
- Imaging: MRI, image-guided vascular access
- All neonatal anaesthesia considerations also apply

## Airway
- Subglottic stenosis or tracheomalacia after prolonged intubation (ex-prem)
- Intubation may be difficult or need a smaller ETT

## Respiratory
- Hyaline membrane disease / respiratory distress syndrome (RDS)
  - Differential: other causes of newborn respiratory distress (above)
  - Cause: too little surfactant
  - Complications: air leak and pneumothorax, emphysema, recurrent pneumonia
  - May progress to chronic lung disease
- Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) / chronic lung disease (CLD) (ex-prem)
  - Follows prolonged oxygen or ventilatory support after birth (e.g. for RDS)
  - Graded by oxygen requirement
  - Mechanism: volutrauma, barotrauma and oxygen toxicity
  - Defined by oxygen dependence for longer than 28 days (>28 days), sometimes also IPPV or CPAP
  - Features: respiratory failure from low lung compliance, airway obstruction, pulmonary oedema and atelectasis
  - Raised PVR leads to pulmonary hypertension
  - CXR: hyperinflated areas, cystic infiltrates, reticular honeycomb pattern
  - Treated with steroids, diuretics, bronchodilators and often home oxygen
  - Perioperative: needs higher peak pressures and FiO2 to reach acceptable SpO2; may need nasal CPAP or IPPV after surgery; regional techniques (e.g. inguinal herniotomy) sometimes used
  - Prevention
    - Limit FiO2 to a target SpO2: 93-98% for term babies, 86-96% for preterm babies
    - Lung protective ventilation: permissive hypercapnia, volume-targeted ventilation, HFOV, pressure gauge on T-piece or Neopuff
    - Wean to nasal CPAP or HFNC where possible
- Subglottic stenosis (ex-prem)
  - Complication of prolonged intubation; can make intubation difficult

## Apnoea of prematurity
- Common in preterm infants (up to 30%), may come with bradycardia (ex-prem)
- Peaks at 44 weeks but can occur up to 60 weeks post-conceptual age (PCA); low risk beyond >60 weeks
- Apnoeic episode: no breathing for >20s, or a pause with bradycardia or cyanosis
- Types
  - Central: immature respiratory control with blunted chemoreceptor response
    - Central CO2 response is blunted in the first few weeks of life, most in preterm infants
    - Weak peripheral response to hypoxia; brief rise in ventilation then apnoea
  - Obstructive: pharyngeal collapse, nasal obstruction
  - Mixed
- Perioperative triggers: anaesthetic agents and opioids, hypoxia, stress, sepsis
  - Most common in the first 12 hours; may persist up to 72 hours after surgery
- Risk factors
  - Preterm infant (born <34 weeks, immature respiratory centre) at <60 weeks PCA
  - Term infant <44 weeks PCA; respiratory control matures by 3 weeks in term babies, so term infants older than 1 month (>44 weeks PCA) are probably not at risk after routine minor surgery without opioids
  - Anaemia (haematocrit <30% / Hb <10), hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, sepsis
  - Chronic lung disease
  - Previous apnoeas
- Prevention and detection
  - Term <44 weeks PCA and preterm <60 weeks PCA: monitor for 24 hours after general or regional anaesthesia with apnoea monitor and pulse oximeter (high dependency monitoring), and for 12 hours after any apnoea
  - Caffeine 10 mg/kg IV or oral after induction in high-risk groups
  - Aminophylline 4 mg/kg IV is an alternative but has more side effects: tachycardia, jitteriness, irritability, feed intolerance, vomiting, hyperglycaemia
  - Regional anaesthesia without sedation may lower the risk
  - Avoid clonidine as a caudal additive in this group: case reports of more respiratory depression
- Management
  - Confirm it is apnoea and decide central or obstructive
  - Put the airway in a neutral position
  - Stimulate: startle, or rub face, abdomen, feet or chest; most episodes stop by themselves or with light stimulation
  - No breathing: bag-mask ventilation and follow Basic Life Support
  - Alternative: nasal CPAP

## Cardiovascular
- Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)
- Higher risk of other congenital heart disease (ex-prem)
- IV access can be very hard after long ICU stays; may need a PICC (ex-prem)

## Neurological
- Intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH), with long-term deficits such as cerebral palsy and hydrocephalus
  - Up to 40% of newborns <34 weeks PCA may have IVH
  - Uncommon at term because the germinal matrix starts to involute at 34 weeks PCA
  - Risk rises with prematurity, low birth weight, RDS, coagulopathy, hypoxia and acidosis
  - Avoid swings in blood pressure, over-transfusion and anaemia
- Periventricular leukomalacia (PVL)
- Lower MAC than full-term infants
- Ex-prem: higher risk of developmental delay

## Gastrointestinal
- Necrotising enterocolitis (NEC): disease of low birth weight preterm infants
  - Gut ischaemia leads to mucosal injury, perforation, peritonitis, fluid and electrolyte upset, sepsis and shock
  - Resuscitate with fluid and correct coagulopathy before surgery; fluid and blood losses can be large
- Perforated viscus
- Feed intolerance, gastro-oesophageal reflux
- Higher risk of neonatal jaundice

## Hepatic, metabolic and renal
- Immature liver enzyme systems
- Prone to hypoglycaemia
- Immature renal function
- Total body water can exceed 75% of body weight; circulating blood volume about 90-100 mL/kg

## Haematological
- Anaemia is common
  - Anaemia of prematurity: EPO deficiency
  - Also from frequent blood sampling
  - Citrate in transfused products can cause hypocalcaemia (immature liver and kidney clearance)
- Platelets
  - Thrombocytopenia is possible; ask neonatology to exclude sepsis
  - Transfuse if <50k with ongoing bleeding or an invasive procedure, or after replacing >1-2 times blood volume
  - Dose: neonates and infants 5-10ml/kg; older children 10ml/kg
- Coagulation: prolonged PT/PTT is common (e.g. vitamin K deficiency)

## Pain
- Mature physiological responses to pain
- Analgesia blunts the stress response and later behavioural change

## Other
- Retinopathy of prematurity (retrolental fibroplasia): linked to gestational age and duration of oxygen therapy
  - May need anaesthesia for laser treatment
  - Accept SpO2 > 90% rather than aiming near 100%, to avoid needless high FiO2
- Weaker cellular and tissue immunity: more sepsis; strict asepsis for invasive procedures
- Thermoregulation: larger surface area to weight ratio, thin immature skin and little subcutaneous fat, so heat loss is greater

## Preoperative assessment
- Look for respiratory distress: tachypnoea, grunting, nasal flaring, chest wall recession, cyanosis

## Ex-premature child grown up
- Persistent problems: subglottic stenosis, chronic lung disease, feed intolerance, failure to thrive
- Difficult IV access

## Flashcards
- Q: WHO definition of premature birth?
  - A: Gestational age <37 weeks (259 days), regardless of birth weight
- Q: Which infants need 24 hours of apnoea monitoring after anaesthesia?
  - A: Term infants <44 weeks PCA and preterm infants <60 weeks PCA (and 12 hours after any apnoea)
- Q: Caffeine dose to reduce postoperative apnoea?
  - A: 10 mg/kg IV or oral after induction
- Q: Target SpO2 for preterm babies to limit oxygen toxicity?
  - A: 86-96% (term babies 93-98%)
- Q: Definition of an apnoeic episode in an infant?
  - A: Cessation of breathing >20 s, or with bradycardia or cyanosis
