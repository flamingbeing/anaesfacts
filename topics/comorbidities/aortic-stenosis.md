# Aortic Stenosis

Fixed cardiac output state: keep heart rate slow, SVR and preload up, and coronary perfusion maintained.

## Key points
- Severe: AVA 1 cm², mean gradient 40mmHg
- Slow and tight
- Maintain coronary perfusion

## Presentation
- Asymptomatic, or the 3 cardinal symptoms: angina, syncope, dyspnoea; reduced effort tolerance
- Ejection systolic murmur; pulsus parvus et tardus
- ECG: LVH
- TTE grades severity

## Severity indices
- Severe: AVA 1 cm², peak velocity 4m/s, mean gradient 40mmHg
- Gradient derived from peak velocity with the Bernoulli equation
- Grading per AHA 2020

## Pathophysiology
- High risk of haemodynamic instability
- Fixed cardiac output
  - Outflow obstruction limits LV emptying and stroke volume
  - Cannot tolerate the fall in SVR from GA or regional
  - Falling SVR lowers coronary perfusion pressure, leading to ischaemia, weaker contraction and further falls in BP: a downward spiral
  - Cannot raise output on exertion, so cerebral perfusion falls: exertional syncope (survival about 3 years)
- Ventricular hypertrophy
  - High gradient raises afterload and wall stress, causing compensatory LVH
  - Stiff ventricle needs high filling pressure: low-normal HR, sinus rhythm, raised preload
  - Raised LA pressure, pulmonary venous hypertension, with or without pulmonary oedema and heart failure: dyspnoea (survival about 2 years)
- Myocardial ischaemia, especially subendocardial
  - LVH raises oxygen demand (thicker wall, higher tension)
  - Supply falls: low aortic pressure from fixed output, high LVEDP from poor compliance, so lower coronary perfusion pressure
  - Supply-demand mismatch: angina (survival about 5 years)
  - Avoid tachycardia; maintain diastolic pressure
- Complications: heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, arrhythmias
- Assess risk with TTE and counsel properly

## Preoperative assessment (severe AS)
- Valve replacement before non-cardiac surgery (AHA 2024)
  - Symptomatic, elective surgery: surgical AVR or TAVI first (high risk of perioperative sudden death)
  - Asymptomatic with EF <50%: treat as symptomatic
  - Asymptomatic with EF >50%: time-sensitive and minor surgery can go ahead
    - Consider AVR before elective major surgery (thoracic, abdominal, major orthopaedic)
- Counsel as high-risk surgery
- Cardiology referral
- Consider premedication; avoid raising heart rate

## Intraoperative management
- Haemodynamic targets
  - Low HR, 60-70 bpm; avoid tachycardia
  - Keep sinus rhythm
  - High-normal preload
  - High-normal afterload
- Monitoring and equipment
  - 5-lead ECG plus standard monitoring (NIBP, SpO2)
  - Arterial line before induction
  - Central line for fluid guidance and vasoactive drugs
  - Consider cardiac output monitor (e.g. FloTrac) for dynamic fluid and output assessment
  - Consider defibrillator pads if arrhythmia risk is high
  - Forced-air warmer, temperature probe and fluid warmer
  - PA catheter relatively contraindicated: may trigger arrhythmias
  - TOE for valve and ventricular function
- Technique: avoid falls in SVR and tachycardia
  - LA or peripheral nerve block where possible
  - Otherwise GA
  - Neuraxial blocks only with extreme caution: fixed output cannot compensate for afterload reduction, leading to severe hypotension, ischaemia and reduced contractility
- Induction
  - Titrated, cardiostable induction
  - Opioid-based to blunt the sympathetic response to laryngoscopy
  - Vasopressors drawn up; treat hypotension promptly
- Maintenance
  - Good analgesia to prevent catecholamine-driven tachycardia, hypertension and ischaemia
  - Treat hypotension with direct alpha agonists (metaraminol, phenylephrine)
  - Careful fluids guided by output monitoring: keep preload without pulmonary oedema
  - Treat arrhythmias promptly to keep sinus rhythm
  - Avoid hypoxia and anaemia

## Postoperative care
- Low threshold for HDU or ICU
- Good analgesia to prevent tachycardia; consider regional

## Cardiopulmonary bypass considerations
- Avoid hypotension: it can start a spiral to cardiac arrest
  - Lower coronary perfusion gradient causes ischaemia, lower output and lower BP
- In cardiac arrest, closed-chest compressions generate little flow across the stenotic valve: CPR may fail
- Avoid arrhythmias, especially during atrial cannulation
  - Dependence on atrial kick: SVT or AF quickly causes hypotension and ischaemia
  - Be fully ready to start CPB before atrial manipulation
- Myocardial protection is harder
  - Hypertrophied muscle is hard to protect with cardioplegia
  - Use surface cooling, myocardial temperature monitoring and retrograde cardioplegia
- Aortic suture line: treat hypertension quickly to avoid bleeding and dissection
  - After AVR, LV output is no longer opposed by the valve, so hypertension stresses the suture line

## Aortic sclerosis
- Valve thickening without stenosis

## Flashcards
- Q: What are the criteria for severe AS?
  - A: AVA 1 cm², peak velocity 4m/s, mean gradient 40mmHg.
- Q: Survival after symptom onset in AS for angina, syncope and dyspnoea?
  - A: About 5, 3 and 2 years respectively.
- Q: Which asymptomatic AS patients should have AVR before elective surgery?
  - A: Those with EF <50% (and consider before elective major surgery).
- Q: What heart rate target is used in AS?
  - A: 60-70 bpm, avoiding tachycardia.
- Q: Why may CPR fail in severe AS?
  - A: Chest compressions produce little flow across the stenotic valve.
