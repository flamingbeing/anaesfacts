# Liver Failure in Children

Acute or chronic liver failure in children: multisystem disease with coagulopathy, bleeding, encephalopathy, sepsis and altered drug handling.

## Acute liver failure: definition
- Rare and complex; mortality over 70% without supportive care and/or liver transplantation
- Adult definition: acute encephalopathy, coagulopathy and evidence of acute liver necrosis, with hepatic dysfunction starting within 8 weeks of the first signs of liver disease, with no pre-existing liver disease
- Children: encephalopathy may be absent, late or missed even when disease is severe
  - Paediatric definitions stress significant coagulopathy from acute hepatic dysfunction that does not respond to parenteral vitamin K
- Onset by duration of liver dysfunction:
  - Hyperacute: up to 10 days
  - Acute: 11-30 days
  - Subacute: over 30 days (jaundice more common)
- May be the first presentation of an underlying metabolic (Wilson's disease) or autoimmune disease

## Acute liver failure: causes
- Vary with age
- Neonatal haemochromatosis (rare disorder of iron handling): commonest cause in infants
- Viral hepatitis: 80% of fulminant hepatic failure in children of all ages
- Neonates and infants
  - Infection: septicaemia, hepatitis B, adenovirus, parvovirus B19, echovirus, coxsackie, non-A-G hepatitis, EBV, HIV
  - Metabolic: neonatal haemochromatosis, tyrosinaemia type I, mitochondrial disorders, fatty acid oxidation defects, alpha-1-antitrypsin (α1-antitrypsin) deficiency
  - Drugs: paracetamol, iron, vitamin A, salicylates, anticonvulsants, isoniazid
  - Vascular: congenital heart disease, asphyxia, Budd-Chiari syndrome
  - Others: neuroblastoma, hepatoblastoma, leukaemia, familial erythrophagocytic syndrome
- Older children
  - Infection: viral hepatitis A-G, EBV, CMV, HSV, leptospirosis, bacterial sepsis
  - Metabolic: Wilson's disease
  - Drugs: paracetamol, isoniazid, salicylates, valproic acid, drugs of abuse in adolescents (glue sniffing, ecstasy), halothane hepatitis
  - Toxins: carbon tetrachloride, Amanita phalloides (death cap mushroom), iron poisoning
  - Neoplastic: lymphoma, leukaemia, hepatocellular carcinoma
  - Vascular: after cardiac surgery, shock, sickle cell anaemia

## Acute liver failure: presentation
- Multisystem complications: sepsis, acute renal failure, severe coagulopathy, GI bleeding, ascites, cerebral oedema, high-output cardiac failure with low SVR, hypoglycaemia, hypokalaemia, metabolic acidosis
- Jaundice is the commonest presenting symptom, often after a flu-like prodrome
- Anorexia, nausea, vomiting and ascites are common
- Encephalopathy is often not obvious, especially in neonates and infants (jaundice and coagulopathy may be the first signs)
  - Infants: irritability, altered sleep pattern
  - Older children: aggressive behaviour
  - When present, tends to be pre-terminal
- Hepatic encephalopathy grades (higher grade means worse disease and outcome):
  - Grade 1: lethargy, slightly reduced conscious level
  - Grade 2: stupor, disorientation, combative
  - Grade 3: no response to command, responds to pain
  - Grade 4: no response to command or pain; extensor posturing and rigidity

## Acute liver failure: anaesthetic concerns
- Anaesthetise for emergency procedures only: high perioperative morbidity and mortality

## Chronic liver failure: causes
- Incidence 6:100,000 live births; suspect in any infant with jaundice beyond 14 days
- Commonest cause: extrahepatic biliary atresia
  - Hepatic portoenterostomy (Kasai procedure) within the first 8 weeks of life
  - If successful: up to 90% 10 year survival
  - Failed Kasai is the commonest indication for liver transplant worldwide
- Other causes
  - Biliary obstruction: choledochal cyst, cystic fibrosis, sclerosing cholangitis
  - Infective: acute viral hepatitis B, chronic active hepatitis, CMV
  - Metabolic: Wilson's disease, alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency, tyrosinaemia, haemochromatosis
  - Autoimmune: chronic active hepatitis

## Chronic liver failure: presentation and assessment
- May be asymptomatic, present as acute liver failure, or have insidious onset
- Signs of chronicity: small hard liver, jaundice, skin signs (palmar erythema, spider naevi, caput medusae), splenomegaly, ascites, oedema, growth failure, muscle wasting
- Encephalopathy triggers: sedatives, infection, GI bleeding, high-protein diet, hypokalaemia, surgery
  - Falling conscious level may signal cerebral oedema needing intubation and ICU care
- Child-Pugh score (Child, modified by Pugh): grades severity, predicts prognosis and risk of major surgery
  - Criteria ABCDE: Albumin, Bilirubin, Coagulopathy (INR), Distension (ascites), Encephalopathy
  - 5–6 points: Child's A, minimal mortality (<5%)
  - 7–9 points: Child's B, modest mortality (5-50%)
  - 10–15 points: Child's C, marked mortality (>50%)
- Bilirubin, albumin and prothrombin time track disease progression well
  - Poor prognosis: rising bilirubin and prothrombin time, falling albumin
  - Encephalopathy, nutrition and ascites: subjective but still useful
- Standard LFTs are poor markers of function, with no prognostic value
  - ALT and AST mark liver damage but may fall in late disease
  - ALP rises in cholestasis and biliary obstruction
- Causes of death: sepsis, massive haemorrhage, renal failure, worsening hepatic function with encephalopathy

## Anaesthetic problems: haemorrhage
- Liver makes all clotting factors except vWF (VIII)
- PTT and INR usually prolonged; good markers of liver function
- Platelets fall in number and function, mainly from splenic sequestration
- Risk of massive bleeding from oesophageal, gastric or rectal varices (portal hypertension)
  - Regular variceal screening
  - Treatment: prophylactic β-blockers, sclerotherapy or banding, porto-systemic shunt
- Anaemia from chronic disease, chronic bleeding and malnutrition
- Before surgery: FBC, coagulation screen, crossmatch, enough platelets and clotting factors available

## Anaesthetic problems: ascites
- Oedema and ascites mean advanced liver dysfunction
- Raised intra-abdominal pressure: diaphragmatic splinting, respiratory distress, reduced FRC
- May be on diuretics (spironolactone) or have a peritoneovenous shunt
- Draining ascites intraoperatively can cause marked hypovolaemia and hypotension afterwards as it reaccumulates; give extra fluid

## Anaesthetic problems: respiratory and cardiovascular
- Low PaO2: intrapulmonary shunts, V/Q mismatch, pleural effusions, ascites (reduced FRC, splinting)
- More perioperative atelectasis and chest infection
- Hyperdynamic circulation: high CO, low SVR
- Shunts (cutaneous, porto-pulmonary); portal hypertension leads to ascites and varices

## Anaesthetic problems: renal and metabolic
- Renal impairment is common and carries a poor prognosis
  - Low SVR and perfusion pressure activate RAAS, so GFR falls further
  - ADH rises with hypoperfusion, so urine output falls further
- Hepatorenal syndrome: renal failure with liver failure; concentrated urine with low urinary sodium (<10 mmol/L); sodium and water retention
- Renal failure may also follow sepsis or acute tubular necrosis
- Hypoglycaemia risk (low glycogen stores, impaired gluconeogenesis): monitor glucose closely
- Acid-base affected by renal dysfunction or diuretics

## Anaesthetic problems: infection
- Impaired immunity, higher sepsis risk
- Strict asepsis for procedures
- Suspect infection if cardiovascular instability or worsening encephalopathy

## Anaesthetic problems: drug handling
- Phase I (cytochrome P-450 mixed function oxidases): hydrolysis, oxidation, reduction
- Phase II: conjugation with glucuronide, sulphate, acetate, glycine or methyl groups
- Reduced hepatic clearance of drugs relying on these, e.g. opioids, benzodiazepines
- Biliary obstruction reduces biliary excretion, e.g. pancuronium (prolonged block)
- Low albumin raises free drug fraction, so greater effect per dose (e.g. thiopental)
- Volume of distribution increases: hypoalbuminaemia (protein-bound drugs), ascites, sodium and water retention
- Drugs that reduce CO and hepatic blood flow impair hepatic metabolism
  - Avoid halothane; desflurane, sevoflurane and isoflurane are safe

## Preoperative investigations
- FBC, coagulation, U+E, LFTs, blood glucose, CXR, ABGs, hepatitis B serology

## Intraoperative management
- Benzodiazepine premedication (midazolam, diazepam) with caution: may precipitate encephalopathy
- Low threshold for invasive monitoring (CVP, arterial line, urine output) and booking HDU or ICU bed
- Ascites raises regurgitation and aspiration risk: give H2 antagonist or PPI; RSI may be needed
- Thiopentone: clearance unchanged (fat soluble, rapid redistribution), but patients may be sensitive; reduce dose
- Propofol: clearance unchanged; half-life longer with prolonged infusion; reduce induction dose to avoid hypotension
- Muscle relaxants
  - Atracurium or cisatracurium of choice
  - Rocuronium, vecuronium and pancuronium last longer
  - Less plasma cholinesterase prolongs suxamethonium and mivacurium
  - Use neuromuscular monitoring
- Maintenance: isoflurane, sevoflurane (theoretical compound A risk) or desflurane; avoid halothane (reduces hepatic blood flow)
- Opioids
  - More sensitive to morphine, fentanyl and alfentanil
  - Morphine half-life prolonged: dose less often; lengthen PCA lockout
  - Remifentanil unaffected (blood and tissue esterases)
- Avoid NSAIDs perioperatively
- Regional anaesthesia useful for postoperative analgesia in some; check coagulation; reduce LA doses (hepatic clearance)

## Fluid management
- Careful fluid balance (blood loss, insensible loss, ascites) to avoid renal failure
- Avoid lactate-containing solutions; use dextrose solutions to prevent hypoglycaemia
- Replace losses with albumin to keep intravascular volume
- With coagulopathy, FFP may be the main replacement fluid
- Keep urine output at 1 mL/kg/hour; mannitol may be needed if fluid balance is correct
