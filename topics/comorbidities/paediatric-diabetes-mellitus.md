# Diabetes Mellitus in Children

The commonest metabolic disease of childhood; perioperative aim is normal glucose (4-10 mmol/L), normal electrolytes and an early return to eating.

## Type 1 (insulin-dependent) diabetes
- 90% of childhood diabetes; beta-cell destruction leads to insulin deficiency
- Cause partly autoimmune: 80% have islet cell antibodies at diagnosis; linked with other autoimmune disease (thyroid)
  - Genetic and environmental factors too; more common if a parent or sibling has diabetes
- Presentation: a few weeks of thirst, polyuria, tiredness, poor appetite and weight loss
  - Hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, sometimes ketosis
  - Some present acutely in ketoacidosis and coma
- Diagnosis with symptoms, any of:
  - Random venous plasma glucose >11.1 mmol/L
  - Fasting plasma glucose >7.1 mmol/L
  - Plasma glucose >11.1 mmol/L 2 hours after a 75 g glucose load
- Diagnosis without symptoms: plasma glucose >11.1 mmol/L on a second day (fasting, random or 2 hours after 75 g load)
- Impaired glucose tolerance: fasting glucose <7.0 mmol/L and 2-hour glucose >7.8 but <11.1 mmol/L

## Treatment and control
- Insulin, from animal (porcine) or human sources or made by recombinant DNA technology in bacteria or yeast
- Three types: short, intermediate and long acting
- Regimens are individual; usually an intermediate or long-acting insulin plus a short-acting one
- Blood glucose target 4-9 mmol/L, avoiding hypoglycaemia
- HbA1c reflects control over the previous 6-8 weeks; ideal in children 6.5-7.5% or less (tight targets risk hypoglycaemia)
- Diet, exercise and avoiding obesity matter long term
- Retinopathy, neuropathy and nephropathy are rare in children; screening starts 5 years after diagnosis or yearly from age 12

## Type 2 diabetes
- Low insulin or peripheral insulin resistance; mainly adults (age >40 years)
- Rare in children but rising with obesity
- Maturity onset diabetes of the young (MODY): impaired glucose tolerance; treatment aims at weight loss
- Oral hypoglycaemics not used; insulin only occasionally if symptomatic or hyperglycaemic

## Anaesthetic considerations
- Team approach: surgeon, anaesthetist and diabetes team
- Surgical stress response: catabolism, higher metabolic rate, protein and fat breakdown, starvation, glucose intolerance
  - Varies with type and length of surgery and infection
  - Exaggerated in diabetes: hyperglycaemia, ketosis or ketoacidosis
- Aims
  - Normoglycaemia, target 4-10 mmol/L
  - Avoid low potassium, magnesium and phosphate
  - Early return to oral intake
- Minor surgery: up to 30 min, eating within 4 hours afterwards
- Poor control, emergency or major surgery: glucose, insulin and potassium infusions

## Minor surgery
- Admit the day before elective surgery
- Check blood glucose; ideal 4-10 mmol/L
  - Over 15 mmol/L: test urine for ketones
  - Negative or trace: continue with monitoring
  - Positive: surgery may need postponing; ask the diabetes team
- First on the list if possible
- Morning list
  - Usual insulin the day before
  - Fast from midnight
  - Omit usual morning insulin; give 20% of total daily dose as long-acting analogue (insulin detemir) subcutaneously when IV fluids start
  - Start IV 0.45% saline with 5% dextrose at maintenance rate at 08.00
- Afternoon list
  - Usual insulin the day before
  - Before breakfast: 20% of total daily dose as long-acting analogue (detemir) and 10% as short-acting insulin (Actrapid)
  - Breakfast, then fast
  - IV 0.45% saline with 5% dextrose at maintenance rate
  - Hourly blood glucose after the insulin

## Postoperative care after minor surgery
- Eating or drinking within 2 hours
  - After eating, give 20% of total daily dose as soluble insulin (Actrapid)
  - Resume usual regimen after the second meal
  - Stop IV fluids once eating and drinking
  - Monitor glucose until stable
- Not eating within 2 hours
  - Combined glucose-insulin-potassium infusion, or separate glucose and insulin infusions as for major surgery
  - Combined infusion: 500 mL 0.45% saline with 5% dextrose + 10 mmol KCl + 10 units soluble insulin, at maintenance rate
  - Glucose hourly until awake and stable, then 2-hourly
  - Glucose >21 mmol/L: increase to 16 units per 500 mL
  - Glucose <7 mmol/L: reduce to 8 units per 500 mL

## Major or emergency surgery
- Continuous insulin infusion with separate IV fluids
- Insulin: 50 units soluble insulin in 50 ml 0.9% saline (1 unit/mL)
  - 0.05 units (mL)/kg/hour if glucose <15 mmol/L
  - 0.1 units (mL)/kg/hour if glucose 15 mmol/L or more
  - Should keep glucose between 5 and 12 mmol/L
- IV insulin half-life 3-4 min: do not stop it unless hypoglycaemic (glucose <3 mmol/L)
  - Then stop for 15 min
  - If hypoglycaemia keeps recurring, switch to 10% glucose fluids
- IV fluids: 0.45% saline with 5% dextrose + 10 mmol KCl per 500 mL bag, at 2.5 mL/kg/hour
- Hourly glucose before, during and after surgery
- U&E before surgery and at least daily on IV fluids
- Continue until eating and drinking; give usual subcutaneous insulin after the first meal and stop the infusion 1 hour later
- Insulin needs may be higher than usual early after surgery; the diabetes team advises

## Hypoglycaemia
- The commonest acute complication of type 1 diabetes
- Causes: missed meals, unplanned exercise, insulin errors
- Awake symptoms: hunger, sweating, tremor, tachycardia, anxiety, weakness, mood change, slurred speech, confusion; then seizures and coma if untreated
- Conscious and able to eat: oral carbohydrate
- Unconscious: 2 ml/kg of 10% dextrose, then IV fluids with 5% or 10% glucose

## Flashcards
- Q: Perioperative glucose target in a diabetic child?
  - A: 4-10 mmol/L.
- Q: Insulin infusion rate for major surgery?
  - A: 50 units in 50 ml; 0.05 units/kg/hour if glucose <15 mmol/L, 0.1 units/kg/hour if 15 or more.
- Q: When is IV insulin stopped, and for how long?
  - A: Only if glucose <3 mmol/L, for 15 min (half-life 3-4 min).
- Q: Treatment of hypoglycaemia in an unconscious child?
  - A: 2 ml/kg of 10% dextrose, then IV fluids with 5% or 10% glucose.
- Q: Glucose level that prompts a urine ketone check before minor surgery?
  - A: Over 15 mmol/L.
