# Myotonic Syndromes

Inherited disorders where muscle cannot relax after contraction: avoid suxamethonium and cold, expect respiratory and cardiac involvement, and monitor in high dependency.

## Overview

- Mostly autosomal dominant; muscle fails to relax after voluntary contraction or stimulation
- Trouble letting go after a handshake
- Myotonia triggered by cold, suxamethonium or surgical stimulation
- Three diseases: myotonic dystrophy, myotonia congenita, paramyotonia

## Myotonic dystrophy

- Commonest myotonia; prevalence 3-5:100000; usually presents in the second or third decade
- Autosomal dominant with variable penetrance; defect on chromosome 19
- Myotonia plus a typical pattern of wasting and weakness, progressive
  - Face: sunken temples, ptosis, hanging jaw
  - Muscles of mastication, sternomastoid
  - Bulbar: dysarthria, dysphagia
  - Distal limbs
- Features outside muscle
  - Early frontal balding
  - Cataracts
  - Low IQ
  - Cardiomyopathy, conduction defects (AV block, AF), mitral valve prolapse
  - Restrictive lung disease
  - Central and obstructive sleep apnoea
  - Delayed gastric emptying
  - Infertility
  - Adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, diabetes
- Neonatal form
  - Floppy baby with respiratory distress; may need ventilation for months
  - Weak cry, poor swallow (often tube-fed for the first weeks)
  - Facial diplegia (droopy face), tent-shaped mouth
  - Arthrogryposis of hands or feet, sometimes severe
  - Later childhood: becomes like the adult form
  - Mother may be diagnosed only after the affected baby is born
- Diagnosis
  - Family history
  - EMG of small hand muscles: repetitive discharges with delayed relaxation, fading in amplitude and frequency
  - Muscle biopsy (neonates) or lens opacities (adults) help prognosis
- Treatment supportive; drugs that raise the depolarisation threshold: phenytoin, carbamazepine, quinine, procainamide
- Death usually in the fifth or sixth decade

## Myotonia congenita (Thomsen disease)

- Autosomal dominant; 2:50000
- Myotonia without dystrophy; childhood stiffness, worse after rest
- Constant contraction gives a muscular look
- Normal life expectancy
- May improve with age, or need antimyotonic drugs (quinine, procainamide)

## Schwartz-Jampel syndrome

- Autosomal recessive
- Short stature, skeletal anomalies, persistent contraction and muscle hypertrophy
- Higher risk of malignant hyperthermia: avoid trigger agents

## Paramyotonia

- Rare, autosomal dominant; myotonia brought on by cold
- Keep patient warm

## Preoperative assessment

- Define the extent and distribution of weakness, focusing on respiratory and cardiac involvement
- Respiratory
  - Weak respiratory muscles, poor cough, bulbar palsy and recurrent aspiration reduce reserve
  - Lung function: restrictive pattern (reduced VC, ERV, maximum breathing capacity, maximal inspiratory pressure)
  - Consider blood gases in advanced disease or before major surgery
- Cardiovascular
  - Common at every stage; unrelated to severity of muscle disease
  - ECG for conduction problems (AF, bradycardia, ectopics, heart block)
  - Echo if cardiomyopathy or heart failure suspected

## Intraoperative management

- Airway and breathing
  - Usually intubate; aspiration risk from bulbar palsy, poor cough and slow gastric emptying
  - Very sensitive to respiratory depression from sedatives (barbiturates, benzodiazepines, propofol, volatiles) and opioids
  - Blunted central response to hypoxia and hypercapnia; central or obstructive sleep apnoea
- Circulation
  - Sensitive to myocardial depression from volatiles
  - Low threshold for an arterial line
- Myotonia triggers: cold, suxamethonium, anticholinesterases, shivering, surgical handling, diathermy to muscle
  - Keep warm throughout
  - Local anaesthetic infiltrated into the muscle may ease myotonia during handling
  - Non-depolarising relaxants do not relieve myotonia
- Suxamethonium contraindicated: masseter spasm, difficult intubation and ventilation, poor surgical conditions, potassium release
- Non-depolarising relaxants are safe
  - Increased sensitivity; may not be needed for intubation or surgery
  - Use neuromuscular monitoring
  - Titrate anticholinesterase reversal carefully: unpredictable and can provoke contraction; a short-acting relaxant may avoid the need
  - Intubation possible with a non-depolarising relaxant, a volatile, or an induction agent plus short-acting opioid
- Regional techniques usable, but muscle relaxation may not follow; regional analgesia spares opioids
- Possible weak link with malignant hyperthermia; volatiles widely used without problems; monitor temperature

## Postoperative management

- Chest physiotherapy to reduce pneumonia
- High dependency after all but the most minor procedures (frequent cardiorespiratory complications)

## Flashcards

- Q: Why is suxamethonium contraindicated in myotonia?
  - A: Causes myotonia including masseter spasm, difficult intubation and ventilation, and potassium release
- Q: Do non-depolarising relaxants relieve myotonic contraction?
  - A: No; local infiltration of the muscle may help instead
- Q: Chromosome and inheritance of myotonic dystrophy?
  - A: Chromosome 19, autosomal dominant with variable penetrance
- Q: Key cardiac checks in myotonic dystrophy?
  - A: ECG for conduction defects; echo for cardiomyopathy (involvement unrelated to muscle severity)
- Q: Which myotonic condition is triggered by cold?
  - A: Paramyotonia
