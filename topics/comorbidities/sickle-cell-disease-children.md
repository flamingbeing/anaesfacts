# Sickle Cell Disease in Children

Haemoglobinopathy of the beta-globin chain; perioperative aims are to prevent sickling by avoiding hypoxia, acidosis, cold and dehydration.

## Condition
- Haemoglobinopathy affecting beta-chain synthesis; autosomal dominant inheritance
- Mutation on chromosome 11: valine replaces glutamic acid at position 6 of the beta chain
- Sickle cell anaemia (HbSS): homozygous, severe
  - Chronic haemolytic anaemia, painful crises, respiratory compromise, end-organ damage, usually early death
- Sickle cell trait (HbAS): heterozygous, mild
  - Often no anaemia and no symptoms; sickles only with severe hypoxia
  - Some protection against falciparum malaria
- Epidemiology
  - Highest in tropical Africa and Madagascar: up to 40% of the population
  - Fairly common in Turkey, Greece, other Mediterranean countries and North Africa
  - UK black population: HbSS about 0.25%, HbAS up to 10%

## Clinical features
- Red cells with deoxygenated HbS sickle with hypoxia, acidosis, cold or cellular dehydration
- Deoxygenated HbS polymerises into stacked crystals that deform the cell membrane
- Sickle cells are stiffer and stickier: blood viscosity rises and microcirculatory flow falls
- Microvascular thrombosis worsens local hypoxia, causing a cycle of further sickling (spleen, bone)
  - Reversible at first; repeated sickling makes it irreversible
  - Irreversibly sickled cells live about 20 days
- Presents after 6 months of age as HbF falls
- HbF protects against sickling; higher HbF means milder disease
  - HbF 5-15% (HbS 85-95%) in sickle cell disease
- Pregnant women and neonates screened where incidence is high
- High mortality in the first 5 years:
  - Infection (pneumococcus, haemophilus, salmonella)
  - Sequestration crisis (sickling in organs with pooling of blood)
  - Aplastic crisis (sudden fall in haemoglobin, usually after parvovirus)
- Prevention: pneumococcal and H. influenzae vaccines, penicillin prophylaxis from 4 months, folate, teaching parents to recognise sequestration

## Preoperative assessment
- Screen at-risk patients with the Sickledex test
  - Sodium metabisulphite deoxygenates the sample; sickling shows HbS is present
  - Does not separate HbSS, HbAS and HbSC
  - If positive and time allows: haemoglobin electrophoresis
- Assess renal and respiratory end-organ damage
- Active infection: postpone elective surgery
- Hydroxyurea helps some patients by raising HbF: less sickling, fewer and milder crises

## Avoiding sickling
- Main goal: maintain oxygenation throughout
  - In HbSS, sickling becomes irreversible below PaO2 5.5 kPa; all cells sickle at SaO2 below 50%
  - Reserve is limited by chronic anaemia, lung damage and damaged vessels
  - Low threshold for postoperative oxygen
  - Tourniquets cause local hypoxia and may be contraindicated
- Avoid acidosis
- Avoid hypothermia: monitor temperature and actively warm; postoperative cold and shivering raise sickling risk
- Little evidence for fluid loading

## Transfusion (dilution of sickle cells)
- Controversial and not risk free
- Idea: replace HbS with transfused HbA, by exchange or simple transfusion
- Exchange versus simple transfusion: no difference in sickling, but more iatrogenic complications with exchange
- Recommended for homozygous patients having major surgery
  - Give 1-2 days before elective surgery
  - Target HbSS 30% and haematocrit 30%
  - Fresh blood preferred intraoperatively
- Alloimmunisation is common (Rhesus, Kell, Lewis): extended phenotype matching needed
- Cell salvage is not used: sickling in the cell saver is common

## Postoperative care
- Much higher surgical morbidity and mortality than normal
- Low threshold for HDU or ICU

## Sickle cell crises
- Painful crisis
  - Recurrent and acute; long bones, ribs, spine or abdomen (exclude a surgical cause)
  - Often opioid tolerant; multimodal analgesia led by the pain team, using what has worked before for that patient
  - Keep warm and well hydrated
- Acute chest syndrome
  - Fever, respiratory distress, new infiltrates on CXR, chest pain
  - Cause: infection (viral, mycoplasma) or after surgery from diaphragmatic splinting and atelectasis
  - Prevent: physiotherapy, good analgesia, early mobilisation, watch for worsening respiratory function
  - Treat: oxygen; ventilatory support if severe
  - May help: bronchodilators, dexamethasone, broad-spectrum antibiotics if postoperative pneumonia suspected
  - Transfusion may improve oxygenation and outcome in anaemic hypoxic patients
- Sequestration crisis
  - Second commonest cause of death in children with the disease
  - Blood pools in the spleen causing acute hypovolaemia
  - Treat with whole blood transfusion
- Aplastic crisis (sudden fall in haemoglobin and reticulocytes) and haemolytic crisis (often with painful crisis): transfusion and analgesia

## Flashcards
- Q: Below what PaO2 does sickling become irreversible in HbSS?
  - A: 5.5 kPa; all cells sickle at SaO2 below 50%
- Q: Preoperative transfusion targets before major surgery in HbSS?
  - A: HbSS 30% and haematocrit 30%, given 1-2 days before surgery
- Q: Why does sickle cell disease present after 6 months of age?
  - A: Protective HbF falls
- Q: What does the Sickledex test fail to distinguish?
  - A: HbSS, HbAS and HbSC; confirm with haemoglobin electrophoresis
- Q: Features of acute chest syndrome?
  - A: Fever, respiratory distress, new CXR infiltrates, chest pain
