# COPD, Bronchiectasis and Cystic Fibrosis

Chronic obstructive lung disease: optimise first, avoid bronchospasm and air trapping, clear secretions and favour regional and opioid-sparing techniques.

## Key points
- Suspect in long-term smokers
- Grade severity (GOLD), control and any active exacerbation
  - Steroids; smoking cessation
- Complications: respiratory failure, right heart failure, pneumothorax
- Avoid bronchospasm
  - Salbutamol puffs; adequate depth
  - Sevoflurane; avoid histamine-releasing drugs
  - Avoid intubation; LMA where possible
- Avoid air trapping: ventilate to allow full exhalation (low PEEP, slow rate, long expiratory ratio, low peak pressure)
- Suction secretions
- Regional anaesthesia (not interscalene); opioid-sparing

# COPD

## Optimisation
- At personal baseline: continue usual medicines and inhalers
- Not at best and surgery not urgent: step up treatment first
  - More inhaler use, inhaled or oral steroids, sometimes antibiotics
- Preoperative incentive spirometry may improve pulmonary outcomes
- Continue bronchodilators
- Start antibiotics if there are pneumonic changes

## Preoperative assessment
- History: cause, course and severity
  - Smoking, current medicines (including recent systemic steroids)
  - Exercise tolerance
  - Exacerbation frequency, most recent one, hospital admissions
  - Previous need for NIV or mechanical ventilation
  - Smoking-related comorbidities: diabetes, hypertension, peripheral vascular disease, ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, arrhythmias, lung cancer
- Continue long-acting bronchodilators, anticholinergics and inhaled steroids until the morning of surgery
- Optimise before elective surgery to reduce morbidity and mortality

## Pulmonary function testing
- Routine testing is controversial
- PFTs and ABG help predict function after lung resection, not complications after non-thoracic surgery
- Clinical features (smoking, diffuse wheeze, productive cough) predict complications better than spirometry
- Peripheral surgery: no PFTs needed
- If unsure, FEV1 alone is enough to grade severity
- Even "high-risk" results (FEV1 < 70% predicted, FEV1:FVC < 65%, PaCO2 > 45 mm Hg) allow surgery, including lung resection, at acceptable risk
- Use PFTs to guide optimisation, not to predict risk
- Indications for formal pulmonary evaluation (respiratory physician and/or PFTs)
  - (1) Unexplained hypoxaemia on air or home oxygen need
  - (2) Bicarbonate above 33 mEq/L or PCO2 above 50 mm Hg in previously unevaluated disease
  - (3) Previous respiratory failure from a problem still present
  - (4) Severe breathlessness from lung disease
  - (5) Planned pneumonectomy
  - (6) Lung function hard to assess clinically
  - (7) Need to tell apart causes of significant respiratory compromise
  - (8) Need to test bronchodilator response
  - (9) Suspected pulmonary hypertension
- Advanced disease: assess RV function clinically and by echo

## Flow-volume loops
- Lung volumes measure static function; flow rates measure dynamic function
- Expiratory flow plotted against volume gives a flow-volume curve; adding inspiration gives a loop
- Normal
  - Flow is zero at TLC; peak expiratory flow comes early, then falls linearly to RV
  - Inspiration from RV to TLC is fastest mid-way: U-shaped curve
- COPD
  - Lower expiratory flow at every lung volume
  - Expiratory limb concave upward, attributed in the note to uniform airway emptying
  - RV increased by air trapping

## Nutrition
- Low albumin (<3.5 g/dL) strongly predicts postoperative pulmonary complications
- Malnutrition raises the risk of prolonged air leak after lung surgery

## Intraoperative management: regional anaesthesia
- Preferred to GA: avoids laryngospasm, bronchospasm, barotrauma and hypoxaemia from intubation and positive pressure
- Suits extremity surgery and surgery not entering the peritoneum; lower abdominal surgery also possible
- GA is usual for upper abdominal and intrathoracic surgery
- Some studies show more postoperative respiratory failure after GA; unclear whether surgery or anaesthetic is to blame
- Peripheral nerve blocks carry low pulmonary risk
- Avoid interscalene block in severe COPD: causes ipsilateral phrenic palsy
- Regional is only useful if heavy sedation is not needed
  - COPD patients are very sensitive to respiratory depression from sedatives, especially the elderly
  - Small doses of midazolam are often tolerated
- Avoid sensory blocks above T6: impair active exhalation (expiratory reserve volume, PEFR, maximum minute ventilation), giving a weak cough

## Intraoperative management: general anaesthesia
- Volatiles
  - Rapid elimination (especially desflurane and sevoflurane) limits early residual respiratory depression
  - Bronchodilate; used for status asthmaticus
  - Desflurane irritates the airway; sevoflurane better for induction and emergence with reactive airways
  - Emergence can be slow with severe obstruction: trapped gas holds volatile in the lungs
- TIVA with propofol is an alternative
  - Remifentanil eases tube tolerance, lowering propofol needs and hypotension
- Nitrous oxide: great caution
  - Can enlarge or rupture bullae, causing tension pneumothorax
  - Limits inspired oxygen
- Volatiles blunt hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, increasing shunt: may need higher FiO2
- Opioids are less suited than volatiles for maintenance: slow clearance prolongs respiratory depression
  - Thiopental and midazolam effects also last longer in COPD
- ETT bypasses natural humidification: humidify gases and use low flows to keep secretions moist

## Ventilation
- Higher risk of ventilator lung injury: avoid dynamic hyperinflation and auto-PEEP
- Low tidal volume 6–8 mL/kg, peak pressure below 30 cm H2O, FiO2 for SpO2 above 90%: lower lung inflammatory markers
- Moderate to severe disease may have cystic air spaces that can rupture with positive pressure
- If unstable on the ventilator, think tension pneumothorax and bronchopleural fistula

## Air trapping (auto-PEEP, intrinsic PEEP, dynamic hyperinflation)
- Occurs with positive pressure and too little expiratory time
- Effects
  - Raised intrathoracic pressure reduces venous return and raises pulmonary artery pressure
  - Higher PVR strains the RV
  - Hyperinflated lungs compress the heart, limiting diastolic filling despite adequate preload
  - Septal shift and ventricular interdependence: distended RV impairs LV filling
- Detection
  - 1. Capnograph has no plateau and is still rising at the next breath (dead space mixing lowers ETCO2)
  - 2. Ventilator flow trace: expiratory flow not back to zero before the next breath
  - 3. Measure intrinsic PEEP with an expiratory hold
- Can cause serious instability by lowering preload
- Treatment: slower rate, longer expiration; disconnect from ventilator to let the patient exhale fully if urgent

## Bronchospasm
- Common, as in asthma; usually from airway manipulation or light anaesthesia
- Treatment
  - Deepen with volatile or propofol
  - Short-acting bronchodilator via the ETT
  - Suction secretions
  - IV steroids and/or adrenaline if first steps fail

## Oxygen
- Routine high FiO2 is questioned
  - Oxygen forms reactive oxygen species that damage nuclear and cell membranes
  - Surgery, anaesthesia and positioning promote airway closure and atelectasis
  - Atelectasis forms faster in lung units filled with 100% oxygen than with air

## Other hazards
- Barotrauma with bullae, especially when high airway pressures are needed
- Volatiles depress spontaneous ventilation more in COPD than in normal lungs

## Postoperative care
- Aim: keep lung volumes up, especially FRC (the key postoperative volume), and allow effective cough

### Lung expansion
- Deep breathing, incentive spirometry, chest physiotherapy and positive pressure techniques reduce complications in high-risk patients
  - All work better than nothing, by raising lung volumes and reducing atelectasis
- Incentive spirometry: cheap, simple, gives a target volume to reach and hold; needs cooperation
- Teaching before surgery works better than starting afterwards
- CPAP for patients who cannot do deep breathing or incentive spirometry
- Nasal positive pressure limits the expected loss of lung volume

### Analgesia
- Neuraxial opioids can allow early extubation
  - No sympathetic block, weakness or loss of proprioception: early walking possible
  - Walking raises FRC and improves oxygenation (better V/Q matching)
  - Especially useful after thoracic and upper abdominal surgery
  - Breakthrough pain may need systemic opioid
  - Sedation and delayed respiratory depression, especially with poorly lipid-soluble opioids such as morphine
- Neuraxial analgesia lowers pneumonia rates compared with parenteral opioids
  - Recommended after high-risk thoracic, abdominal and major vascular surgery
  - Intercostal or paravertebral blocks (single or continuous) if neuraxial is not possible

### Postoperative ventilation
- May be needed after major abdominal or thoracic surgery in severe COPD
- Likely if FEV1:FVC < 0.5 or PaCO2 > 50 mm Hg before surgery
- Correct chronic hypercarbia slowly: rapid correction causes metabolic alkalosis with arrhythmias, CNS irritability and seizures
- Targets: SpO2 about 90%, PaCO2 to keep pH 7.35–7.45
- Slower rate or lower I:E ratio gives more time to exhale but can cut minute ventilation, worsening hypercapnia, hypoxia and acidosis
  - Rising PVR then strains the RV
  - Acidaemia-driven electrolyte shifts can cause arrhythmias in COPD or asthma
- Extubation to CPAP or BiPAP may reduce air trapping and work of breathing
  - Concern: gastric insufflation, vomiting and aspiration with an unprotected airway
- Salbutamol and ipratropium may help if air trapping has a reactive component
- Fragile patients after extubation: trial of BiPAP lowers rate, work of breathing and reintubation
- Early mobilisation: deeper breaths, lung expansion and cough

# Bronchiectasis

## Background
- Acquired, from
  - Infection: CF, rheumatic disease, infections, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis
  - Impaired drainage: ciliary dyskinesia, smoking
  - Airway obstruction: foreign body aspiration
  - With or without a host defence defect
- Shares features with COPD: inflamed, collapsible airways; airflow obstruction; frequent admissions
- Diagnosis
  - Daily purulent sputum and cough
  - CT: bronchial wall thickening, dilated bronchi and bronchioles

## Preoperative assessment
- History: cause (CF, recurrent infection); cough, breathlessness, sputum
- Examination: SpO2, chest signs, signs of pulmonary hypertension
- Investigations
  - CXR: bronchiectatic changes (signet ring sign)
  - Spirometry, ABG, sputum culture

## Optimisation
- May need 3-10 days before major elective surgery of
  - IV antibiotics guided by sputum culture
  - Physiotherapy
- Preoperative prednisone if indicated; if on long-term steroids, increase the dose by 10mg
- Pseudomonas aeruginosa colonisation: ceftazidime plus gentamicin, or imipenem plus gentamicin
- May have coexisting asthma
- Involve a respiratory physician for full optimisation
- Maximal bronchodilators (nebulisers)
- Postpone surgery during an exacerbation

## Intraoperative management
- Regional if possible
- GA needs regular suctioning, so an ETT unless very short
- Appropriate analgesia
- Extubate sitting up

## Postoperative care
- Physiotherapy straight away
- Titrated O2
- IV antibiotics for at least 3 days
- Nutrition
- Respiratory physician if concerned

# Cystic Fibrosis

## Background
- Perioperative complication rate about 10%
- Faulty chloride channel (CFTR) in epithelial cells; gene on chromosome 7
- Loss of chloride transport upsets sodium and chloride balance needed for thin mucus
- Thick mucus is poorly cleared by cilia
- Exocrine failure
  - Respiratory: progressive lung disease, frequent chest infections, chronic hypoxaemia, cor pulmonale; chronic sinusitis and nasal polyps
  - Pancreatic insufficiency and CF-related diabetes
  - Hepatobiliary disease secondary (2nd) to obstruction: biliary cirrhosis, portal hypertension
  - Meconium ileus
  - Infertility
  - Osteoporosis
  - Chronic recurrent abdominal pain
  - Coagulopathy from poor vitamin K absorption

## Preoperative assessment
- History
  - Meconium ileus in neonates
  - Frequent chest infections; almost all have bronchiectasis symptoms
  - Nutritional needs; other systems; nasal polyps
  - Right heart failure (cor pulmonale)
  - Medicines: pancreatic enzymes, antibiotics
- Examination: weight, respiratory reserve, new chest signs, cachexia
- Investigations
  - FBC, U&E, CRP
  - Sputum microscopy, culture and sensitivity
  - ECG
  - CXR (bullae, pneumothorax), CT if needed
  - Spirometry (obstructive)
  - ABG
  - Echo (pulmonary hypertension)
  - Functional assessment: 6MWT or CPET

## Optimisation
- Exclude or treat active chest infection
- Chest physiotherapy before theatre
- Discuss with respiratory physician
- Reflux precautions
- May present for lung or liver transplant

## Intraoperative management
- As for bronchiectasis
- Minimise opioids to avoid constipation
- High barotrauma risk
- Sevoflurane (less airway irritation)
- Avoid nasal tubes if possible

## Postoperative care
- Close watch for chest infection (higher risk)
- Early extubation
- Monitor nutrition: 80% have malabsorption
- Early mobilisation and physiotherapy
- Multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia
- May need HDU
- Humidified O2
- Postoperative ventilation if FEV1 <1 litre

## Flashcards
- Q: Which ventilator settings limit air trapping in COPD?
  - A: Low tidal volume 6–8 mL/kg, slow rate, long expiration, low PEEP, peak pressure below 30 cm H2O.
- Q: Which preoperative values predict need for postoperative ventilation in COPD?
  - A: FEV1:FVC < 0.5 or PaCO2 > 50 mm Hg.
- Q: Why avoid interscalene block in severe COPD?
  - A: It causes ipsilateral phrenic nerve palsy.
- Q: Above which sensory level should regional blocks be avoided in COPD?
  - A: T6, as higher blocks weaken active exhalation and cough.
- Q: What is the immediate treatment for haemodynamic collapse from air trapping?
  - A: Disconnect from the ventilator to allow full exhalation.
