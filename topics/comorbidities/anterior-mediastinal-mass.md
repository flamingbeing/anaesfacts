# Anterior Mediastinal Mass

A mass that can compress the airway, great vessels and heart, often only after lying flat, induction or paralysis: risk stratify, keep spontaneous breathing, avoid paralysis, have a rescue plan.

## Key points
- Causes (4Ts): thyroid, thymoma, teratoma, "terrible" lymphoma
- Compression: airway, great vessels (SVC obstruction), heart; also pleural effusion
- Risk assess: safe vs uncertain vs unsafe
- Identify the position of comfort
- Avoid GA where possible; keep spontaneous ventilation, avoid paralysis
- Rigid bronchoscope and ECMO on standby

## Anatomy of the mediastinum
- Space between the 2 pleural cavities, from thoracic inlet to diaphragm
- Split into superior and inferior by the thoracic plane (horizontal line from the sternal angle, T4)
- Superior: thymus, aortic arch, SVC, areolar tissue, lymph nodes
- Inferior, divided by the pericardium into
  - Anterior
  - Middle: heart and pericardium, tracheal bifurcation, main bronchi, lung hila, phrenic nerves, lymph nodes
  - Posterior: descending aorta, oesophagus, vagus, sympathetic chain, thoracic duct, azygos and hemiazygos veins, lymph nodes

## Causes
- Anterior (4Ts)
  - Thyroid: retrosternal goitre (e.g. multinodular goitre, thyroid cancer)
  - Parathyroid adenoma or cancer
  - Thymoma, thymic cyst, thymic cancer, thymic hyperplasia
  - Teratoma (germ cell tumour)
  - Lymphoma, metastases, lymphadenopathy, granulomatous nodes (TB, sarcoidosis)
  - Cystic hygroma
  - Lipoma, granuloma
- Middle
  - Thyroid cancer
  - Lymphoma, metastases, lymphadenopathy, granulomatous nodes (TB, sarcoidosis)
  - Bronchial cancer, bronchogenic cyst
  - Pericardial cyst
  - Aortic aneurysm
- Posterior
  - Aortic aneurysm
  - Oesophageal cancer or cyst
  - Neurogenic tumours: neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroma, neurofibroma, schwannoma
- Other mediastinal structures can also give masses: trachea and bronchi, heart and pericardium, great vessels, oesophagus, spine

## Surgery
- Diagnostic: biopsy by mediastinoscopy or VATS
- Therapeutic: resection by median sternotomy or thoracotomy
- Unrelated emergency surgery, e.g. ascending aortic aneurysm, caesarean section

## Pathophysiology
- Mass effects often worsen when supine, after induction or after paralysis
- Airway (tracheobronchial) compression: extrinsic obstruction
  - Inability to ventilate after lying flat, induction or paralysis
  - Dynamic hyperinflation leading to obstructive shock
  - Tracheomalacia after surgery
- SVC obstruction
  - Difficult intubation (tongue and laryngeal oedema)
  - Cardiovascular collapse at induction from SVC collapse
  - Major venous bleeding even from minor trauma
  - Raised ICP
  - Poor drug delivery from arm cannulae: site IV access in the legs
- Cardiovascular compression: hypotension or arrest after lying flat, induction or paralysis
  - SVC: less RV preload, lower CO
  - Pulmonary artery: higher RV afterload (RV outflow obstruction) and less LV preload, lower CO
  - Aorta: higher LV afterload, lower CO
  - Heart itself: less RV preload, lower CO
- Vagus nerve compression and palsy: difficult intubation
- Oesophageal compression
- Pericardial invasion: effusion or tamponade, lower CO
- Pleural invasion: pleural effusion
- GA and paralysis worsen compression of airway and vessels
- IPPV raises intrathoracic pressure and worsens compression

## Goals
- Stratify risk of airway or cardiovascular collapse under GA: postural symptoms, Pemberton's sign, imaging
- Plan for safe, uncertain and unsafe patients; consider rigid bronchoscope, LMA or bypass on standby
- Know the rescue position
- Keep spontaneous ventilation; avoid neuromuscular blockade

## Other cancer considerations
- Metastases: depend on the lesion
- Medication
- Metabolic and systemic effects
  - Hyperthyroidism with retrosternal goitre
  - Myasthenia gravis with thymoma
  - Cervical nodes, hepatomegaly or splenomegaly with lymphoma
  - Paraneoplastic syndromes of lung and mediastinal tumours
    - Endocrine: hyperparathyroidism, Cushing's syndrome, SIADH, carcinoid syndrome
    - Neuromuscular: myasthenia gravis (thymic tumours; sensitive to non-depolarising relaxants), Eaton-Lambert syndrome (small cell cancer; sensitive to all relaxants), peripheral and autonomic neuropathy
    - Haematological: anaemia, thrombocytopenia, thrombosis
    - Others: nephrotic syndrome, amyloidosis

## Preoperative assessment
- Mass effects
  - Tracheobronchial: dyspnoea, orthopnoea, wheeze
    - Optimise: airway stent before surgery; may need rigid bronchoscopy first
  - Cardiovascular: dyspnoea, orthopnoea (can mimic airway obstruction), syncope (especially with Valsalva), hypotension, murmur, arrhythmia
  - SVC obstruction: facial flushing, distended neck veins, Pemberton's sign, airway or tongue oedema, hoarseness, CNS signs (headache, blurred vision, altered mental state)
    - Optimise: steroids (beware lymphoma), diuretics, SVC stent before surgery
  - Oesophageal: dysphagia
  - Vagus nerve: hoarseness
- Effect of exercise and posture: grade by tolerance of lying flat
  - Asymptomatic: lies flat with no symptoms
  - Mild: lies flat with some cough or pressure
  - Moderate: lies flat only briefly
  - Severe: cannot lie flat
- Find the rescue position (position of comfort), set by where the mass sits relative to the compressed structure: e.g. sitting upright, lateral, prone
- Systemic effects
  - Thymoma: hyperthyroidism
  - Myasthenia gravis: weakness

## Investigations
- CXR (PA and lateral): site of mass, pericardial effusion
- CT trachea and thorax: the key test
  - Site of mass and relation to nearby structures
  - Site, severity and extent of tracheal and vascular compression
  - Pericardial effusion
  - Practical points: scan takes < 20s, head and chest can be raised 30 degrees without spoiling it, can be done supine, lateral or prone
- TTE: for vascular compression symptoms or suspected effusion; shows cardiac, systemic and pulmonary vascular compression
- Lung function, especially flow-volume loops upright and supine
  - Distinguishes fixed from variable intrathoracic obstruction
  - A larger mid-expiratory plateau on lying flat is pathognomonic of variable intrathoracic obstruction and marks risk of airway collapse at induction
  - But correlation with degree of obstruction is poor (Slinger)
- Underlying disease: thyroid function; Tensilon test or acetylcholine receptor antibodies for myasthenia

## Predictors of perioperative respiratory complications
- Cardiorespiratory symptoms or signs at presentation
- CT: tracheal diameter < 50%
- Lung function: mixed obstructive and restrictive pattern, PEFR < 40%

## Preoperative optimisation
- Multidisciplinary: thoracic and ENT surgeons, medical and radiation oncology
- Avoid GA where possible
  - Local anaesthetic biopsy of the mass or a node (e.g. awake mediastinoscopy under local)
  - Non-surgical biopsy: CT-guided needle biopsy (local plus sedation), EBUS biopsy, ultrasound-guided pleural tap sitting up with little or no sedation (useful in lymphoblastic lymphoma, which often has effusions)
- High risk: consider chemotherapy first for chemosensitive tumours, or radiotherapy for large radiosensitive high-risk masses (leaving an unirradiated window for later biopsy); may blur the histology
- Counselling: high-risk surgery
- Premedication: avoid anxiolytics if tracheal obstruction is suspected

## Risk categories
- Safe
  - Asymptomatic adult
  - CT tracheal or bronchial diameter > 50% of normal
  - No CT signs of SVC, PA or cardiac compression
  - Management: cardiostable GA
- Uncertain
  - Adult with mild or moderate symptoms
  - Asymptomatic adult with CT diameter < 50% of normal
  - Symptomatic child with CT diameter > 50% of normal
  - Adult or child unable to give a history
  - Management: awake or asleep intubation
- Unsafe
  - Adult with severe symptoms
  - Child with CT diameter < 50% of normal
  - CT signs of SVC, PA or cardiac compression: risk of hypoxaemia, hypotension, arrest
  - Management: bypass standby

## Intraoperative management
### Monitoring and equipment
- Arterial line before induction, with or without CVP
- Airway: fibreoptic scope, with or without rigid bronchoscope
- Large-bore IV (in the legs if SVC obstruction), rapid infusers, blood in theatre

### Induction
- Sit the patient up in the position of comfort; some need induction semi-upright or fully sitting
  - Position may need changing after induction to relieve obstruction: the table must move easily
- Option 1: awake fibreoptic intubation
  - Safest and most reversible; avoids GA effects and best keeps spontaneous breathing
  - Lets you see the level and degree of compression before placing the tube
- Option 2: asleep, cardiostable, keeping spontaneous breathing until the airway is secure or the case is done
  - Inhalational induction with sevoflurane, or titrated IV propofol with or without ketamine
  - Ketamine: 1) FRC is unchanged during spontaneous breathing; 2) better rib cage tone stops the diaphragm moving cephalad, which may prevent mass-related obstruction
  - Intubate without relaxant, with airway topicalisation
  - Spontaneous breathing may not offset the haemodynamic effects of deep volatile with a large effusion, cardiac compression or cardiovascular compromise
- Tracheobronchial obstruction
  - Must be able to get past the obstruction
    - Proximal tracheal obstruction: ETT; consider reinforced tube; or FOB-guided endobronchial intubation of the open bronchus
    - Mid to distal trachea, carina or main bronchus: consider rigid bronchoscopy; bronchoscope and bronchoscopist present at induction
    - Other: distal jet ventilation through a proximally placed tube
  - Flexible scope on standby to see the obstruction
  - Consider heliox
    - Pros: laminar flow in critical obstruction, symptom relief, eases induction and weaning
    - Con: limits FiO2, a problem in hypoxaemic patients
- PA or cardiac compression (induce as for airway)
  - Keep preload: fluids
  - Keep contractility: avoid negative inotropes
  - Keep SVR: vasopressors
- SVC obstruction
  - Difficult airway: consider steroids
  - Fluid to support preload
  - Raised ICP precautions
  - IV cannulae in the legs
  - Blood available in theatre

### Unsafe patients
- Rigid bronchoscope and experienced bronchoscopist at induction
- Patient draped and surgeons scrubbed for immediate sternotomy and lifting the mass off
- Femoral vessels cannulated, perfusionist ready; consider elective femoro-femoral bypass before induction
  - If bypass is planned, start it electively before induction: emergency rescue bypass rarely ends well (Slinger)
  - Standby bypass at induction may not be safe: at least 5-10min to cannulate and establish flow and oxygenation, even with a primed pump and ready team

### Rescue for failed ventilation or collapse before resection
- Wake the patient, restore spontaneous breathing
- Move to the planned rescue position
- Immediate sternotomy and lifting the mass off the great vessels
- Emergency bypass
- Emergency rigid bronchoscopy to ventilate; then ventilate through it, place a single- or double-lumen tube under vision, or wait for spontaneous breathing

### Maintenance
- Spontaneous breathing throughout vs relaxant
  - Spontaneous breathing without paralysis keeps normal transpulmonary pressure and holds the airway open
  - First confirm the tube is beyond the obstruction
  - For IPPV, take over gradually by hand to prove ventilation works; if in doubt, stay spontaneous with occasional assisted breaths
  - Give relaxant only once IPPV is proven; use short-acting agents so the patient can be woken quickly if compression develops
- Watch for airway or cardiovascular compression during the case (e.g. surgical handling)
- Watch for bleeding, especially with SVC obstruction

### Emergence
- Consider staying intubated and ventilated if
  - Obstruction not relieved (e.g. diagnostic procedures)
  - Long-standing mass with tracheomalacia risk: FOB before extubation to exclude it
  - Long surgery
  - Intraoperative complications
  - Massive transfusion
  - Bypass needed
- Extubate awake, fully reversed; consider an airway exchange catheter

## Postoperative care
- At least high dependency; consider ICU
- Complications can follow an apparently good extubation
  - Respiratory: airway obstruction or oedema, pneumonia, atelectasis
  - Cardiovascular: hypovolaemia, hypotension

## Children
- Present with obstruction earlier: a smaller trachea means a small fall in diameter gives a large fall in area and rise in resistance

## VATS excision in a safe patient
- Thymoma: supine with the body tilted to one side, arms by the side; the machine comes in from the opposite side
- Patients tend to roll backwards more than in routine lung surgery: need back supports, not just 1 tight band around the hips as in normal VATS

  - A: 50% of normal
