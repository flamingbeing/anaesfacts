# Comorbid Diseases in Pregnancy

Haematological, liver, cardiac, respiratory, endocrine and neurological disease in pregnancy, and what each means for delivery and anaesthesia.

## Haemostatic disorders: general
- Multidisciplinary care with haematology involved
- Avoid external cephalic version, ventouse and mid-cavity forceps

## Thrombocytopenia: causes
- Gestational thrombocytopenia (70-80%)
  - Diagnosis of exclusion; cause unclear (possibly too little thrombopoietin plus higher consumption and turnover)
  - Usually late 2nd or 3rd trimester
  - Normal count before pregnancy; recovers within 2 months after delivery
  - 90% stay above 100 (mild); moderate 50-100; severe below 50
  - Usually no symptoms; no risk to mother or baby; no treatment or special mode of delivery needed
- Immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP)
  - About 4% of pregnant women with low platelets
  - Any trimester; commonest cause of low platelets in the 1st and 2nd trimester
  - Platelets below 100 × 10^9/L; may bruise easily
  - Antibodies usually present, but their absence does not exclude ITP
  - Platelets below 50: oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg (note reads "x10/day"), started a week before planned delivery, first line
  - Emergency caesarean: IVIG 1 g/kg plus platelet transfusion (platelets alone are rapidly consumed)
- Pre-eclampsia and HELLP (platelets below 100,000/ul needed for the diagnosis)
  - Dexamethasone 10 mg 12-hourly, 2-4 doses before delivery and 2 doses after
  - Worsening haemolysis, thrombocytopenia or renal failure: plasmapheresis
- Acute fatty liver of pregnancy
- DIC
- Other causes
  - Reduced production: marrow failure, folate or B12 deficiency, iron deficiency
  - Increased destruction: TTP, haemolytic uraemic syndrome; infection (HIV, hepatitis B or C, sepsis); autoimmune (SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome); drugs (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia)
  - Congenital: von Willebrand disease type 2b

## Minimum platelet counts
- Neuraxial block: above 70,000/uL (SOAP 2021), or above 80 by other societies
- Operative vaginal or caesarean delivery: above 50,000/uL (SOAP 2021)
  - ACOG: transfuse platelets in pre-eclampsia for active bleeding, or to reach 50,000 × 10^6/L before caesarean
- Vaginal delivery: above 30,000/uL
- Below 20,000/uL: higher risk of spontaneous bleeding
- Below 10,000/uL: higher risk of spontaneous internal bleeding

## Thrombocytopenia: workup
- Recent FBC; within 6 hrs if pre-eclampsia
- Coagulation screen

## Von Willebrand disease
- Neuraxial can be offered in type 1 if VWF activity and FVIII:C are above 0.5 IU/ml, with normal coagulation screen and platelets
- Neuraxial contraindicated in types 2 and 3
- Tranexamic acid
- Check sodium if given desmopressin (DDAVP)

## Haemophilia A and B
- Factor VIII or IX above 0.5 IU/ml before neuraxial block
- Recheck and correct before removing an epidural catheter

## Liver disease in pregnancy
- Acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP)
  - Starts with vague symptoms (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, malaise, anorexia), then acute liver failure: jaundice, encephalopathy, coagulopathy, hypoglycaemia
  - Marked liver failure (hypoglycaemia, encephalopathy, ascites, coagulopathy) points to AFLP rather than HELLP or severe pre-eclampsia
  - Commonest cause of acute liver failure in pregnancy
  - Hypertension in up to 50%
  - Usually third trimester; reported from 22 weeks to four days after delivery
- HELLP or severe pre-eclampsia
  - Hypertension in 85% of HELLP
  - Second half of pregnancy, usually third trimester; can present after delivery, usually within 48 hours
  - Large overlap with AFLP; both can coexist
- Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy
  - Itch is the key feature
  - Right upper quadrant pain, nausea, poor appetite, poor sleep or steatorrhoea possible
  - No hypertension
  - Usually late second or third trimester; brief first-trimester symptoms linked to ovarian hyperstimulation
  - Transaminases raised in 60%, usually under two times normal, but can exceed 1000 international units/L
  - Bilirubin raised in 25%; in over 90% total bilirubin stays below 6 mg/dL
  - Raised serum bile acids
- Hyperemesis gravidarum
  - First trimester onset, often into early second trimester, usually settled by 20 weeks
  - Abnormal liver tests in about 50% of those admitted
- Exclude non-pregnancy causes: viral hepatitis (B, E, herpes simplex), autoimmune hepatitis, gallstones, Budd-Chiari, paracetamol or other drug injury

## Cardiac disease: background
- Often the #1 cause of overall maternal death in developed countries (such as Singapore and the UK); counted as an indirect cause
- Pregnancy
  - Cardiac output rises 30-50%, mostly from stroke volume, later partly heart rate
  - SVR falls until the end of the second trimester, then slowly rises to term
  - Dilutional anaemia: plasma volume expands more than red cell mass
  - Higher flow raises gradients across existing valve lesions
  - Hypercoagulability raises thrombosis risk, a concern with prosthetic valves
- Labour
  - Cardiac output rises up to 30% in first stage and up to 80% just after delivery, from stroke volume, staying high for up to 24 hours
  - Each contraction autotransfuses 300-500 ml from placenta to circulation; systolic and diastolic BP rise
  - Neuraxial analgesia may cause transient hypotension from vasodilation
- After delivery
  - Preload rises as caval compression is relieved; blood loss may offset this
  - Haemodynamic shifts peak 24-72 hours after delivery: highest risk of heart failure from valve disease or ventricular dysfunction
- Risk factors for morbidity
  - NYHA III or IV, or cyanosis
  - Myocardial dysfunction
  - Left heart obstruction (aortic or mitral stenosis)
  - Previous arrhythmia
  - Previous cardiac event
- Prosthetic valves: switch warfarin to LMWH
- Cardiopulmonary bypass in pregnancy is high risk to the fetus
  - Normothermic with good pressures: 10% risk
  - Hypothermic: 30% fetal mortality

## Modified WHO risk classes (2018 ESC)
- Combines all maternal cardiovascular risk factors, including heart disease and comorbidity
- Class I very low, class II low to moderate, class III high, class IV extremely high (pregnancy contraindicated)
- Class I
  - 1) Uncomplicated small or mild pulmonary stenosis, patent ductus arteriosus, mitral valve prolapse
  - 2) Successfully repaired simple lesions: ASD, VSD, PDA, anomalous pulmonary venous drainage
  - 3) Isolated atrial or ventricular ectopics
- Class II
  - 1) Unoperated ASD or VSD
  - 2) Repaired tetralogy of Fallot
  - 3) Most arrhythmias
- Class II–III (depends on the patient)
  - 1) Mild left ventricular impairment
  - 2) Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
  - 3) Native or tissue valve disease not in class I or IV
  - 4) Marfan syndrome without aortic dilatation
  - 5) Aorta <45 mm with bicuspid aortic valve disease
  - 6) Repaired coarctation
- Class III
  - 1) Mechanical valve
  - 2) Systemic right ventricle
  - 3) Fontan circulation
  - 4) Unrepaired cyanotic heart disease
  - 5) Other complex congenital heart disease
  - 6) Aortic dilatation 40–45 mm in Marfan syndrome
  - 7) Aortic dilatation 45–50 mm with bicuspid aortic valve disease
- Class IV: seven conditions, pregnancy contraindicated
  - 1) Pulmonary arterial hypertension of any cause
  - 2) Severe systemic ventricular dysfunction (LVEF <30%, NYHA III–IV)
  - 3) Previous peripartum cardiomyopathy with any residual LV impairment
  - 4) Severe mitral stenosis; severe symptomatic aortic stenosis
  - 5) Marfan syndrome with aorta >45 mm
  - 6) Aortic dilatation >50 mm with bicuspid aortic valve disease
  - 7) Native severe coarctation

## Cardiac disease: preoperative assessment
- High index of suspicion, especially with risk factors
- Transthoracic echo
- Multidisciplinary: obstetrics, neonatology, cardiology
- Mode of delivery
  - Vaginal delivery with a titrated epidural preferred: less blood loss, more stable haemodynamics, lower DVT risk
    - Good epidural limits pain-driven tachycardia and afterload rise
    - Assisted second stage, to avoid maternal pushing
    - Caesarean usually for obstetric reasons only
  - Planned caesarean when vaginal delivery is contraindicated (usually WHO class IV)
    - Marfan syndrome with dilated aorta; aortic dissection
    - Severe aortic stenosis
    - Severe pulmonary arterial hypertension, including Eisenmenger syndrome: fixed pulmonary resistance cannot follow labour's output changes
    - Acute decompensated heart failure
- May need to deliver in a cardiac theatre

## Cardiac disease: intraoperative management
- Endocarditis prophylaxis if indicated
- Oxytocin with great caution: omit the bolus, use infusion only
- Avoid ergometrine: vasoconstriction, hypertension, raised risk of ACS and pulmonary oedema
- Avoid carboprost: may cause pulmonary oedema
- IABP or LVAD may be needed as a bridge to recovery or transplant
- Early invasive monitoring
- Regional: careful incremental epidural is safe in most; avoid single-shot spinal
- GA: blunt the hypertensive response to intubation; nitric oxide or volatile

## Cardiac disease: postoperative management
- First 1-2 hours after delivery are most critical, because of autotransfusion
- HDU or ICU care

## Specific cardiac conditions
- Pulmonary hypertension and Eisenmenger syndrome
  - High mortality (>70%)
  - Right heart failure, as lungs and RV cannot cope with changing cardiac output
  - Most deaths 2-9 days after delivery
  - Often elective GA caesarean with full invasive monitoring (consider PA catheter); regional still possible
  - Treatment: nifedipine, nitric oxide, prostacyclin, sildenafil
  - Goals: avoid rises in pulmonary pressure (standard care, caution with oxytocin, no carboprost); optimise RV preload and afterload
- Cyanotic heart disease
  - Avoid falls in SVR: top up epidural slowly, with phenylephrine to hold afterload
- Aortic stenosis
  - Avoid tachycardia and falls in afterload; keep sinus rhythm; GA or epidural top-up
- Mitral stenosis
  - Highest risk of the valve lesions; manage in a tertiary centre
  - Main danger is pulmonary oedema at delivery from the large rise in cardiac output, worse with pre-eclampsia
  - Large left atrium: start beta-blockers and heparin to prevent AF
  - Arterial line early in labour
  - Slow epidural onset
  - Minimal IV fluid: boluses of 200 ml at most
  - Vaginal delivery fine; for caesarean, epidural top-up or low-dose CSE
- Regurgitant lesions
  - Well tolerated, as lower SVR reduces the regurgitant fraction
- Marfan syndrome with aortic root dilatation
  - Root >4-4.5 cm before pregnancy: offer replacement first
  - Echo every 1-2 months through pregnancy
  - Highest dissection risk near term and just after delivery
  - Start beta-blockers early
- Heart failure
  - Diuretics, digoxin, hydralazine and nitrates are safe
- Myocardial infarction
  - Usually 3rd trimester; often no risk factors
  - Early angiography: LAD dissection is the commonest cause, treated with stenting
  - Otherwise standard ACS care
  - Try to delay delivery until 3 weeks after MI, with good analgesia
  - Clopidogrel rules out regional techniques
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy
  - Dilated cardiomyopathy in the final 1 month of pregnancy to 5 months after delivery
  - Cause unknown; often severe LV dysfunction; biopsy may show myocarditis
  - Initial treatment
    - Reduce preload: fluid restriction, diuretics
    - Reduce afterload: hydralazine, nitrates, amlodipine
    - Antiarrhythmic: digoxin
  - Other treatment
    - Low-dose beta-blocker improves survival in dilated cardiomyopathy
    - Heparin VTE prophylaxis if EF <35%
    - Avoid ACE inhibitors
  - May recover; otherwise later pregnancies carry high mortality
- Further reading: 2018 ESC guidelines on cardiovascular disease in pregnancy; 2017 AHA statement on complex congenital heart disease in pregnancy

## Respiratory disease
- Restrictive: sarcoidosis, fibrosis, post-radiation fibrosis, fibrosing alveolitis
- Obstructive: asthma, bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis
- Asthma is the most common
  - Reassure in early pregnancy, as minute ventilation increases
  - Airway reactivity may fall, though some worsen
- Fall in FRC with closing capacity within tidal volume can severely compromise some women
- Baseline lung function tests at booking are useful

## Endocrine disease
- Most common: diabetes and thyrotoxicosis
- Diabetes
  - Pre-existing (type I, type II, MODY) or gestational
  - Higher risk of pregnancy-induced hypertension, polyhydramnios, caesarean and preterm labour
  - Gestational diabetes: x2 caesarean rate versus non-diabetic women

## Neurological disease: general principles
- Avoid spinal if maternal tethered cord is a concern
- Avoid suxamethonium in most neurological conditions (acetylcholine receptor upregulation)
  - RSI options: high-dose rocuronium, or remifentanil 3 mcg/kg
- Avoid esmolol: fetal bradycardia
- Up to 40% report headache after delivery

## Epilepsy
- Epilepsy is the commonest cause of seizures in pregnancy; eclampsia the commonest around delivery
- Seizures increase in pregnancy: missed medication, altered pharmacokinetics, hormonal change

## Multiple sclerosis
- 10% of cases present in pregnancy
- No evidence of harm from neuraxial block, despite theoretical exposure of demyelinated cord to local anaesthetic
  - If worried, use epidural or CSE
  - Many consent for a chance of relapse after neuraxial block
- Treat intraoperative hypotension aggressively to protect cord perfusion
- Relapses may fall slightly in pregnancy but increase in the 3 months after delivery

## Spina bifida
- MRI required to exclude tethered cord; then block at an unaffected level
- 70% with cord abnormality have a dimple or hairy patch on the lower back
- Epidural
  - Higher risk of dural puncture from abnormal ligaments
  - More cephalad spread: more permeable dura, smaller epidural space
  - Less caudal spread: an epidural placed above the lesion does not spread down
  - Options: a second epidural below the lesion, or spinal injection or catheter
- Associated with difficult intubation

## Cerebrovascular events
- Cerebral venous thrombosis: more common after delivery; supportive care for ICP plus anticoagulation
- Subarachnoid haemorrhage
  - Higher aneurysm rupture risk, from greater blood volume
  - After clipping: no extra risk with vaginal delivery
  - Unclipped: caesarean is safer

## Infection
- Meningitis
- Spinal TB: consider in immigrants with low back pain

## Guillain-Barré syndrome
- Demyelinating polyneuropathy, often after a viral illness
- Regional: reduces autonomic instability by removing pain; more sensitive to local anaesthetic, so titrate slowly
- GA if respiratory or bulbar involvement

## Myasthenia gravis
- Autoimmune, commonly in young women
- Variable course: 40% deteriorate, mostly after delivery
- More preterm labour
- Sensitive to calcium channel blockers
- Avoid magnesium: can precipitate myasthenic crisis
- Optimise anticholinesterases before delivery; switch to IV for labour
- Aim for a short 2nd stage, as muscles fatigue
- Caesarean
  - Regional preferred; caution if breathing is impaired
  - GA: resistance to suxamethonium, with phase II block; use non-depolarising blockers sparingly (more sensitive)

## Neurofibromatosis
- Type 1: freckling and café-au-lait patches; peripheral nerve tumours; CNS involvement <10%
- Type II: CNS tumours in 90%, such as acoustic neuromas
- Regional contraindicated unless MRI excludes a local spinal lesion; patchy epidural block more likely
- Other risks: neurofibroma in the airway, cardiomyopathy

## Intracranial space-occupying lesions
- Regional contraindicated: sudden fall in CSF pressure can cause cerebellar herniation
- ICP rises up to 40 in labour and up to 70 in the 2nd stage
- So GA caesarean

## Spinal cord injury
- More aortocaval compression: lower circulating volume and less lower limb sympathetic tone
- Avoid suxamethonium for 9 months after injury
- Contractions not felt if the lesion is above T5
- Lesions above T5: risk of autonomic dysreflexia
  - Vasoconstriction below the lesion, vasodilation above
  - Hypertension, headache, pallor, bradycardia, which can progress to cardiac arrest
  - Labour is a strong trigger; the syndrome may be a sign of labour
  - A prophylactic epidural may protect
  - Antihypertensives may be needed
  - Risk lasts 48 hrs after delivery, so keep the epidural until then
  - Spinal for caesarean is fine

## Idiopathic (benign) intracranial hypertension
- Raised ICP with no mass, hydrocephalus or infection and normal CSF: a diagnosis of exclusion
- Morning headache, visual disturbance, nausea
- More common in obesity
- Worse in pregnancy, better after delivery
- Severe cases may need a lumboperitoneal shunt
- Delivery
  - Symptomatic: elective caesarean, avoiding the CSF pressure rise of labour
  - Asymptomatic: effective epidural and short instrumental 2nd stage
- Spinal may be unpredictable from altered CSF flow; CSE may be better
- Shunt in place: neuraxial contraindicated

## Flashcards
- Q: What platelet count does SOAP 2021 require for neuraxial block in pregnancy?
  - A: Above 70,000/uL (above 50,000/uL for operative delivery)
- Q: What share of pregnancy thrombocytopenia is gestational?
  - A: 70-80%
- Q: When do haemodynamic shifts peak after delivery?
  - A: 24-72 hours after delivery
- Q: Name the mWHO class IV conditions where pregnancy is contraindicated.
  - A: PAH, LVEF <30% or NYHA III–IV, peripartum cardiomyopathy with residual LV impairment, severe MS or symptomatic AS, Marfan aorta >45 mm, bicuspid aorta >50 mm, native severe coarctation
- Q: Above which spinal level does autonomic dysreflexia risk apply, and for how long after delivery?
  - A: Above T5; risk lasts 48 hrs after delivery
