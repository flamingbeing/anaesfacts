# Myasthenia Gravis and Lambert-Eaton Syndrome

MG patients are resistant to suxamethonium and very sensitive to non-depolarising blockers; prefer regional anaesthesia, avoid relaxants where possible, and reverse with sugammadex.

## Principles of management
- Risk assessment: lung function tests, Osserman grade, Leventhal criteria
  - 20/30/40 rule can guide the need for intubation
- Continue pyridostigmine and prednisolone
- Regional anaesthesia is better than GA
  - Avoid interscalene blocks and high spinals
- Avoid paralysis if possible
  - Reduce non-depolarising relaxant doses to 20%; rocuronium is ideal
  - Suxamethonium can be used (unlike other neuromuscular conditions) but the dose needs increasing x2
  - Reverse with sugammadex rather than neostigmine whenever possible
- Avoid volatiles
- Avoid drugs that worsen weakness: aminoglycosides, some analgesics (lignocaine, magnesium, gabapentin), beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, antiepileptics (phenytoin), phenothiazines
- Bulbar weakness gives aspiration risk: RSI
- Plan extubation criteria
- Risk of myasthenic crisis or cholinergic crisis (SLUDGE)
- Check for thymoma or anterior mediastinal mass

## Neuromuscular blocking drugs
- Avoid unless truly necessary; most operations do not need them
  - Potent volatiles, and to a lesser degree deep IV anaesthesia, often give enough relaxation
- If needed: rocuronium or vecuronium, then sugammadex
- Response to blockers and to reversal is variable and unpredictable, even in ocular-only MG or remission
  - Reversal can trigger cholinergic crisis
  - Anticholinesterase therapy alters the depth and duration of block
- Monitor with quantitative train-of-four whenever blockers are used

### Suxamethonium (depolarising)
- MG patients are resistant, probably because they have fewer acetylcholine receptors
- ED95 is 2.6 times normal (0.8 versus 0.3 mg/kg)
- Anticholinesterases (e.g. pyridostigmine) inhibit plasma cholinesterase and may prolong its action
- Higher risk of phase II block, especially with repeated doses

### Non-depolarising blockers
- Extremely sensitive (rocuronium, vecuronium, cisatracurium)
  - Tiny doses or residual block can cause respiratory distress or loss of airway protection after emergence
- Give small increments of 0.1 to 0.2 times the ED95, titrated to effect with quantitative TOF monitoring
- If extubation is planned, use a steroidal agent (rocuronium or vecuronium) so sugammadex can be used
- Mivacurium is broken down by plasma cholinesterase; pyridostigmine taken that morning may prolong block (variable)

## Reversal
- Neostigmine
  - Unpredictable, especially in patients on anticholinesterases
  - Titrate to effect to avoid cholinergic crisis
- Sugammadex is recommended
  - Unaffected by anticholinesterase therapy; reverses block rapidly, predictably and safely in MG
- Confirm reversal with a quantitative TOF ratio >0.9

## Drugs that may worsen MG
- Antibiotics: aminoglycosides, tetracycline
- Steroids: avoid dexamethasone
  - Glucocorticoids can cause weakness despite being an MG treatment; avoid starting them perioperatively
- Lignocaine, magnesium, gabapentin
- Beta blockers, calcium channel blockers
- Antiarrhythmics (procainamide)
- Lithium

## Preoperative assessment
- Coordinate elective surgery with the patient's neurologist
- History and examination
  - Degree of weakness: ocular or generalised
  - Bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysarthria, nasal or quiet speech): aspiration risk
  - Respiratory muscle weakness, breathlessness
  - Previous myasthenic crisis and need for intubation
  - Current MG therapy
  - Other autoimmune diseases (thyroiditis, rheumatoid arthritis, SLE)
- Investigations
  - AChR antibody
  - Repetitive nerve stimulation
  - ECG (may show myocardial changes)
  - Electrolytes
  - Single breath count test
    - One breath in, then count aloud at about two counts per second until the next breath is needed
    - Reaching 30 suggests adequate lung function; 20 or less suggests significant inspiratory weakness
    - Each count is roughly 116 mL of vital capacity at two counts per second

### Respiratory function
- 20/30/40 rule suggests need for intubation
- Forced vital capacity (FVC)
  - FVC < 2.9L (Leventhal) or <60% predicted strongly predicts postoperative ventilation
  - Concern: <20 mL/kg
- Negative inspiratory force (NIF) or maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP)
  - Most negative pressure generated against an occluded airway on inspiration; reflects diaphragm strength
  - Normal: >-60 to -100 cmH2O
  - Low risk: >-40cmH2O
  - Concern: NIF >-30 cmH2O means significant weakness
  - Crisis or intubation threshold: often -20 cmH2O
- Maximum expiratory pressure (MEP)
  - Most positive pressure generated against an occluded airway on expiration; reflects cough strength and secretion clearance
  - Concern: MEP < 40 cm H2O

### Thymic mass
- Patients having thymectomy for a thymic mass may obstruct their airway on induction; review chest CT

## Severity classification
- Osserman classification
  - Grade I: ocular only
  - Grade IIa: mild generalised, responds well to treatment
  - Grade IIb: moderate generalised, responds less well
  - Grade III: severe generalised
  - Grade IV: myasthenic crisis needing ventilation
- Quantitative MG (QMG) score: 0 to 39; higher means more severe
  - Items: ocular (2 items), facial (1 item), bulbar (2 items), gross motor (6 items), axial (1 item), respiratory (1 item)

## Predicting postoperative myasthenic crisis
- Retrospective thymectomy studies (transsternal and videoscopic) link several factors to postoperative ventilation
- Leventhal criteria (four predictors of postoperative ventilation)
  - (1) MG duration of 6 years or more
  - (2) Chronic respiratory disease
  - (3) Pyridostigmine 750 mg per day or more
  - (4) Vital capacity of 2.9 litres or less
- Patient factors
  - Previous myasthenic crisis or respiratory failure
  - Disease burden: duration over six years, preoperative bulbar symptoms, Osserman grade III / IV
  - Chronic lung disease
  - Investigations: vital capacity <2.9L; AChR antibody >100 nmol/mL; larger decrement (18 to 20 percent) on low frequency repetitive nerve stimulation
  - Treatment: pyridostigmine >750 mg/day (starting dose 60mg every 6 hours); preoperative steroids
  - Thymoma
- Surgical: major surgery, e.g. blood loss >1000 mL
- Anaesthetic: GA with neuromuscular block (rather than regional)

## Preoperative optimisation
- Timing: operate in a stable phase needing minimal immunomodulation or steroids
- First on the list: patients are strongest early in the day
- Continue anticholinesterases up to and including the morning of surgery
  - Stopping them can quickly cause respiratory and bulbar weakness
  - They alter the response to both types of blocker and make reversal unpredictable
  - Pyridostigmine: onset 15 to 30 minutes, peak at about two hours, lasts three to four hours or longer
  - Dosing is individualised; often started at 30 mg orally and titrated
  - IV dose is about one-thirtieth of the oral dose (1 mg IV equals 30 mg PO)
- Continue steroids; consider stress dosing
  - Long-term steroids may suppress the HPA axis and cause adrenal insufficiency
  - Not at risk: any dose for less than three weeks; morning prednisone <5 mg daily (or equivalent) for any duration; <10 mg prednisone every other day; continue the usual regimen
  - Prednisone >20 mg/day for three weeks or more, or Cushingoid appearance: give stress-dose steroids before induction
- Long-term immunotherapy can be held briefly
  - Includes azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, ciclosporin, methotrexate, mycophenolate, rituximab, tacrolimus
  - No published guidance; long duration of action means a missed morning dose is unlikely to matter
  - Check electrolytes, renal and liver function, and full blood count
  - Azathioprine does not meaningfully affect non-depolarising block in humans
- Other optimisation
  - IVIG has been used before thymectomy
  - Respiratory muscle training

## Premedication
- Reassurance and explanation often avoid the need for sedatives
- If needed, give the smallest dose in increments (e.g. midazolam 0.5 mg IV) and watch for bulbar or respiratory weakness

## Intraoperative management

### Choice of technique
- Use locoregional anaesthesia when possible
  - Avoids IPPV and aspiration risk
  - Opioid sparing, less respiratory depression
  - May avoid the need for neuromuscular blockers

### Regional anaesthesia
- Choose amide local anaesthetics (ropivacaine, mepivacaine, bupivacaine, lidocaine) over esters
  - Anticholinesterases may slow ester hydrolysis and prolong block; high ester concentrations (e.g. procaine) may impair neuromuscular transmission
- Neuraxial: mid-thoracic or higher blocks paralyse accessory respiratory muscles; poorly tolerated with respiratory or bulbar weakness
- Supraclavicular and interscalene blocks paralyse the diaphragm via the phrenic nerve for hours; poorly tolerated with respiratory compromise

### Drugs
- Avoid neuromuscular blockers when possible
- Also avoid aminoglycosides, some analgesics (ester local anaesthetics, magnesium), beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, phenytoin, phenothiazines

### Airway
- Consider RSI with bulbar weakness and aspiration risk
- To intubate without relaxants and blunt reflexes such as laryngospasm:
  - Propofol: rapid onset, short acting, suppresses airway reflexes
  - High-dose remifentanil
  - Spray the cords with lignocaine

### Maintenance
- Avoid maintenance relaxants where possible
- Remifentanil is useful for relaxant-free anaesthesia

### Extubation
- Plan to extubate at the end: short acting drugs, multimodal analgesia to limit opioids, chest physiotherapy, avoid drugs that impair neuromuscular transmission
- Usual criteria: adequate ventilation and oxygenation, strength and airway protection, with objective confirmation of reversal if blockers were used

## Postoperative care
- May need high dependency care, or ICU for ventilatory support
  - Decide on clinical features, surgery, anaesthetic, intraoperative and recovery course, and pain needs
- Plan postoperative anticholinesterase management with the neurologist beforehand
- Restart long-term immunotherapy once taking oral medication
- Day surgery is possible for minor procedures under sedation, regional anaesthesia or GA without relaxants
  - Admit anyone with bulbar or respiratory weakness in recovery

### Breathlessness or desaturation after surgery
- Residual block (TOF <0.9)
- Myasthenic crisis (dry)
- Cholinergic crisis after neostigmine (secretions, bronchospasm)
- Opioids (check pupils)
- Upper airway obstruction or compression by haematoma
- Others: atelectasis, PE, pneumothorax, aspiration

## Myasthenic crisis
- Respiratory or bulbar weakness severe enough to need intubation or delay extubation
- Triggers: surgical stress alone, infection, residual anaesthetic drugs, stopping or tapering MG treatment, drugs that worsen MG
- Must be told apart from cholinergic crisis, as treatment differs; neurophysiology may help
- Warning signs: dysphagia, voice change, obstruction, weak cough, poor secretion handling
  - Respiratory drive is normal, so the first sign may be a faster rate with shallower breaths
  - Accessory muscle use or paradoxical abdominal movement, even while still intubated
  - Blood gases may first show hypocapnia; a rising pCO2 means impending respiratory failure
- Management
  - Coordinate with a neurologist
  - Delay extubation; intensive care
  - Urgent plasma exchange or IVIG, plus immunomodulation
  - IV pyridostigmine is 1/30 of the oral dose

## Cholinergic crisis
- Risk in anyone receiving anticholinesterases
- Paradoxical weakness with cholinergic excess (SLUDGE: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI upset, emesis)
- Rare outside theatre; may follow anticholinesterase reversal in MG, often with prolonged paralysis
- Avoiding blockers avoids reversal and so this risk
- Treatment: atropine (0.4 to 2 mg IV) or glycopyrrolate (0.2 to 1 mg IV) to block muscarinic effects, if not already given; withhold further anticholinesterase; repeat doses may be needed

## Drug safety in MG

### May unmask or worsen MG (contraindicated)
- Neuromuscular blockers
- Antibiotics
  - Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, norfloxacin): black box warning
  - Ketolides (telithromycin, solithromycin): black box warning
  - Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, neomycin, tobramycin)
    - Impair neuromuscular transmission by any route; dose dependent and related to serum level
    - Neomycin is the most potent
- Cardiovascular
  - Beta blockers (atenolol, labetalol, metoprolol, propranolol): rarely cause frank weakness in normal people but often cause fatigue
  - Calcium channel blockers
  - Procainamide, quinidine, trimetaphan
- Some antimalarials: chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine, quinine
- Others: anti-PD-1 antibodies (nivolumab, pembrolizumab), botulinum toxin, magnesium preparations, lithium, penicillamine, phenytoin

### Usually tolerated but occasionally worsen MG
- Anaesthetic agents: volatiles (isoflurane, halothane); local anaesthetics if given intravenously by accident
- Antibiotics and antivirals: clindamycin, tetracyclines (doxycycline, tetracycline), macrolides (azithromycin, clarithromycin, erythromycin), sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, vancomycin, colistin, polymyxin B, colistimethate, antiretrovirals (ritonavir)
- Anticonvulsants: carbamazepine, ethosuximide, gabapentin, phenobarbital
- Psychiatric: butyrophenones (haloperidol), lithium, phenothiazines (chlorpromazine, prochlorperazine)
- Eye drugs: betaxolol, echothiophate, proparacaine, timolol, tropicamide
- Others: cisplatin, emetine (ipecac), fludarabine, glatiramer, interferon alpha, interleukin-2, iodinated contrast, riluzole
- Glucocorticoids (dexamethasone, methylprednisolone, prednisone)
  - High doses can markedly worsen MG early in treatment; start high doses only in hospital alongside plasma exchange or IVIG for crisis

### Considered safe
- Penicillins, cephalosporins (rare reports of ampicillin causing respiratory depression)
- Linezolid, daptomycin, metronidazole, teicoplanin
- Fluconazole (not voriconazole)

## Pregnancy
- Antenatal anaesthetic review; plan labour analgesia and operative delivery anaesthesia early
- Assess bulbar and respiratory weakness and whether a mid-thoracic block would be tolerated
- Labour analgesia
  - Neuraxial is preferred: reduces or avoids systemic opioids and respiratory depression
  - Low dose dilute local anaesthetic with opioid gives good analgesia with little motor block
- Instrumental delivery
  - First stage (uterine smooth muscle) is unaffected; second stage (striated muscle) may weaken, so instrumental delivery is more likely
  - Extend the epidural to lumbosacral levels for forceps or vacuum
- Caesarean section
  - Neuraxial is usual, but the mid-thoracic block affects accessory respiratory muscles
  - Significant bulbar or respiratory weakness: GA
  - Alternative: slowly titrated epidural if time allows
- Pre-eclampsia
  - Magnesium is contraindicated
  - Use phenytoin for seizures
- Neonatal MG
  - 10–20% of neonates develop transient MG from placental transfer of AChR antibodies
  - Paediatric or NICU team must attend the delivery

## Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS)
- Rare presynaptic disorder: reduced acetylcholine release; much rarer than MG
- Often linked to small cell lung cancer or other cancers; can be autoimmune
- Key differences from MG
  - Sensitive to both suxamethonium and non-depolarising blockers (more sensitive to non-depolarisers than MG)
  - Strength improves with exercise; reflexes absent
  - Autonomic dysfunction is common
  - Bulbar symptoms less common; respiratory failure is rare
- Neostigmine reversal may be ineffective
- Avoid blockers unless essential; if used, small titrated doses with quantitative monitoring
- Treatment: guanidine, aminopyridines (e.g. 3,4 diaminopyridine), anticholinesterases (pyridostigmine); IVIG; oral immunosuppressants (prednisone, azathioprine, mycophenolate, ciclosporin); less often plasma exchange or rituximab

### Perioperative care
- Continue treatment up to surgery: stopping it has caused respiratory failure
- Long-term steroids may need stress dosing at induction
- Autonomic dysfunction can cause exaggerated hypotension with induction agents and vasodilators
- Postoperative concerns as for MG
