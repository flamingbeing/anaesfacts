# Haemophilia and von Willebrand Disease in Children

The three commonest inherited bleeding disorders: haemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency), haemophilia B (factor IX deficiency) and von Willebrand disease (vWF deficiency or dysfunction).

## Haemophilia A: background
- X-linked recessive; 1:5000 males; mutation at Xq28
- Family history positive in two-thirds
- Severity tracks factor VIII activity (VIIIc) as % of normal:
  - Severe <1%
  - Moderate 2-5%
  - Mild 5-30%
  - Normal 50-200%
- Split of severe, moderate and mild: 50%, 10% and 40%
- Age at presentation depends on severity and type of trauma

## Haemophilia A: clinical features
- Spontaneous bleeds into joints (ankles, knees, hips, elbows) and muscles
  - Painful swelling, loss of function, degenerative arthritis and deformity; muscle necrosis and contractures
- Intracranial haemorrhage after minor head injury in severe disease
- Haematuria is common
- Even mild cases can bleed life-threateningly after surgery (dental extraction, circumcision, tonsillectomy, childbirth)

## Haemophilia A: treatment
- Recombinant factor VIII concentrate is the mainstay
- Half-life 8-12 hours; may need dosing 2 or 3 times daily
- Prophylaxis usually 3 times a week from walking age to limit joint damage

## Haemophilia A: preoperative assessment
- Involve the haematologist and haemophilia nurse specialist; coordinate closely
- Coagulation: prolonged PTT; normal TT, PT and fibrinogen
- Bleeding time normal except in the most severe cases
- Get a baseline factor VIIIc assay
- Know beforehand the dose, frequency and effect of factor VIII on VIIIc levels
- Check for factor VIII antibodies (inhibitors)
  - Present in 10-15%; reduce factor VIII effect
  - Operate only for important procedures
  - Recombinant factor VIIa is an alternative: forms a complex with tissue factor at the bleeding site and activates the extrinsic pathway

## Haemophilia A: factor VIII dosing for surgery
- Sustained levels over 50% needed for surgery
- About 15 IU/kg raises the level by 30%
- Typical: 50 IU/kg just before surgery, 25 IU/kg that evening, then twice daily for the first week
- Dental surgery: usually a single dose of 25 IU/kg
- Check peak level about 20 minutes after a dose; aim for over 100%
- Daily assays for the first postoperative week to keep levels from falling below 50%
- Intraoperative redosing may be needed in long surgery or massive blood loss

## Haemophilia A: other agents
- Tranexamic acid: usually continued for about 1 week after the procedure
- Desmopressin (DDAVP): releases stored factor VIII and vWF from endothelium
  - Mild or moderate haemophilia, minor surgery (e.g. dental): may raise factor VIII enough
  - Dose 0.3 mcg/kg IV

## Haemophilia A: intraoperative and postoperative care
- Avoid IM injections, NSAIDs and regional techniques
- Use oral analgesia, PCA or IV infusions
- IV access can be hard; most have a portacath for regular treatment; access it with strict asepsis
- Hepatitis B and C rates should be falling with recombinant factor VIII

## Haemophilia B (Christmas disease)
- X-linked factor IX deficiency
- 4 times less common than haemophilia A
- Clinically identical to haemophilia A; distinguished only by factor VIIIc and IX assays
- Long PTT; normal PT, TT, fibrinogen and bleeding time
- Treatment: heat-treated factor IX concentrate (e.g. BeneFIX)
  - Long half-life (>18 hours), so once daily dosing for bleeds
- Desmopressin does not work

## von Willebrand disease: background
- Commonest inherited bleeding disorder, about 1% of the population
- Defect on chromosome 12; affects both sexes
- vWF has two roles:
  - Platelet adhesion and aggregation at injured vessels (platelet plug)
  - Carrier for factor VIII, protecting it from early activation and breakdown
- Deficiency therefore impairs both platelet plug and fibrin formation (secondary factor VIII deficiency)

## von Willebrand disease: types
- Type I: mildest and commonest (60-80%)
  - Moderate deficiency of vWF and factor VIII (5-30% of normal)
  - Autosomal dominant
  - Bruising after trauma or surgery; rarely spontaneous bleeding but epistaxis or menorrhagia; often never diagnosed
- Type II (10-30%)
  - Autosomal dominant or recessive; qualitative vWF defect
  - Subtypes IIA, IIB, IIM, IIN
  - IIB: reduced vWF with shortened platelet survival and thrombocytopenia
  - Spontaneous skin and mucosal bleeding (gut, nose, dental)
- Type III: most severe
  - Autosomal recessive
  - No vWF or factor VIII; frequent severe bleeds including joints and muscles

## von Willebrand disease: investigations
- Results can vary in one person; repeat tests to confirm or exclude
- Bleeding time grossly prolonged (normal in haemophilia A)
- Raised PTT; normal PT, TT and fibrinogen
- Reduced platelet aggregation and adhesion
- Reduced factor VIII and vWF antigen
  - Mild: vWF antigen 20-40% of normal; severe: below 10%
- vWF activity: ristocetin cofactor assay
- vWF multimer analysis helps type the disease and guides treatment

## von Willebrand disease: treatment
- Desmopressin (IV, subcutaneous or intranasal): releases the patient's own factor VIII and vWF
  - Ineffective in some type II (especially IIB) and all type III
  - In IIB it can cause platelet aggregation and thrombocytopenia
  - Tachyphylaxis: limit to 3 doses in 48 hours
- Cryoprecipitate, recombinant factor VIII or purified vWF concentrate
- Adjuncts: antifibrinolytics, platelets, oral contraceptive pill
- Types I and II: prophylaxis only before invasive procedures or with spontaneous bleeding
- Type III: may need prophylaxis for recurrent mucosal or joint bleeds

## von Willebrand disease: anaesthetic management
- Plan jointly with haematologist and surgeon
- Surgery on mucosa: normalise the bleeding time first
- Commonest agents: desmopressin and tranexamic acid
- Dental extraction: desmopressin plus tranexamic acid 15-25 mg/kg orally
- Desmopressin 0.3 mcg/kg IV in 10 mL normal saline over 10 min
  - Watch for tachycardia, hypotension, facial flushing
  - Intranasal spray also available
- Elective surgery: often admitted for a desmopressin trial
  - Sample 45-60 min after the dose; hope for factor VIII and vWF over 50% of normal
  - If treating for >2 days, monitor levels (tachyphylaxis)
- Do not give desmopressin to known non-responders, type IIB or III, or children <2 years
- Antidiuretic effect: monitor fluid balance and electrolytes with repeated doses; under 2 years at higher risk of hyponatraemia
- Tranexamic acid orally and as mouthwash for up to 7 days after
- Desmopressin non-responders: single dose of recombinant factor VIII or vWF
- Major surgery: haematology advice on cryoprecipitate, vWF concentrate, pasteurised factor VIII (Humate-P) and platelets
- Avoid aspirin and other NSAIDs, and IM injections
- Universal precautions: higher rates of hepatitis C and HIV from repeated blood products

## Flashcards
- Q: Factor VIII level needed for surgery in haemophilia A, and the usual preoperative dose?
  - A: Sustained over 50%; about 50 IU/kg just before surgery, then 25 IU/kg that evening.
- Q: How much does 15 IU/kg of factor VIII raise the level?
  - A: By about 30%.
- Q: Desmopressin dose and administration for vWD?
  - A: 0.3 mcg/kg IV in 10 mL normal saline over 10 min.
- Q: Who should not get desmopressin?
  - A: Known non-responders, vWD type IIB or III, children under 2 years, and haemophilia B (ineffective).
- Q: Which test separates vWD from haemophilia A?
  - A: Bleeding time: grossly prolonged in vWD, normal in haemophilia A.
