# Epilepsy and Vagus Nerve Stimulators

Keep antiepileptics going, choose drugs that do not lower the seizure threshold, and plan for VNS devices.

## Definitions and classification
- Seizure: paroxysmal bursts of abnormal neuronal activity, seen as altered consciousness or motor, sensory or autonomic events
- Epilepsy: recurrent unprovoked seizures
- Childhood incidence about 4-9:1000; about 60% of all epilepsy is diagnosed in childhood
- Classified by observed features: generalised or partial
- Generalised: usually loss of consciousness, no localised onset
  - Absence: loss of consciousness without abnormal movement; sometimes low tone and automatisms
  - With abnormal movement: atonic, myoclonic, tonic, clonic or tonic-clonic
  - May have a prodrome (behaviour change) or aura; afterwards unconscious and drowsy (postictal phase)
- Partial: localised onset
  - Simple partial: limb jerking, odd taste or smell, hallucination, paraesthesia; may spread to other areas and become generalised
  - Complex partial: localised, with impaired consciousness

## Status epilepticus
- Seizure lasting >30 min, or repeated seizures over a 30 min period without regaining consciousness
- Medical emergency; mortality up to 4% in children
- Early: cerebral metabolism, blood flow, glucose and lactate rise; catecholamine surge with high cardiac output, hypertension, tachycardia and raised CVP
  - These compensations protect the brain for the first 30–60 min
- Later: compensation fails and brain damage may follow; also arrhythmias, hypertension, pulmonary oedema, DIC, myoglobinuria
- These changes are faster in convulsive status but also occur in non-convulsive status (NCSE)
- Refractory status (seizures continue despite two AEDs, e.g. benzodiazepine and phenytoin): high complication risk
  - Induce and maintain GA with midazolam, propofol or barbiturates (thiopental, pentobarbital)
  - High-dose propofol: caution (propofol infusion syndrome); not recommended in children
  - EEG to titrate doses and confirm electrographic seizures have stopped
  - Continue maximal therapy for 12–24 h after the last clinical or EEG seizure, then taper; restart or change if seizures recur

## Paediatric seizures
- Febrile convulsions: 2-5% of children under 5 years
  - Up to a third recur
  - Later epilepsy risk: 1% overall, up to 10% with a family history
- Epilepsy syndromes include infantile spasms, juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, temporal lobe epilepsy, childhood absence epilepsy, benign rolandic epilepsy

## Diagnosis
- History and witnessed seizure
- EEG supports diagnosis and often localises the focus
- MRI, SPECT and PET locate lesions and show local blood flow in seizures; useful before resection for intractable epilepsy

## Treatment
- Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs):
  - Usually started after two or more seizures
  - Main drugs: sodium valproate, carbamazepine, ethosuximide, phenytoin, phenobarbital
  - Add-on for resistant seizures: clobazam, clonazepam, nitrazepam
  - Newer: gabapentin, lamotrigine, topiramate, vigabatrin
  - They act on the balance of excitatory and inhibitory neuronal activity
- Ketogenic diet (high fat, low carbohydrate) helps some intractable cases
  - Give glucose-free IV fluids intraoperatively and monitor glucose to avoid hypoglycaemia
- Surgery for intractable epilepsy is increasing: lesionectomy, anterior temporal lobectomy, hemispherectomy, corpus callosotomy; vagus nerve stimulation helps some

## Preoperative assessment
- Main concerns: anaesthetic drug effects on seizures and AED interactions with anaesthetic drug metabolism
- Seizure type
- Frequency and last seizure: shows control and predicts perioperative seizure risk
- AEDs, doses and time of last dose
  - Give usual AEDs preoperatively (e.g. morning of surgery) and restart as soon as possible after
  - A single missed dose (e.g. day surgery): take as soon as possible after surgery
  - If several doses will be missed or oral intake is delayed: rectal carbamazepine, IM phenobarbital, or IV phenytoin, sodium valproate or levetiracetam
  - Regional techniques may speed return to oral intake
- Associated conditions (e.g. epilepsy syndromes)
- Drug levels:
  - Not needed routinely; anaesthetics barely change AED pharmacokinetics
  - Check if poorly controlled, unstable levels, or before major GI surgery (baseline); involve the neurologist
  - In long ICU stays, check phenytoin levels daily

## Anaesthetic drugs and seizure threshold
- IV induction agents: generally proconvulsant at low doses and anticonvulsant at anaesthetic doses
  - Thiopental: strongly anticonvulsant, safe
  - Propofol: once avoided because of excitatory movements at induction and emergence; now widely used, as it is anticonvulsant at anaesthetic doses (used for refractory status) and the movements are easy to tell from seizures
  - Benzodiazepines: potent anticonvulsants, safe
  - Etomidate: linked with postoperative seizures and prolongs ECT seizures; generally avoided
  - Ketamine: often avoided, especially for co-induction (proconvulsant at low dose); anticonvulsant at anaesthetic doses
- Inhalational agents:
  - Nitrous oxide: seizures in animal (cat) studies only; considered safe
  - Most volatiles cause burst suppression and are safe
  - Enflurane: epileptiform EEG and postoperative seizures; avoid
  - Sevoflurane: case reports of seizure-like activity, mainly children, high concentrations with hyperventilation and hypocapnia
- Opioids: all somewhat proconvulsant (used to activate EEG during focus mapping), but most have long safe use
  - Alfentanil: potent EEG activator; avoid or use with caution
  - Avoid pethidine (metabolite norpethidine is proconvulsant) and tramadol (lowers seizure threshold, probably via monoamine reuptake inhibition)
- Local anaesthetics: safe, but dose carefully; they cross the blood-brain barrier and cause seizures at high levels
- Muscle relaxants: no pro- or anticonvulsant effect
  - Enzyme-inducing AEDs cause resistance to aminosteroids (rocuronium, pancuronium, vecuronium): monitor with a nerve stimulator and adjust dose and interval
  - Laudanosine (atracurium metabolite) is epileptogenic, but seizures only seen in animals
  - Suxamethonium: increases EEG activity without seizures; safe except after prolonged status epilepticus (dangerous hyperkalaemia)
- Centrally acting anticholinergics (atropine, hyoscine): central anticholinergic syndrome with agitation and seizures
- Antiemetics: dopamine antagonists cause extrapyramidal effects and dystonia that can mimic seizures; avoid phenothiazines (prochlorperazine), benzamides (metoclopramide) and butyrophenones (droperidol)

## Antiepileptic effects on other drugs
- Many AEDs induce liver enzymes: especially carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, primidone; also topiramate
  - Lower levels of hepatically cleared drugs: immunosuppressants, antibiotics, amiodarone, beta-blockers (propranolol, metoprolol), calcium channel blockers (nifedipine, felodipine, nimodipine, verapamil)
  - Warfarin: monitor INR closely when enzyme inducers are started or stopped
  - Vecuronium may wear off sooner; atracurium may be better
- Valproate inhibits liver enzymes, slowing clearance of many drugs including other AEDs
- Gabapentin, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, tiagabine and vigabatrin do not induce enzymes

## Paediatric considerations
- Learning difficulties and behaviour problems are common; midazolam premedication may help with uncooperative children

## Seizures under anaesthesia
- Rare; more likely with poorly controlled epilepsy or high-risk surgery such as neurosurgery
- Hard to spot, especially if paralysed; signs: rising end-tidal CO2, tachycardia, hypertension, increased tone, dilated pupils, rising oxygen consumption
- EEG helps if available
- Immediate management: deepen anaesthesia, 100% oxygen, correct causes (hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, hypercapnia), consider differentials

## Postoperative seizures
- ABC; check pulse
- Capillary glucose
- Drugs:
  - Benzodiazepines
  - Eclampsia: magnesium 2g
  - If benzodiazepines fail: phenytoin loading dose 18 mg/kg and seek neurology advice
- Protect the patient
- Ask an assistant to video and time the event
- Differentials:
  - True seizure: underlying epilepsy, or secondary (after neurosurgery, eclampsia, metabolic disturbance, alcohol withdrawal)
  - Postoperative shivering
  - Acute dystonia from dopamine antagonists
  - Psychogenic non-epileptic seizure
  - Syncope

## Vagus nerve stimulator: indications and mechanism
- Intractable epilepsy of any seizure type, failed drug treatment, not suitable for resection
  - 48% of patients get a 50% or greater fall in seizures after 18 months
  - For epilepsy from a structural lesion, microsurgical resection is the treatment of choice and works better than VNS
- Thought to act via vagal afferents that modulate cortical excitability (limbic or noradrenergic systems, or brainstem arousal)
- Also FDA approved for refractory depression (mechanism unclear, positive results)

## Vagus nerve stimulator: insertion
- Placed on the left vagus in the neck, as the right vagus supplies the SA node (avoids arrhythmias); not possible after bilateral or left cervical vagotomy
- Pacemaker-like pulse generator in the left chest wall
- Duration 60–90 min; can be day surgery
- Regional anaesthesia possible, but GA most common
- Tracheal tube safer than a supraglottic airway
- Risk of bradycardia and asystole during device diagnostic testing
- Postoperative vocal cord palsy is unusual and usually temporary
- Vagus lies between internal carotid artery and internal jugular vein: vessel damage and bleeding possible at insertion or removal, but rarely reported
- Switched on 2 weeks after insertion, then current increased as tolerated to a target of 1.5–2.25 mA

## Vagus nerve stimulator: anaesthetic issues
- Vagal stimulation risks:
  - Bradycardia
  - Laryngopharyngeal dysfunction: persistent vocal cord adduction, aspiration risk, airway obstruction
- Device malfunction, as with pacemakers: diathermy, lithotripsy, MRI
- Magnet use

## Vagus nerve stimulator: Association of Anaesthetists recommendations
- Elective surgery: safer to formally deactivate beforehand, with a plan to reactivate afterwards
  - If left on, the patient's own magnet must be in theatre for emergency deactivation
  - Higher risk in surgery with excess vagal tone, e.g. laparoscopy
- Emergency surgery: reasonable to proceed with the device active
  - If problems arise, tape the patient's magnet over the generator (usually anterior chest wall)
  - An active device can cause unilateral cord adduction and obstruction with a supraglottic airway; consider intubation
  - If no VNS magnet, any magnet over 50 gauss (e.g. a pacemaker magnet) can be secured over the generator
- Avoid internal jugular and subclavian cannulation on the device side

## Vagus nerve stimulator: regional anaesthesia
- Deactivate for spinal anaesthesia
- Epidurals with active stimulation: safety unclear, potentially hazardous
- Avoid ipsilateral cervical and supra- or infraclavicular blocks
- Nerve stimulator current could enter the device: use the lowest current, with a path not crossing generator or lead
- Contralateral upper limb, abdominal and lower limb blocks are fine

## Vagus nerve stimulator: critical care and MRI
- Continuing VNS in critical illness is case by case with neurology and ICU: weigh seizures or status if off against cardiorespiratory effects if on
- Deactivate in the MRI suite

## Vagus nerve stimulator: diathermy (as for pacemakers)
- Bipolar rather than monopolar
- Diathermy electrodes as far as possible from generator and lead
- Do not place the generator or lead in the current path or the treated area
- Interrogate the device after surgery

## Vagus nerve stimulator: magnet use
- Responds to device-specific magnets (pacemaker magnets work in a pinch)
- Magnet for at least one second triggers stimulation (can be used during a seizure)
- Magnet for longer than one minute usually deactivates the device
- Consult a neurologist about magnet management

## Flashcards
- Q: Which IV induction agent is generally avoided in epilepsy and why?
  - A: Etomidate; linked with postoperative seizures and prolongs ECT seizures.
- Q: Which opioids should be avoided in epilepsy?
  - A: Pethidine and tramadol; alfentanil with caution.
- Q: How do enzyme-inducing AEDs affect aminosteroid relaxants?
  - A: Resistance and shorter action; monitor block, atracurium may be better.
- Q: Phenytoin loading dose for postoperative seizures not stopped by benzodiazepines?
  - A: 18 mg/kg, and seek neurology advice.
- Q: Minimum magnet strength to deactivate a VNS in an emergency?
  - A: Over 50 gauss (e.g. a pacemaker magnet).
