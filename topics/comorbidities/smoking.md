# Smoking

Smokers have irritable airways, poor oxygen delivery from carboxyhaemoglobin and more cardiac risk; stop 4-8 weeks or more before elective surgery.

## Key points
- Stop smoking 4-8 weeks before surgery; offer nicotine replacement
- Irritable airways: instrument the airway when deep
- Secretions
- Associated COPD, pulmonary hypertension and RV strain, IHD

## Cigarette smoke constituents
- Cigarette: tobacco in paper, with a filter and tipping paper; smoke has a gas phase and a particulate phase
- Passive smoking also exposes others to carcinogens and toxins in second-hand smoke
- Carbon monoxide: main gas-phase component
  - Binds Hb with 200 times the affinity of oxygen; raises COHb and reduces tissue oxygen delivery
  - Negatively inotropic and arrhythmogenic
- Nicotine: main particulate component
  - Central stimulant; at higher doses becomes sedative and depressant
  - Stimulates the sympatho-adrenal system
  - Induces liver enzymes
  - Vasoconstriction impairs wound healing
- Tar: nicotine-free remainder of the particulate phase
  - Composition and toxicity vary with tobacco source
  - Irritates and inflames the airway
  - Suppresses immunity
  - Carcinogenic

## Effects by system
- Respiratory
  - Hyper-reactive airway reflexes: more cough, breath holding and laryngospasm
  - Mucus hypersecretion and poor clearance (ciliary damage): more sputum, sputum retention
    - Small airway plugging worsens V/Q mismatch
    - More pneumonia
  - Obstructive disease: early small airway closure in expiration raises closing volume, with atelectasis and V/Q mismatch
    - Reflex bronchoconstriction to inhaled particles
    - About 20% of smokers have COPD
  - Overall more perioperative respiratory complications: hypoxia, respiratory failure, postoperative ventilation
- Haematological
  - Less tissue oxygen delivery from high COHb (normal < 1%, smokers 8-15%)
    - Pulse oximetry reads falsely high with significant COHb
  - Polycythaemia
- Cardiovascular
  - Nicotine causes adrenal adrenaline release: vasoconstriction, higher HR and myocardial oxygen demand, hypertension
  - More coronary artery disease
    - Also an independent risk factor for peripheral vascular disease, thromboembolism and stroke
    - More subarachnoid haemorrhage
  - Myocardial ischaemia from supply-demand mismatch (sympathetic drive, low oxygen delivery, CAD)
  - Arrhythmias from sympathetic drive and COHb
  - COHb is negatively inotropic
  - Overall higher risk of MACE, cardiac arrest and death
- Neurological: addiction and dependence, anxiety
- Gastrointestinal
  - Brief lower oesophageal sphincter relaxation, normal within minutes of stopping; aspiration data conflicting
  - Less PONV in smokers, possibly from faster volatile metabolism via CYP2E1
- Renal: more ADH secretion
- Immunological
  - Poor wound and fracture healing, more anastomotic breakdown
  - More wound infection and sepsis
  - More cancer, especially lung
- Pharmacological
  - Liver enzyme induction makes hepatic drug handling unpredictable
  - Altered response to muscle relaxants
  - Higher postoperative opioid needs; mechanism unclear, not just faster metabolism (pain thresholds and receptor effects may play a part)

## Smoking cessation
- Timing
  - The longer before surgery, the better
  - Ideally 4-8 weeks or more before elective surgery
  - Even a short stop probably helps, given the acute effects of nicotine and CO on the heart
  - Surgery is a "teachable moment": smokers may be more open to quitting for good
- How to help
  - Counselling, behavioural support, telephone quit lines
  - Nicotine replacement (patches, inhalers, nasal sprays, lozenges, gum); well tolerated
  - Sustained-release bupropion, usually started 1–2 weeks before stopping
- Effects of stopping
  - CO and nicotine effects are short lived
  - Fewer pulmonary complications come from better ciliary, macrophage and small airway function and less sputum; these take weeks to months
  - Stop 12-24h before
    - COHb falls, so the dissociation curve shifts right and more oxygen reaches tissues
    - Nicotine falls: less sympatho-adrenal drive and ischaemia; possible nicotine withdrawal
    - Possibly more sputum if stopped <4-8 week before (recent studies dispute this)
  - Stop 4-8 weeks before: all of the above, plus
    - Less sputum (rises for 1-2wks, then falls over 1mth; controversial), better mucociliary clearance, less cough
    - Less small airway narrowing and wheeze
    - Polycythaemia reverses: lower viscosity, hypercoagulability reverses
    - Better immunity: better wound healing, fewer infections
    - Liver enzymes return to normal
    - Fewer perioperative complications overall
  - Stop 6 months before: complication rate may approach a non-smoker's

## Main considerations
- Pulmonary: airway irritability, mucus, obstructive disease, pneumonia
- Cardiovascular: myocardial ischaemia, arrhythmias
- Other: higher postoperative opioid needs

## Preoperative assessment
- Stop smoking ideally >6-8wks before
- Look for active chest infection or infective exacerbation
- Optimise COPD
  - Continue bronchodilators, steroids, mucolytics, antibiotics
  - Preoperative chest physiotherapy and incentive spirometry
- Optimise other comorbidities, especially cardiovascular

## Intraoperative management
- Regional anaesthesia preferred over GA
- Good preoxygenation
- Adequate depth and analgesia before airway handling or surgical stimulus, to limit bronchospasm and laryngospasm
- Humidify and warm inspired gases to prevent mucus plugging
- Suction secretions before extubation

## Postoperative care
- Continuous SpO2; supplemental oxygen as needed
- Good analgesia, especially after abdominal or thoracic surgery, to protect breathing
- Chest physiotherapy and incentive spirometry
- Continue bronchodilators, steroids, mucolytics

## Flashcards
- Q: What COHb levels are normal and typical in smokers?
  - A: Normal < 1%; smokers 8-15%
- Q: What does stopping smoking 12-24h before surgery achieve?
  - A: Lower COHb (curve shifts right, more tissue oxygen) and lower nicotine (less sympathetic drive)
- Q: How long before elective surgery should smokers ideally stop?
  - A: 4-8 weeks or more; by 6 months risk may approach a non-smoker's
- Q: Why can SpO2 mislead in a heavy smoker?
  - A: COHb gives a falsely high saturation reading
- Q: When is bupropion started relative to the quit date?
  - A: 1–2 weeks before stopping
