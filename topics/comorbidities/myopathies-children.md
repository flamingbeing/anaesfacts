# Myopathies in Children

Inherited muscle diseases; key issues are malignant hyperthermia risk (central core), relaxant sensitivity, aspiration and respiratory weakness, and lactic acidosis (mitochondrial).

## Classification
- Inherited disorders of muscle function, classed by molecular and enzyme defect, often with typical histology
- Age of onset and severity vary widely, even within families
- Three groups:
  - Morphologically defined congenital myopathies: central core disease, nemaline myopathy
  - Mitochondrial myopathies: MELAS, MERRF, Kearns-Sayre, Leigh syndrome
  - Metabolic myopathies: muscle glycogenoses (glycogen storage diseases)

## Central core disease
- Autosomal dominant
- Well-defined round cores of myofibrils, only in type 1 fibres
- May be hypotonic at birth with proximal weakness
- Possible skeletal problems: congenital hip dislocation, pes cavus, kyphoscoliosis, joint hypermobility, short neck, mandibular hypoplasia
- May be mild; muscle biopsy may be needed to diagnose
- Non-progressive; normal intelligence
- Mutation on chromosome 19, close to the gene for the ryanodine receptor (controls calcium release in skeletal muscle)

### Anaesthetic management
- Closely linked to malignant hyperthermia: treat as susceptible and avoid triggers
- Assess muscle weakness before surgery

## Nemaline myopathy
- Thread-like or rod-shaped nemaline bodies (mostly alpha-actinin) in muscle fibres; any skeletal muscle may be affected
- Weakness: proximal, facial, bulbar and respiratory muscles
- Autosomal recessive or dominant; mutations on chromosomes 1, 2 and 19; wide range of onset and severity
- Two clinical forms:
  - Typical (commonest): infantile hypotonia and weakness, usually non-progressive
  - Severe: at birth, with severe hypotonia, respiratory failure, arthrogryposis, death within months
  - Less often presents later in childhood or adulthood
- Dysmorphic features common: micrognathia, hypertelorism, high-arched palate
- Cardiac defects possible: septal defects, PDA, aortic regurgitation

### Anaesthetic management
- Prone to recurrent aspiration and chest infections
  - Optimise beforehand: chest physiotherapy, treat infection
  - Assess respiratory function and cough
- Tracheal intubation may be difficult
- No link with malignant hyperthermia
- Abnormal relaxant responses: resistant to suxamethonium, more sensitive to non-depolarising drugs
- May need postoperative ventilation after major surgery
- Respiratory complications are the commonest cause of death

## Mitochondrial myopathies
- Defects of respiratory chain enzymes and the five complexes of oxidative phosphorylation
- ATP production impaired; lactic acidosis common at presentation
- Multisystem disorders

### MELAS (mitochondrial myopathy, encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, stroke)
- Presents with stroke
- Focal or simple partial seizures, dementia, deafness
- Neurological symptoms may be episodic; stroke-like episodes may be transient, possibly worsened by lactic acidosis
- Spongy degeneration of the brain

### MERRF (myoclonic epilepsy with ragged-red fibres)
- Myoclonus, mixed seizures, ataxia, myopathy
- Cerebral and cerebellar atrophy; deafness; raised lactate

### Kearns-Sayre syndrome
- Eye: progressive ophthalmoplegia, retinitis pigmentosa
- Deafness
- Cardiac conduction defects including complete heart block; cardiomyopathy
- Learning disability, short stature, limb weakness; spongy brain degeneration

### Leigh syndrome
- Autosomal recessive subacute necrotising encephalomyelopathy
- Progressive grey matter degeneration and focal brainstem necrosis
- Presents in infancy or early childhood:
  - Developmental delay, hypotonia, seizures
  - Brainstem dysfunction: apnoea, abnormal breathing, sudden infant death
  - Lactic acidosis, short stature, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
- Very sensitive to respiratory depression from volatile and IV anaesthetics

### Anaesthetic management
- May be sensitive to induction agents
- May be sensitive to relaxants: short-acting drugs in reduced doses with neuromuscular monitoring
- Avoid suxamethonium: risk of hyperkalaemia
- Monitor blood glucose; avoid long fasts; give glucose throughout the perioperative period
- Monitor acid-base; treat severe lactic acidosis with bicarbonate if needed
- Monitor for postoperative apnoea in Leigh syndrome

## Flashcards
- Q: Which congenital myopathy is closely linked to malignant hyperthermia?
  - A: Central core disease; treat as MH susceptible
- Q: Relaxant responses in nemaline myopathy?
  - A: Resistant to suxamethonium, more sensitive to non-depolarising drugs; no MH link
- Q: Why avoid suxamethonium in mitochondrial myopathies?
  - A: Risk of hyperkalaemia
- Q: Metabolic precautions in mitochondrial myopathies?
  - A: Avoid long fasts, give glucose throughout, monitor glucose and acid-base
- Q: Specific postoperative risk in Leigh syndrome?
  - A: Apnoea
