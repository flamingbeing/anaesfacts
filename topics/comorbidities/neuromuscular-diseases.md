# Neuromuscular diseases

Mixed group of inherited and acquired disorders of nerve, junction or muscle: avoid suxamethonium, consider TIVA, and plan for respiratory weakness, aspiration and cardiac involvement.

## Classification
- Hereditary
  - Pre-junctional: peripheral neuropathies (Charcot-Marie-Tooth, Friedreich's ataxia)
  - Post-junctional
    - Dystrophies: Duchenne, Becker
    - Myotonias: myotonic dystrophy, myotonia congenita
    - Hyperkalaemic and hypokalaemic periodic paralysis
    - Metabolic and mitochondrial disorders
- Acquired
  - Pre-junctional: motor neurone disease, multiple sclerosis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, peripheral neuropathy (e.g. diabetes)
  - Junctional: myasthenia gravis, Lambert-Eaton syndrome
  - Post-junctional: inflammatory myopathies, critical illness polyneuropathy and myopathy

## Key problems
- Respiratory: respiratory failure, recurrent pneumonia from aspiration and weak cough, spinal deformity
- Airway: aspiration, poor dentition, OSA, possible difficult airway
- Musculoskeletal: contractures leading to pressure sores
- General: malnutrition, DVT
- Cardiovascular: cardiac muscle involvement; autonomic dysfunction needing invasive monitoring and careful BP control
- Muscle complications
  - Avoid suxamethonium: hyperkalaemia and rhabdomyolysis
  - Possible link with MH or anaesthesia-induced rhabdomyolysis with suxamethonium and volatiles: use TIVA
  - Myotonias can cause rhabdomyolysis spontaneously
- Plan for possible postoperative ventilation

## Preoperative assessment
- Establish the exact diagnosis
- History
  - Respiratory symptoms and effort tolerance
  - Home oxygen or NIV
  - Screen for bulbar weakness and OSA
- Examination: baseline neurological deficits and power
- Cardiac
  - Effort tolerance is hard to judge: limited mobility hides cardiac dysfunction
  - ECG, TTE, stress testing
- Investigations
  - SpO2, bicarbonate (HCO3), ABG
  - Pulmonary function tests: at risk if FVC <50%, NIF <40, MEP <60; 20/30/40 rule (not possible in young children)
  - CXR for infection and scoliosis
  - Sleep study for OSA
  - EMG
  - Electrolytes (correct abnormalities); baseline CK
  - Lactate for metabolic myopathies
- Involve neurologist, respiratory physician, paediatrician
- Counsel patient and family fully about risks
- Premedication
  - Helps some, harms others
  - Short-acting benzodiazepine useful where crying or fear could trigger catecholamine-driven spasms
  - Drugs that depress breathing or reduce muscle tone: reduced doses with continuous monitoring

## Intraoperative management
- Monitoring
  - Invasive lines if cardiac or autonomic involvement
  - Temperature monitoring and control is vital
    - Hypothermia can worsen myotonia, increase sensitivity to non-depolarising relaxants, aggravate rhabdomyolysis, and cause bleeding and arrhythmias
    - Hyperthermia can come from myotonic muscle activity, iatrogenic causes or MH
    - Muscular dystrophies and myotonias: keep MH in mind; unexplained tachycardia with rising ETCO2 needs prompt aggressive treatment
- Technique
  - Regional anaesthesia preferred (also as part of multimodal analgesia), particularly with major respiratory or cardiac disease
    - Do a full neurological examination first
    - Probably avoid with rapidly progressing deficits, so block effects are not confused with progression
    - Caution with spinal: autonomic dysfunction and respiratory weakness
- Airway
  - Bulbar weakness and aspiration risk, yet relaxants may need to be avoided: consider remifentanil
  - Possible difficult airway: high-arched palate and TMJ spasm in myotonic dystrophy
  - RSI with rocuronium (not suxamethonium)
- Maintenance
  - Consider TIVA
    - Volatiles were once contraindicated because of MH; only a few neuromuscular disorders have a proven MH link
  - Avoid both depolarising and non-depolarising relaxants where possible
- Suxamethonium
  - Contraindicated: hyperkalaemia and rhabdomyolysis
    - Denervated or long-immobile muscle develops extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors spread across the membrane
    - Fetal (gamma subunit) receptors may also appear
    - Suxamethonium activates both, causing depolarisation and possibly massive potassium release
    - In myotonias, fasciculations can trigger masseter (TMJ) spasm, preventing intubation and ventilation
  - Exception: myasthenia gravis, where it can be used; dose may need to be up to twice normal due to relative resistance
- Non-depolarising relaxants
  - Many patients are sensitive: weakness, failure to wean, sputum retention, dysphagia
  - If essential: reduced dose (10–20% of the usual dose) with neuromuscular monitoring
  - Mivacurium and atracurium preferred because of how they are broken down
- Anticholinesterases may also cause hyperkalaemia in muscular dystrophies, like suxamethonium
- Protect respiratory function
  - Short-acting drugs
  - Multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia
  - Full reversal with neuromuscular monitoring
  - Extubate fully awake, sitting up; consider NIV after
- Avoid hypothermia
- Careful padding and positioning

## Postoperative care
- Admit for monitoring; not suitable for day surgery
- Consider HDU or ICU
- Good analgesia and physiotherapy
- May need postoperative ventilation or NIV, but extubate early to avoid further respiratory muscle weakening

## Specific conditions in brief
- Myasthenia gravis: no autonomic involvement; suxamethonium can be used
- Guillain-Barré syndrome: autonomic involvement
- Myopathies (Duchenne, myotonic dystrophy): the above plus
  - Cardiomyopathy and conduction problems: ECG and echo
  - Mitral valve prolapse in both
  - Endocrine problems in myotonic dystrophy: adrenal, thyroid, diabetes
  - Autonomic dysfunction: careful induction, arterial line
  - Developmental delay in DMD; intellectual disability in myotonic dystrophy
- Malignant hyperthermia association: central core disease, multiminicore disease, King-Denborough syndrome
- Other myopathies: avoid suxamethonium for hyperkalaemia risk (except MG); also avoid volatiles because of anaesthesia-related rhabdomyolysis (e.g. DMD)

## Muscular dystrophies
- Congenital disorders with progressive weakness of affected muscle groups
  - X-linked: Duchenne, Becker
  - Autosomal recessive: limb-girdle, childhood, congenital
  - Autosomal dominant: facioscapulohumeral, oculopharyngeal

## Duchenne muscular dystrophy
- Most common and most severe muscular dystrophy
- X-linked (males)
- Presents at 2-5 years with weakness
- Wheelchair-bound by 12 years
- Death by 25 years (heart failure or pneumonia)
- Complications
  - Cardiovascular: myocardial degeneration causing heart failure; mitral valve prolapse
  - Respiratory: respiratory muscle weakness, restrictive pattern, weak cough, eventual respiratory failure and infection
  - Haematological: vascular smooth muscle dysfunction increases bleeding
  - Musculoskeletal: progressive severe kyphoscoliosis
- Preoperative
  - CK tracks disease: high early, falls below normal late
  - CXR, spirometry
  - Echo mandatory if wheelchair-bound
  - Liaise with paediatrician
  - Premedication: consider antisialagogue and antacid or prokinetic
- Intraoperative
  - Aspiration risk
  - TIVA to avoid volatile-induced rhabdomyolysis
  - Avoid suxamethonium
  - Non-depolarising relaxants with caution: reduced dose and nerve stimulator
- Postoperative: may need to stay intubated

## Myotonic dystrophy (dystrophia myotonica)
- Also called myotonia atrophica
- Myotonia: sustained skeletal muscle contraction after stimulation
- Progressive atrophy of skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle, reducing cardiorespiratory function
- History
  - Autosomal dominant
  - Presents at 20-30yrs
  - Frontal balding, cataracts, weakness, bulbar palsy, reduced respiratory reserve
  - Cognitive decline after the 2nd decade
  - Death in the 5th - 6th decade
  - Endocrine dysfunction: diabetes, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, gonadal atrophy
- Examination
  - Wasting of facial, sternomastoid and peripheral muscles
  - Respiratory failure from weakness and bulbar signs
  - Signs of cardiomyopathy
  - Mitral valve prolapse (20%)
- Investigations
  - Electrolytes and glucose (endocrine problems)
  - ECG: conduction system degeneration causing arrhythmias and AV block
  - CXR, ABG, spirometry
  - Echo: structural defects, mitral valve prolapse in ~20%
- Management
  - Supportive
  - Anti-myotonic drugs (procainamide, phenytoin, quinine, mexiletine): continue perioperatively
  - Pregnancy may worsen disease; caesarean may be needed for poor uterine contraction
- Intraoperative
  - Aspiration risk (reflux, delayed gastric emptying): antacids, RSI with ETT
  - Avoid suxamethonium: prolonged contraction and potassium release
  - Non-depolarising relaxants with caution
    - Always use a nerve stimulator (though stimulation itself can cause contraction or tetany)
    - Use short-acting agents so neostigmine can be avoided (it can prolong contraction)
    - Best avoided, or use rocuronium with sugammadex
  - Invasive monitoring: marked risk of cardiovascular depression
  - Keep warm; avoid shivering (provokes myotonia)
- Myotonia
  - Triggers
    - Drugs: suxamethonium, anticholinesterases (use sugammadex), opioids
    - Physical: shivering, temperature change, acidosis, electrocautery, nerve stimulation
  - Not relieved by regional block or neuromuscular blockers
  - Treat with sodium channel blockers (local anaesthetics, antiarrhythmics), phenytoin, quinidine
- Postoperative: HDU, respiratory monitoring, physiotherapy, analgesia (ideally regional)

## Motor neurone disease (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis)
- Degeneration of upper and lower motor neurones in the spinal cord
- Cognition usually normal until terminal respiratory failure, raising ethical issues about long-term ventilation
- History: weakness starting in proximal hand muscles, progressing to axial and bulbar weakness
- Examination: wasting, fasciculation, weakness, respiratory failure, autonomic dysfunction
- Investigations: EMG
- Management: symptomatic
- Intraoperative
  - Bulbar dysfunction: ETT (many already have a tracheostomy)
  - IPPV
  - Autonomic dysfunction: hypotension at induction, start of IPPV and position changes; monitor closely
  - Wide-bore IV access, fluids, vasopressors ready
  - Avoid suxamethonium
  - Non-depolarising relaxants at lower doses
- Postoperative: may need respiratory support

## Flashcards
- Q: Which PFT values flag high respiratory risk in neuromuscular disease?
  - A: FVC <50%, NIF <40, MEP <60 (20/30/40 rule).
- Q: If a non-depolarising relaxant is essential in neuromuscular disease, what dose is used?
  - A: 10–20% of the usual dose, with neuromuscular monitoring.
- Q: In which neuromuscular disease can suxamethonium be used, and how is the dose changed?
  - A: Myasthenia gravis; dose may need up to twice normal due to resistance.
- Q: Which myopathies are associated with MH?
  - A: Central core disease, multiminicore disease and King-Denborough syndrome.
- Q: How is a myotonic contracture treated?
  - A: Sodium channel blockers (local anaesthetics, antiarrhythmics), phenytoin, quinidine; regional block and relaxants do not relieve it.
