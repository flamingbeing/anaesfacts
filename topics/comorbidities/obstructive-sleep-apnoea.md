# Obstructive Sleep Apnoea

Screen with STOP-BANG and check AHI and bicarbonate; expect a difficult airway, spare opioids, extubate awake sitting, and monitor SpO2 after surgery.

## Summary
- Preoperative: STOP-BANG, then AHI and bicarbonate; cardiovascular risk (hypertension, cor pulmonale); patient brings own CPAP; children: avoid premedication
- Technique: regional preferred; children: IV induction preferred
- Monitoring: SpO2 and EtCO2 during sedation
- Induction: plan as for difficult mask ventilation and intubation
- Maintenance: multimodal opioid-sparing; short-acting anaesthetics (desflurane) and opioids (remifentanil, fentanyl)
- Emergence: extubate fully awake, sitting, fully reversed, onto CPAP
- Complications: difficult airway, airway obstruction, postoperative respiratory dysfunction
- Postoperative: sitting, with or without CPAP; same-day admission; 1hr in recovery; high dependency or SpO2 monitoring on the ward
- Equipment: TCI pumps for remifentanil, videolaryngoscope, nerve stimulator, ultrasound and block trolley; book a high dependency bed
- AHI cut-offs (mild/moderate/severe): adults 5/15/30, children 1/5/10

## Pathophysiology
- Airway collapses when the negative inspiratory pressure exceeds the distending pressure of the pharyngeal muscles
  - In obesity, fat in the pharyngeal wall narrows the airway further, so more muscle tone is needed to keep it open
  - CPAP keeps upper airway pressure positive and acts as a pneumatic splint
- Obstruction can occur in any sleep stage but is common in REM sleep
- Complete collapse causes apnoea; partial collapse causes snoring and hypopnoea
- Breathing restarts with arousal, triggered by increasing effort, hypoxaemia and hypercarbia
- Each arousal gives a sympathetic surge, leading to
  - Systemic hypertension, then LVH
  - Pulmonary hypertension, then RVH
  - Arrhythmias
- Chronic hypoxaemia causes polycythaemia
- High negative intrathoracic pressure promotes reflux from stomach to oesophagus

## Causes
- Strongly linked with obesity
- Adenotonsillar hypertrophy
- Craniofacial abnormalities

## Types (overlap is common)
- Obstructive: pharyngeal wall collapse (85%)
  - Normal inspiratory balance: negative pressure from diaphragm and intercostals against tone in the oropharyngeal dilator and abductor muscles
  - Upset by more fat in the pharyngeal wall (more compliant)
  - And by airway shape: open axis becomes anteroposterior rather than lateral, so genioglossus tone is ineffective in inspiration
  - Polysomnography: intermittent desaturation with full recovery from the nadir
- Central, also called obesity hypoventilation syndrome (5%)
  - Severe disease with marked end-organ damage
  - Blunted respiratory centres, possibly leptin insensitivity
  - Diurnal variation in ventilation
  - PaCO2 > 45 (type II respiratory failure)
  - Reduced CO2 sensitivity; hypoventilation
  - Higher O2 consumption and CO2 production from metabolically active fat and the work of carrying weight and breathing
  - BiPAP works despite reduced drive
  - Polysomnography: prolonged desaturation without recovery
- Mixed

## Screening: STOP-BANG
- Snoring: loud (heard through a closed door, or bed partner elbows them)
- Tired: daytime sleepiness
- Observed apnoea
- Pressure: hypertension
- BMI >35
- Age > 50 years
- Neck circumference >40cm
- Gender: male
- Score
  - 0-2: low risk
  - 3-4: intermediate risk; high sensitivity, low specificity, good for screening
  - 5 or more: high risk; sensitive and specific
- Epworth Sleepiness Scale: subjective daytime sleepiness

## Associated conditions
- Airway: more likely to be difficult
- Cardiovascular: hypertension, arrhythmias, pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, polycythaemia
- Neurocognitive (children): lower IQ, poorer memory and learning, bedwetting
- Endocrine: impaired glucose tolerance, dyslipidaemia, raised ACTH and cortisol, testicular and ovarian dysfunction, hypothyroidism
- Reflux disease
- Children: linked with adenotonsillar hypertrophy, but severity does not track tonsil size

## Treatment
- Lifestyle: weight loss, stop smoking and alcohol, more exercise
- CPAP
- Uvulopalatopharyngoplasty
- Mandibular advancement devices
- New diagnosis: limited evidence that 1-3 months of CPAP before surgery changes outcome

## Preoperative assessment
- History from patient and family: snoring, apnoeas, frequent arousals (vocalising, shifting, limb movement), morning headache, daytime sleepiness (e.g. STOP-BANG)
- Examination: airway, nasopharynx, neck circumference, tonsil size, tongue volume
- If OSA is suspected, anaesthetist and surgeon decide together whether to
  - (1) manage on clinical criteria alone, or
  - (2) get a sleep study and start treatment before surgery
  - Clinical criteria should favour sensitivity over specificity, so some patients will be over-treated
- Assess
  - Severity: clinical (including STOP-BANG) and sleep study results
  - Complications: previous difficult airway, hypertension or other cardiovascular disease
  - Surgery: type, risk of postoperative pulmonary dysfunction, expected analgesic needs
- Inform patient, family and surgeon of the perioperative implications

## Investigations
- FBC for polycythaemia
- SpO2: if hypoxic at rest, do an ABG
- ECG: if right heart strain, echo to look for RVH
- Baseline ABG: normal bicarbonate makes OHS unlikely
  - With heart failure or PaCO2 >50, postpone elective surgery until after 3 months (3/12) of CPAP

## Preoperative preparation
- Consider starting CPAP, especially in severe OSA; NIPPV if CPAP is not enough
- Consider mandibular advancement or oral devices and weight loss where feasible
- After corrective airway surgery (e.g. UPPP, mandibular advancement), assume ongoing risk unless a sleep study is normal and symptoms have not returned
- Manage the airway according to difficult airway guidelines
- Avoid sedative premedication

## Intraoperative management
- Technique
  - Superficial surgery: local anaesthesia or peripheral nerve block, with or without moderate sedation
  - Peripheral surgery: regional preferred
  - GA with a secured airway is better than deep sedation without one, especially if surgery may compromise the airway
- Sedation
  - Monitor ventilation continuously with capnography (obstruction may go unnoticed)
  - Consider CPAP or the patient's oral device during sedation if they use one
- GA: short-acting agents, opioid-sparing
- Emergence
  - Extubate awake unless contraindicated
  - Confirm full reversal of neuromuscular block first
  - Extubate and recover lateral, sitting or otherwise non-supine

## Postoperative care
- Risk factors for respiratory depression: OSA severity, systemic opioids, sedatives, site and size of surgery, REM rebound apnoea on the third or fourth postoperative night
- Four areas to manage: (1) analgesia, (2) oxygenation, (3) positioning, (4) monitoring
- Analgesia
  - Reduce systemic opioids
  - Multimodal: paracetamol, NSAIDs, other measures (ice, TENS)
  - PCA: avoid background infusions, or use with great caution
  - Regional techniques to reduce or avoid systemic opioids
  - Neuraxial: weigh better analgesia with less systemic opioid against respiratory depression from rostral spread; consider local anaesthetic alone
- Avoid other sedatives (benzodiazepines, barbiturates): they increase respiratory depression and obstruction
- Oxygenation
  - Continuous oxygen until baseline SpO2 is held on room air
  - Continue CPAP or NIPPV if used before, unless surgery forbids; own equipment improves compliance
  - Keep non-supine throughout recovery if possible
- Monitoring in recovery
  - Do not discharge to an unmonitored setting (home or unmonitored ward bed) until no longer at risk of respiratory depression
  - Keep an extra half hour (total 1hr) to watch for recurrent events: desaturation, bradypnoea or apnoea, pain-sedation mismatch
  - If events occur, watch another 30mins, then consider high dependency
  - Let the patient sleep: judge room-air oxygenation unstimulated, ideally asleep, not while prompting deep breaths
- Disposition
  - Continuous pulse oximetry after leaving recovery for inpatients at increased risk
  - By high dependency, ward telemetry, or a trained observer in the room
  - Continue while risk persists, typically at least overnight
  - Frequent or severe obstruction or hypoxaemia: consider nasal CPAP or NIPPV

## OSA in children
- Different AHI cut-offs (see summary)
- Preoperative: avoid premedication and inhalational induction
- Intraoperative: possible difficult airway, opioid-sparing, extubate awake
- Postoperative: SpO2 monitoring, paediatric high dependency care

## Sleep study (polysomnography)
- Records ECG, EEG, eye movements, EMG, snoring volume, oronasal airflow and SpO2
- Uses: grades OSA severity to guide treatment (e.g. CPAP) and preoperative risk stratification

### Respiratory events
- Apnoea: no airflow for >10s despite effort, with a fall in oxygenation
- Hypopnoea: reduced flow with desaturation; definitions vary, e.g. airflow down by 30% or >4% desaturation
- Apnoea-hypopnoea index (AHI): apnoeas plus hypopnoeas per hour of sleep
  - Normal: AHI <5
  - Mild: AHI 5 to <15
  - Moderate: AHI 15 to <30
  - Severe: AHI 30 or more
  - Children: AHI above 1 is abnormal; 2 or more often prompts treatment referral
- Respiratory disturbance index (RDI)
  - Adds respiratory effort-related arousals (RERAs): arousals with a flow change short of apnoea or hypopnoea criteria
  - RDI can far exceed AHI in young thin patients, who desaturate by 4% less readily

### Hypoxaemia
- Report nadir and mean SpO2 during sleep
- Hypoxaemic burden: percentage of sleep time below a set SpO2, e.g. 90 percent
- Oxygen desaturation index (ODI): drops per hour of sleep beyond a set fall from baseline, usually 4 percent (ODI 4 percent); some labs also report 3 percent (ODI 3 percent)

### EEG data
- Sleep efficiency normally 80%
- Sleep latency < 30 min; REM latency 60-120 min
- Stages: about 5% N1, 50% N2, 20% N3 (slow wave sleep), 20-25% REM
- Arousal index normally < 10-25 (varies widely with age)
  - An arousal longer than 15 seconds counts as an awakening; patients notice awakenings but usually not arousals
- Norms depend on age: with age, less REM and slow wave sleep, more arousals and wake after sleep onset, lower efficiency
- EEG abnormalities: epileptiform activity, alpha intrusion

## Uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP)
- South-facing RAE tube
- Fairly short: a couple of hours

## Flashcards
- Q: AHI cut-offs for mild, moderate and severe OSA in adults and children?
  - A: Adults 5/15/30; children 1/5/10.
- Q: STOP-BANG thresholds for BMI, age and neck circumference?
  - A: BMI >35, age > 50, neck >40cm; score 5 or more is high risk.
- Q: When should elective surgery be postponed in OSA?
  - A: Heart failure or PaCO2 >50: after 3 months of CPAP.
- Q: How long to observe an OSA patient in recovery?
  - A: Total 1hr; if events, another 30mins, then consider high dependency.
- Q: When is REM rebound apnoea most likely after surgery?
  - A: Third or fourth postoperative night.
