# Recent Upper Respiratory Tract Infection

Active or recent URTI raises the risk of airway complications, especially in children; weigh patient, surgical, anaesthetic and social factors before proceeding or postponing.

## Risk assessment
- Patient: infants, airway, asthma
- Surgical factors
- Anaesthetic: ETT
- Social factors
- Aim: avoid laryngospasm and bronchospasm

## Condition (children)
- A common dilemma: the child with an active or recent cold
- Usually viral: rhinovirus, adenovirus, parainfluenza, RSV in infants, influenza (winter epidemics)
  - Occasional bacterial superinfection
- Active URTI: at least two of:
  - Sore throat
  - Sneezing
  - Rhinorrhoea
  - Nasal congestion
  - Malaise
  - Cough
  - Fever or laryngitis
  - Parent confirms it
- Recent URTI: within 2 weeks (up to 4 weeks)
  - Risk is highest during acute infection but stays raised for 2-6 weeks afterwards
- Nasopharyngeal swabs or aspirates can confirm it but are rarely needed, costly and impractical
- Consider CXR and FBC if examination suggests lower respiratory tract involvement

## Pathophysiology
- Airway hyper-reactivity after viral URTI: chemical mediators and neural reflexes
- Inflammatory mediators released at sites of viral damage (bradykinin, prostaglandins, histamine, interleukins) cause bronchoconstriction
- Viral neuraminidase may inhibit M2 muscarinic receptors, so more acetylcholine is released, leading to bronchoconstriction

## Anaesthetic concerns
- Distinguish from allergic rhinitis: chronic, may be seasonal, child well, clear history from parents
- Airway and respiratory complications rise 5-7 times even in a systemically well child
  - May not apply to very short facemask cases, e.g. grommets
  - Rises to about 10-fold with an ETT
  - More laryngospasm, bronchospasm, coughing, breath-holding
  - More secretions, atelectasis, pneumonia
  - Usually benign, but can cause profound hypoxia, bradycardia and need for emergency intubation
- Infants and toddlers are already prone to respiratory complications (anatomy and physiology)
- Risk is higher still with lower respiratory tract infection
- Viraemia may cause carditis and arrhythmias under anaesthesia

## Reasons not to cancel on symptoms alone
- Delay may cause complications of the underlying surgical problem
- A preschool child may have up to eight colds a year; some are never symptom free
  - Often (especially ENT) the surgical problem partly causes the symptoms, so the child may not be symptom free until after surgery
- Socioeconomic
  - Families make special arrangements; cancellation wastes time and money
  - Inefficient use of hospital resources

## Decision to proceed or cancel
- Shared decision with the surgeon and fully informed parents; document the discussion
- Patient factors
  - History of allergic rhinitis
  - Severe URTI or lower RTI:
    - Respiratory: purulent secretions, productive cough, crackles or wheeze, respiratory distress or failure (tachypnoea, cyanosis, desaturation)
    - Systemic: fever above 38°C, lethargy (less active), poor appetite or feeding, toxic appearance
    - Factors favouring going ahead: mild URTI, no lower tract or systemic features, symptoms resolving or resolved
    - Also ask about sick contacts and their severity, e.g. influenza A
  - Passive smoking (parents smoke)
  - Significant comorbidities:
    - Age under 1 year (infants carry higher risk than older children)
    - Prematurity, chronic lung disease
    - Asthma
    - OSA, obesity
    - Congenital heart disease
    - Neuromuscular disorders
    - Immunocompromise
- Surgical factors
  - Urgent surgery: no choice; discuss urgency with the surgeon
  - Major, prolonged surgery (versus brief, minor)
  - Airway or ENT, eye, or thoracic surgery (versus non-airway surgery)
- Anaesthetic factors
  - Tracheal intubation (versus LMA), especially under 5 years
- Social factors
  - Frequent URTI: hard to find a symptom-free gap with 6-8 episodes per year
  - Several previous cancellations: real emotional and financial strain
  - Compelling social reasons: parents' time off work, school holidays

## If surgery is postponed
- Wait more than 4 weeks from the first day of symptoms, as airway hyper-reactivity can take that long to settle
- Alternative: more than 2 weeks from the last day of symptoms

## If surgery goes ahead
- Consider preoperative salbutamol
- Consider atropine to reduce vagal reflex bronchoconstriction
- Airway
  - Avoid an ETT; minimise handling of a sensitive airway
    - LMA
    - Regional technique where possible (harder in children)
  - IV induction with propofol if possible (blunts laryngeal reflexes); avoid thiopentone and inhalational induction
  - Ensure adequate depth of anaesthesia and analgesia before airway instrumentation
    - Consider nebulised or topical local anaesthetic to the cords to reduce airway sensitivity
- Maintenance
  - Keep anaesthesia and analgesia adequate
  - Avoid desflurane
  - Ideally avoid histamine-releasing drugs, e.g. morphine, atracurium
- Extubation
  - Suction secretions well, but only while deep and before extubation
  - Extubate deep or fully awake, never in stage 2
    - Awake: patient can clear secretions and protect the airway
    - Deep: avoids reflex airway constriction
- Postoperative
  - Monitor for complications
  - Supplementary oxygen may be needed
  - Chest physiotherapy if needed
  - Adequate analgesia
  - Consider keeping well hydrated so secretions stay thin and easy to clear
- Management is mainly respiratory risk reduction plus reducing airway irritability (laryngospasm prevention, asthma drugs, secretion management as for smokers)

## Complications to manage
- Laryngospasm
- Wheeze and bronchospasm

## Adults
- Acute cough: similar approach to children
- Chronic cough: look for a cause and consider respiratory referral
  - COPD or chronic bronchitis
  - Bronchiectasis
  - Lung cancer

## Flashcards
- Q: How much does a URTI raise the risk of airway complications in children?
  - A: 5-7 times; about 10-fold if an ETT is used
- Q: How long should elective surgery be postponed after a URTI?
  - A: More than 4 weeks from the first day of symptoms, or more than 2 weeks from the last day
- Q: Which systemic features favour cancelling?
  - A: Fever above 38°C, lethargy, poor feeding, toxic appearance
- Q: Preferred airway and induction if proceeding?
  - A: Avoid ETT (use LMA); IV propofol induction; avoid thiopentone, inhalational induction and desflurane
- Q: When should the airway be removed?
  - A: Deep or fully awake, never in stage 2
