# Rib Fractures and Flail Chest

Pain limits breathing and coughing, so good multimodal and regional analgesia is the key to preventing lung complications.

## Mechanism
- In the elderly, minor trauma can fracture ribs

## Complications
- **Pneumothorax** from lung puncture
- **Haemothorax**
- **Flail chest**

## Diagnosis
- Chest X-ray, including oblique views
- **CT** is the most sensitive test

## Course and surgical management
- Ribs usually become fairly stable in 10 days to two weeks
- Solid callus healing by about six weeks
- Surgical fixation, especially for **flail chest with respiratory compromise**

## Problems in ICU
- Pain:
  - Shallow breathing leads to **low tidal volumes, hypoventilation and atelectasis**
  - **Poor cough and secretion clearance, pneumonia**
- **Underlying lung contusion**:
  - Almost always present when the injury is bad enough to break ribs, especially with a flail segment
  - Lung becomes oedematous, with bleeding and necrosis
  - Stiff, non-ventilated lung causes shunt and a fall in PaO2
- **Flail chest**:
  - Negative intrapleural pressure sucks the flail segment in while the rest of the chest wall moves out (paradoxical movement)
  - Underlying lung fails to expand, so tidal volume falls
  - Can cause severe **respiratory distress**

## Analgesia
- Effective **multimodal analgesia** is the cornerstone; it reduces **pulmonary complications**
- Drugs: paracetamol, NSAIDs, IV opioids
- Regional techniques (for moderate to severe pain):
  - **Thoracic epidural**: traditionally the best evidence
  - **Paravertebral, erector spinae plane, intercostal and serratus anterior** blocks or catheters: may work as well as epidural in suitable patients, with fewer and milder complications
  - Problems: patient may be on spinal precautions, or unable to turn lateral for the block
- Surgical rib fixation may be needed to control pain
- Chronic pain may develop

## Ventilatory management
- **Supplemental oxygen**
- **Invasive ventilation** with **lung protective ventilation** (as for pulmonary contusion)
  - **Insert a chest drain for pneumothorax** before starting positive pressure ventilation
  - Avoid fluid overload
- Chest physiotherapy, incentive spirometry, early mobilisation
- Surgical fixation may be needed to restore breathing mechanics

## Flail chest management
- Supplemental oxygen, invasive ventilation
- Surgical fixation to control pain and restore breathing mechanics

## Flashcards
- Q: What is the most sensitive imaging for rib fractures?
  - A: CT
- Q: How long until fractured ribs become fairly stable, and until firm callus?
  - A: About 10 days to two weeks; callus by about six weeks
- Q: What must be done before ventilating a patient with rib fractures and a pneumothorax?
  - A: Insert a chest drain
- Q: Which regional technique has traditionally had the best evidence for rib fractures?
  - A: Thoracic epidural analgesia
