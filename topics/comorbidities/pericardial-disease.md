# Pericardial Disease

Acute pericarditis delays elective surgery; constrictive pericarditis causes severe diastolic dysfunction and needs normal heart rate, a full ventricle and low intrathoracic pressure.

## Acute pericarditis

### History
- Chest pain, worse on leaning forward
- Often follows a viral infection
- May be complicated by myocarditis

### Examination
- Pericardial rub

### Investigations
- ECG: widespread ST elevation not matching a coronary territory; arrhythmias

### Management
- Postpone elective surgery for 6 weeks
- Supportive care
- Steroids: uncertain role

## Constrictive pericarditis

### Causes and course
- After infection, or secondary to autoimmune disease (e.g. SLE)
- Responds well to pericardectomy, but this can be very high risk depending on how chronic the constriction is
  - Chronic constriction leads to severe diastolic dysfunction

### History
- Fatigue
- Reduced exercise tolerance
- Shortness of breath

### Examination
- Pulsus paradoxus: exaggerated inspiratory fall in systolic BP (more than 10 mmHg)

### Investigations
- Echo: systolic function preserved, diastolic function markedly reduced

### Intraoperative management
- Avoid both bradycardia and tachycardia; keep HR normal at 60-80
- Keep well filled
- Keep intrathoracic pressure low; maintain spontaneous ventilation if possible
- Inotrope of choice: milrinone (lowers SVR and increases contractility)
- Neuraxial or regional techniques ideal

## Flashcards
- Q: How long should elective surgery be postponed after acute pericarditis?
  - A: 6 weeks
- Q: Typical ECG of acute pericarditis?
  - A: Widespread ST elevation not fitting a coronary territory
- Q: Heart rate target in constrictive pericarditis?
  - A: Normal, 60-80; avoid both bradycardia and tachycardia
- Q: Preferred inotrope in constrictive pericarditis and why?
  - A: Milrinone, as it lowers SVR and increases contractility
- Q: Definition of pulsus paradoxus?
  - A: Inspiratory fall in systolic BP of more than 10 mmHg
