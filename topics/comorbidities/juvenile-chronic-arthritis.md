# Juvenile Chronic Arthritis

Childhood-onset chronic arthritis of unknown cause; anaesthetic concerns are restricted mouth opening, cervical spine instability and drug side effects.

## Definition and epidemiology
- Arthritis starting before age 16 years, unknown cause, lasting at least 3 months
- A mixed group of disorders; one of the commonest childhood connective tissue diseases
- Two onset peaks: 1-3 years and 8-12 years
- Usually rheumatoid factor (RhF) negative, unlike adult rheumatoid arthritis; probably a different disease process
- Classified by joints involved and whether systemic features are present at onset

## Pauciarticular onset
- Up to 50% of children; 1-4 joints in the first 6 months
- Persistent monoarthritis is usually the knee
- Hands, feet, cervical spine and TMJ rarely involved
- Subgroup 1: mainly girls under 4 years
  - Seldom progresses to severe polyarthritis (20%)
  - 30% develop iridocyclitis, a serious problem
- Subgroup 2: mainly boys over 9 years
  - Large lower-limb joints
  - Many go on to ankylosing spondylitis
  - 10% may get acute iridocyclitis

## Polyarticular onset
- 40% have 5 or more joints at presentation
- Symmetrical knees, wrists, ankles and small joints of hands and feet
- Cervical spine involved in up to 70%: pain, reduced movement or torticollis
- Bilateral TMJ disease limits mouth opening and jaw growth, causing micrognathia
- Systemic features usually mild: fever, anaemia, lymphadenopathy, rash
- Seropositive subgroup (smaller): girls over 8 years; more destructive disease of hands, feet and hips; rheumatoid nodules; poorer response to treatment
- Seronegative subgroup: asymmetrical small and large joints, especially TMJ and cervical spine; seldom destructive

## Systemic onset
- Fever, rash and arthritis at any childhood age; equal sex ratio
- Daily intermittent fever of 39.5-40.5°C; child very unwell during spikes
- Salmon-pink maculopapular rash, not itchy, variable, most visible with fever
- Arthritis may follow weeks or months later
- Clinical diagnosis after excluding infection, malignancy and other connective tissue disease
- May have hepatosplenomegaly and generalised lymphadenopathy; less often pericarditis, pleuritis, pericardial effusion
- Bloods: anaemia of chronic disease, thrombocytosis, raised ESR and WCC, RhF negative

## Treatment
- Physiotherapy plus drugs to control symptoms and protect joints
- NSAIDs first line
- Second line (systemic and polyarticular onset): methotrexate, ciclosporin, gold, penicillamine
- Steroids limited by long-term harm; used for acute febrile episodes, carditis and Coombs-positive haemolytic anaemia (oral, or pulsed IV methylprednisolone)

## Anaesthetic management
- Careful positioning with padding of pressure points
- Airway
  - TMJ arthritis: reduced mouth opening and small mandible; laryngoscopy may be difficult; fibreoptic intubation may be needed
  - Cricoarytenoid involvement: hoarseness, stridor, difficulty swallowing
- Cervical spine
  - Before neck manipulation, examine neurologically and radiologically for instability, atlantoaxial subluxation and ankylosis
- Drug side effects
  - Anaemia; platelet dysfunction (NSAIDs)
  - Leucopenia, nephritis, hepatitis (gold)
  - Cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis (methotrexate)
  - Cushingoid effects (steroids)
- Rare pleuritis or pericarditis: ECG, CXR and echocardiography before surgery

## Flashcards
- Q: How is juvenile chronic arthritis defined?
  - A: Arthritis starting before age 16 years, of unknown cause, lasting at least 3 months.
- Q: How often is the cervical spine involved in polyarticular onset JCA?
  - A: Up to 70% of cases.
- Q: Why may laryngoscopy be difficult in JCA?
  - A: TMJ arthritis limits mouth opening and causes mandibular hypoplasia (micrognathia); cricoarytenoid involvement may also occur.
- Q: Which serious eye complication affects 30% of young girls with pauciarticular JCA?
  - A: Iridocyclitis.
