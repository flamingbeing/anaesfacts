# Tuberculosis

Check for active disease and drug side effects; delay elective surgery until non-infectious; prevent airborne spread.

## Overview
- Check severity
- Airborne precautions apply
- Types of surgery
  - Diagnostic: lymph node biopsy, bronchoscopy
  - Therapeutic: complications of TB (intestinal obstruction, hydrocephalus, splenic abscess) or unrelated surgery

## Preoperative assessment
- TB status: check for active disease (patient may be acutely unwell)
  - Pulmonary
    - Cough, haemoptysis (can be catastrophic)
    - CXR: typical apical cavitating lesion; may be atypical in HIV
    - Mediastinal nodes compressing bronchi (wheeze)
    - Cavitation, consolidation or effusion reducing lung function, with respiratory distress
  - Extrapulmonary: almost any site; often bone, abdomen, meninges, pericardium, pleura, lymph nodes
  - Constitutional: fever, sweats, weight loss
- Diagnosis: TB culture (2-12 weeks), AFB smear, nucleic acid amplification test
- Treatment side effects
  - Rifampicin: hepatitis, hypersensitivity
  - Isoniazid: hepatitis, peripheral neuropathy, drug-induced lupus
  - Ethambutol: optic neuritis
  - Pyrazinamide: hepatitis, hyperuricaemia
  - Hepatitis is the main significant side effect
  - Drug interactions with anaesthetic agents
- Comorbidities: co-infection, e.g. HIV (more active disease, atypical presentation, MDR TB)
- Surgical urgency: delay elective surgery until the patient is no longer infectious

## Intraoperative management
- Airborne transmission risk is highest during bronchoscopy, mask ventilation, intubation and suctioning
- Location: dedicated route to and from theatre, negative pressure theatre, clear signs on doors
- Staff
  - Immunisation against TB
  - PPE: gloves, gown, eye shield, N95 mask
  - Keep staff numbers in theatre to a minimum
  - After significant exposure: tuberculin test; if positive, 6-9 months of isoniazid chemoprophylaxis to prevent active disease
- Equipment
  - Disposable where possible (masks, laryngoscope blades)
  - Protect anaesthetic equipment: change the breathing circuit, sterilise reusable items
- Minimise aerosols and coughing
  - RSI to avoid positive pressure mask ventilation
  - Adequate depth and neuromuscular blockade to prevent coughing
  - Smooth extubation
  - Good antiemetic cover to limit nausea, retching and coughing after surgery
- Poor respiratory function
  - Lung protective ventilation
  - Opioid-sparing analgesia

## Flashcards
- Q: How long does TB culture take?
  - A: 2-12 weeks
- Q: What is the management after significant TB exposure with a positive tuberculin test?
  - A: 6-9 months of isoniazid chemoprophylaxis
- Q: Which TB drug causes optic neuritis?
  - A: Ethambutol
- Q: Why use RSI in a patient with infectious TB?
  - A: To avoid positive pressure mask ventilation and aerosol generation
