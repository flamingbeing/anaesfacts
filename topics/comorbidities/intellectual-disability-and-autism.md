# Intellectual Disability and Autism

Manage like any anxious child (premedication, parent present, gas induction) but tailored to the individual; plan ahead, keep it calm, treat pain and PONV generously.

## Summary
- Similar to general paediatric practice
  - Premedication, parental presence, inhalational induction, possibly difficult IV access
  - Pain assessment is difficult
- Tailor everything to the individual
- Consent

## Background
- Children with special needs may come for investigations, medical care or surgery
- Often **very anxious** in hospital and may not fit the usual routine
- In extreme cases the procedure is postponed or abandoned, or only possible with **heavy sedative premedication or restraint**
- Aim: meet individual needs in information, communication and pain assessment to keep the day calm for child and family
- Physical health may be good, but **developmental delay is often part of a syndrome** with other comorbidities (e.g. **cerebral palsy**, **Down syndrome**, metabolic disorders)

## Preoperative assessment
- Have a system that gives the **ward and anaesthetist advance warning** of a child with special needs
- Ideally complete a checklist before admission on the child's **physical and psychological needs**: mobility, how they communicate, likes, dislikes, phobias
  - Can be done by phone, or by a parent visiting without the child
  - Helps plan the logistics of the day
  - Encourage families to **bring familiar toys, activities or comfort objects**
  - **Autism**: **keep to the usual daily routine** as far as possible; avoid surprises
- **Communication (autism or hospital) passport**: a document of the child's needs, routines and communication strategies; have it available during the stay

## Preparation
- **First on the list** to shorten fasting
- **Low-stimulus setting**: quiet waiting area; calm anaesthetic room with parent or carer present, dim lighting (as far as safe), little noise and as few staff as possible
- For children with low cognitive ability, a **familiar carer** and physical comfort are especially important

## Premedication
- Encourage topical local anaesthetic cream for cannulation, though not all tolerate it; otherwise cannulate after inhalational induction
- **Sedative premedication** reduces anxiety and helps cooperation
  - Good preparation helps the child take oral medicine; it may need **hiding in cordial or a spoonful of a favourite food such as jam or yoghurt**
  - **Midazolam** is commonly used, but some children with ASD become paradoxically dysphoric
  - For challenging behaviour, ketamine or **ketamine plus midazolam often works**

## Restraint
- **Avoid physical restraint wherever possible**
  - Some children are routinely held for safety in daily life; **therapeutic holding by skilled parents or carers may be acceptable** for induction
- If restraint is unavoidable as a last resort, have enough trained staff to do it quickly and effectively; warn parents beforehand

## Documentation
- **Record how the child behaved** at induction and afterwards to **guide future anaesthetics**

## Intraoperative management
- **Good analgesia is essential**: generous local anaesthetic techniques, paracetamol, NSAIDs
- **Low threshold for antiemetics** and an **isotonic crystalloid bolus** to reduce PONV
- These matter more here because the **cause of postoperative distress is hard to tell apart**: pain, nausea, numbness from local anaesthetic, emergence delirium, unfamiliar nurses, leftover premedication

## Postoperative care
- Aim for a **quick smooth recovery and early discharge** after straightforward day surgery in physically well patients
  - After discussion with carers, may go home once back to baseline orientation and mobility, without meeting usual criteria (eating, drinking, passing urine)
- **IV cannulae often cause distress**: **remove early** once the airway is secure and no further IV drugs are needed
- **Secure and bandage** cannulae, nasogastric tubes and monitoring to prevent dislodgement
- **Pain assessment in non-verbal children is hard**: use behavioural pain scales and help from parents and carers
  - Many cannot use PCA after major surgery; nurse-controlled opioid protocols (background infusion plus boluses by pain score) may help
  - Help protect areas numbed by local anaesthetic (e.g. lip biting after dental surgery)

## Communication
- **Simple words**, speak clearly and directly, eye contact if possible
- Avoid jargon, abbreviations, acronyms, sarcasm and metaphor
- Children with limited speech may already use **augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)**; give information in their familiar method

### Augmentative and alternative communication
- Methods that add to speech or writing, for both expression and understanding
  - Unaided (body only): body language, facial expression, gesture, signing
  - Aided (needs equipment)
    - Low tech: writing, drawing, picture boards, symbols, objects
    - High tech (powered): message devices, computers, electronic aids

### Social stories
- Help people with ASD handle new social situations by explaining context, others' viewpoints and missed social cues, so they can model their responses
- Must be written carefully for the individual, ideally with carers
- If the child was prepared with a social story, work within that understanding on the day
- Example for local anaesthetic cream:
  - The nurse will put some cream on my hand
  - The cream is cold
  - It is held on by a plaster
  - The nurse will be very pleased if I keep my hand still

## Flashcards
- Q: Where on the list should a child with autism or learning disability go, and why?
  - A: First, to shorten fasting and waiting
- Q: Which premedication may cause paradoxical dysphoria in ASD, and what is an alternative?
  - A: Midazolam; ketamine or ketamine plus midazolam
- Q: Why give generous analgesia and antiemetics in these children?
  - A: Causes of postoperative distress are hard to tell apart
- Q: What is AAC?
  - A: Augmentative and alternative communication: unaided (gesture, signing) or aided (picture boards, devices)
