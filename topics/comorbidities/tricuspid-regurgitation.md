# Tricuspid Regurgitation

Keep the RV full, fast and contracting, and keep pulmonary vascular resistance low.

## Haemodynamic goals
- Preload: keep RV preload up for forward flow
  - A fall in CVP can sharply reduce RV stroke volume
- Heart rate: normal to high
  - Supports forward flow and limits peripheral congestion
  - Most patients are in chronic AF, so sinus rhythm is rarely achievable
- Contractility: maintain RV contractility
  - RV failure is the main cause of deterioration
  - The RV copes with volume load but not pressure load
  - May need inotropes perioperatively, especially with positive-pressure ventilation or raised PVR
  - Myocardial depressants can precipitate severe RV failure
- Afterload: systemic afterload changes matter little, unless aortic or mitral valve disease coexists
- Pulmonary vascular resistance: keep it low
  - Lower PVR improves RV function and forward flow
  - Hyperventilation to hypocapnia lowers PVR
  - Avoid high airway pressures
  - Avoid nitrous oxide (raises pulmonary artery pressure)
  - Inotropes that dilate the pulmonary circulation: dobutamine, isoprenaline (isoproterenol), milrinone
  - Inhaled nitric oxide can selectively lower PVR

## Flashcards
- Q: What heart rate is preferred in tricuspid regurgitation?
  - A: Normal to high.
- Q: What is the main cause of clinical deterioration in tricuspid regurgitation?
  - A: RV failure.
- Q: Which inhaled agent should be avoided in tricuspid regurgitation and why?
  - A: Nitrous oxide; it raises pulmonary artery pressure.
- Q: Which inotropes suit tricuspid regurgitation?
  - A: Dobutamine, isoprenaline or milrinone (pulmonary vasodilators).
- Q: Does systemic afterload matter much in isolated tricuspid regurgitation?
  - A: No, unless there is coexisting aortic or mitral disease.
