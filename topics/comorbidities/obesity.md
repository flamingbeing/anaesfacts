# Obesity

A multisystem disease: difficult and physiologically unforgiving airway, reduced FRC, OSA, cardiovascular disease, altered drug dosing and handling problems.

## Summary
- Airway: difficult airway, aspiration, apnoea intolerance
- Poor pulmonary compliance, atelectasis
- OSA: caution with opioids, CPAP
- OHS, leading to pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale
- Heart failure, IHD
- Metabolic syndrome
- Altered pharmacology
- Difficult lines and monitoring
- Difficult regional anaesthesia
- Positioning problems
- Difficult surgery

## Definitions (BMI, kg/m2)
- WHO:
  - Underweight: <18.5
  - Normal: 18.5 ‐ 24.9
  - Overweight: 25 ‐ 29.9
  - Obese: > 30
    - Class I: 30-34.9
    - Class II: 35-39.9
    - Class III (morbid obesity): >40
- Singapore Ministry of Health 2016 (Asian cut-offs):
  - Normal: 18.5 ‐ 23
  - Overweight: >23
  - Obese: >27.5
- Proposed additions:
  - Super obese: > 50
  - Super-super obese: > 60
- Central obesity (waist > half height), as opposed to peripheral obesity with fat outside the body cavities:
  - Difficult airway and ventilation more likely
  - Higher risk of cardiovascular disease and thrombosis
  - Higher risk of metabolic syndrome

## Causes
- Primary: calorie intake exceeds metabolism
  - Multifactorial: genetic and environmental
  - Impaired regulation of appetite and satiety
    - Satiety hormones: leptin, adiponectin, insulin, ghrelin
    - Leptin signals satiety and reduces eating and food-seeking
    - Obese patients often have high plasma leptin but reduced leptin sensitivity
    - Ghrelin increases appetite
- Secondary: endocrine disease (Cushing's, hypothyroidism)

## Airway
- Difficult airway, aspiration risk and apnoea intolerance
- NAP4: airway complications in obese patients are twice those in the general population, and higher still in the morbidly obese
  - Difficult airway and failed rescue techniques
  - Aspiration
  - Airway obstruction at emergence or in recovery
- Obesity is a risk factor for difficult mask ventilation, difficult LMA and difficult FONA
- BMI alone is a weak predictor of difficult intubation or laryngoscopy
  - Fat distribution matters more, especially parapharyngeal fat (thick neck)
- Also a physiologically difficult airway: reduced FRC, higher metabolic rate, aspiration risk
- Anatomical changes:
  - Excess soft tissue in the pharynx and larynx, especially with OSA
  - Large tongue
  - Larger neck circumference
  - Large anterior chest wall
  - Limited neck extension from the upper back fat pad: may need head and trunk elevation
- Associated with OSA

## Respiratory system
- Lung mechanics: FRC falls, compliance falls, work of breathing increases, leading to atelectasis and faster desaturation
  - Heavy chest wall lowers chest wall compliance; raised intra-abdominal pressure splints the diaphragm
  - High airway pressures because of reduced compliance
  - FRC may fall below closing capacity, so small airways close during normal breathing
    - Causes V/Q mismatch and shunt
    - Shortens safe apnoea time between induction and intubation
    - More postoperative atelectasis
  - Higher work of breathing (lower chest wall compliance, weaker respiratory muscles) can cause dyspnoea
- Higher O2 consumption and CO2 production from metabolically active fat: faster desaturation, more hypoxaemia and CO2 retention
- Wheeze-like symptoms from airway closure are common, caused by obesity itself rather than airway disease
- Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA):
  - 95% undiagnosed
  - About 70% of people with OSA are obese; 40% of obese people have OSA
  - Night-time apnoea and obstruction: snoring, broken sleep, daytime sleepiness
  - Makes ventilation and intubation even harder
  - Repeated night-time sympathetic surges may cause hypertension
  - May retain CO2 chronically
- Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS, Pickwickian syndrome):
  - Respiratory centres gradually become insensitive to PaCO2, leading to respiratory failure
  - Triad: obesity (BMI >30), daytime hypercapnia (PaCO2 ≥45 mmHg), sleep-disordered breathing
  - Also: hypoxaemia, polycythaemia, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale
  - 90% of OHS patients have OSA; 70% have severe OSA

## Cardiovascular system
- Blood volume, MAP and cardiac output increase to meet the metabolic demand of fat
  - Total body oxygen demand rises
  - Cardiac output rises by 0.1 L/min for each 1 kg of extra weight
  - Stroke volume rises by about 1.25ml per 1kg/m2 of BMI
  - Heart rate stays stable or rises slightly
- Metabolic syndrome: more atherosclerosis, coronary disease and IHD; more diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidaemia
- Left and right heart failure:
  - Hypertension leads to LVH, diastolic dysfunction and LV failure
  - OSA raises PVR, leading to pulmonary hypertension, RV failure and cor pulmonale
  - Fat between myocardial fibres and lipotoxicity from free fatty acids can cause myocyte loss and cardiomyopathy
- Arrhythmias:
  - From myocardial hypertrophy, hypoxaemia and fatty infiltration of the conduction system
  - Left atrial dilation increases AF risk
  - QT prolongation in 30% of obese patients, with more arrhythmia and sudden death

## Gastrointestinal
- Higher aspiration risk: raised intra-abdominal pressure (with or without GORD), larger gastric volumes, delayed gastric emptying
- Fatty liver (fat >10% of liver weight) is very common; ranges from simple steatosis to cirrhosis

## Endocrine
- Insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes (T2DM)

## Haematological
- Higher risk of thrombosis and DVT:
  - Raised intra-abdominal pressure impedes venous return
  - Immobility and poor calf muscle pump
  - Products of adipose tissue promote a hypercoagulable state

## Anaesthetic and surgical problems
- Effort tolerance is hard to assess
- Difficult venous access
- Difficult NIBP: needs the correct cuff size
- Positioning:
  - Table may be too narrow or below the weight limit (usually 130kg maximum); secure the patient safely
  - Transfers are difficult: more staff, hover mattress or slide sheets
  - More peripheral and superficial nerve compression injuries: take care with positioning; let the patient position themselves on the table
- Regional techniques may be anatomically difficult
  - Epidural venous engorgement: reduce epidural and intrathecal doses
- Difficult surgical access, so longer operations

## Preoperative assessment
- Airway:
  - Difficult laryngoscopy: fat distribution (thick neck), OSA
  - Aspiration risk: reflux symptoms, GLP-1 agonists, previous gastric bypass
- OSA:
  - STOP-BANG, sleep study, blood gases
  - CPAP use and compliance
  - Plan postoperative care: bring CPAP, high dependency bed
  - Look for pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure
- Cardiovascular risk:
  - Effort tolerance may be hard to judge
  - Cardiorespiratory referral
  - Consider echocardiography
  - Consider treadmill or dobutamine stress testing
- Optimise BP, diabetes, and left or right heart failure

## Premedication
- Avoid sedative premedication
- Consider antacids or PPI and prokinetics

## Intraoperative management

### Monitoring and access
- Venous access is often difficult; a CVC may be needed; anterior chest wall veins can help
- Ultrasound for access
- Correct-sized BP cuff; if the upper arm is impossible, a forearm cuff nearly always works (not validated but widely used)
- Arterial lines rarely needed unless for cardiovascular reasons
- Depth of anaesthesia monitoring
- Careful glucose control may be needed

### Equipment
- Bed, trolley and operating table: check the maximum weight
  - Table extensions and arm boards; avoid over-abducting the arm (brachial plexus injury)
  - Wide straps, gel padding
- Ramping: Troop pillow, pillows or blankets, raised arm supports, a step for the anaesthetist
- Large laryngoscope blades; airway adjuncts; consider fibreoptic scope
- Long spinal, regional and vascular needles

### Positioning and protection
- Pad pressure areas to prevent sores and nerve injury
- If able, the patient walks in, positions themselves on the table and is induced there, reducing manual handling
- Enough trained staff to move the patient if needed; hover mattress for transfer after surgery
- Calf compression devices for DVT prophylaxis

### Airway
- ETT, not LMA
- Ramped with Troop pillow, head up
- Peri-oxygenation (continuous oxygenation)
- Consider RSI

### Ventilation
- Adequate (higher) PEEP and recruitment manoeuvres
- Avoid spontaneous ventilation with an LMA (atelectasis)

### Maintenance
- Short-acting agents such as remifentanil and desflurane speed recovery and reduce postoperative hypoventilation and hypoxaemia
- Multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia; short-acting opioids (avoid morphine)

### Emergence
- Ensure full reversal of neuromuscular block, guided by neuromuscular monitoring (reversal dosing is hard to get right, and residual block is dangerous)
- Enough staff to hold the patient

## Postoperative care
- Recover sitting up
- High dependency care if OSA or high risk of OSA
- Consider avoiding day-case surgery:
  - Not an absolute contraindication; do not decide on BMI alone
  - Complications are most likely during anaesthesia and the immediate postoperative period
  - Risk depends mainly on the type of surgery and amount of postoperative opioid, not the type of anaesthesia
- Multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia; caution with long-acting opioids and sedatives
- Ward:
  - Continuous SpO2 monitoring; keep SpO2 at preoperative levels with minimal O2 and no hypoventilation before discharge from recovery
  - Restart the patient's own CPAP; keep in recovery longer until free of unstimulated apnoeas
  - Early mobilisation and robust thromboprophylaxis

## Airway management in morbid obesity
- Risks: difficult intubation, bag-mask ventilation and FONA; apnoea intolerance; aspiration
- Aspiration risk:
  - RSI with tracheal intubation
  - Suxamethonium may be ideal: rapid onset shortens the gap to intubation, and the patient can wake (less relevant now with sugammadex); but may increase oxygen consumption
  - Modified RSI: gentle face mask ventilation; release cricoid if the airway is difficult
  - Caution with LMAs if BMI is >35kg/m2 (aspiration); use a second-generation device if chosen
- Difficult airway:
  - Manage the airway in theatre, not a remote location
  - Consider ultrasound marking of the cricothyroid membrane for FONA
  - Ramped (head-elevated laryngoscopy position, HELP): tragus level with the sternum to align the airway axes
    - Specialised pillows (Troop, Oxford HELP pillow) or stacked blankets
    - Brings difficult intubation rates close to non-obese levels
    - Improves ventilation and preoxygenation and reduces reflux
  - Videolaryngoscopy; low threshold for awake fibreoptic intubation (rarely needed)
  - Paralysis: lean body weight for non-depolarising agents, total body weight for suxamethonium
- Apnoea intolerance (rapid desaturation):
  - Peri-oxygenation (continuous oxygenation) recommended
  - Preoxygenate to ETO2 >0.9; NIV or CPAP to improve recruitment
  - 30 degrees head-up tilt
  - Consider apnoeic oxygenation with HFNC
  - Early ventilation via an LMA (with adjuncts and two-hand mask technique) if mask ventilation is difficult, before or between intubation attempts (SOBA 2025 and DAS 2025)
- OSA: short-acting agents, opioid sparing, postoperative monitoring with O2 or CPAP

## Extubation
- Head-up, fully awake, fully reversed
- Consider extubating onto CPAP or HFNC, especially with OSA

## Pharmacology

### Summary
- Lean body weight for most drugs
- Adjusted body weight for propofol maintenance (avoids awareness), reversal agents and antibiotics
- Total body weight for suxamethonium

### Pharmacokinetic changes
- Dosing by total body weight can overdose
- Titrate to cardiac output, which matches lean body weight in a fit patient
- Volume of distribution changes: lean tissue is a smaller and fat a larger proportion of body weight
  - Lipophilic drugs (thiopental, propofol, opioids, benzodiazepines): larger Vd, normal clearance, longer elimination half-life
  - Hydrophilic drugs: similar absolute Vd, clearance and half-life; dose on ideal body weight +20% to allow for extra lean mass
  - All four aspects of pharmacokinetics are affected
- Raised alpha1-acid glycoprotein (α1) increases binding of basic drugs

### Weight scalars
- Total body weight (TBW): overestimates dose
- Ideal body weight (IBW): ignores body composition changes; underestimates dose
  - Broca formula: men height in cm minus 100; women height in cm minus 105
- Lean body weight (LBW): rises with TBW but less steeply; correlates with cardiac output, which drives early drug distribution
  - Hard to measure accurately
  - James equation: widely used, less accurate
  - Janmahasatian formula: more accurate but complex
  - Simplified (Carron, BJA): men 26 x height² (m²); women 22 x height² (m²)
  - Alternative: IBW plus 20%
  - LBW plateaus at about 100kg in men and 70kg in women
- Adjusted body weight (ABW):
  - ABW = correction factor x (TBW – IBW) + IBW
  - Developed for aminoglycosides; correction factor usually 0.4
  - That is, IBW + 40% of the excess weight
- Body surface area: ignores body composition; traditionally used for chemotherapy

### Dosing by drug (SOBA)
- Lean body weight (max 100kg in men, 70kg in women): most anaesthetic drugs except reversal agents
  - Propofol induction, thiopentone
  - Fentanyl, morphine, remifentanil
  - Paracetamol
  - Local anaesthetics: lidocaine, bupivacaine
  - Non-depolarising NMBDs (atracurium, rocuronium, vecuronium): avoids prolonged block; some dose to IBW
- Adjusted body weight (IBW plus 40% of excess): allows for larger Vd
  - Propofol maintenance (avoids awareness)
  - Reversal: neostigmine (max 5mg), sugammadex
  - Antibiotics
  - LMWH
- Total body weight:
  - Suxamethonium (max 200mg), because plasma cholinesterase activity is increased

## Flashcards
- Q: Which weight scalar is used for suxamethonium in obesity, and what is the maximum dose?
  - A: Total body weight, maximum 200mg
- Q: How is adjusted body weight calculated?
  - A: IBW + 0.4 x (TBW – IBW), i.e. IBW plus 40% of the excess
- Q: At what values does lean body weight plateau?
  - A: About 100kg in men and 70kg in women
- Q: What defines obesity hypoventilation syndrome?
  - A: BMI >30, daytime PaCO2 ≥45 mmHg, and sleep-disordered breathing
- Q: Above what BMI should LMAs be used with caution because of aspiration risk?
  - A: >35kg/m2
