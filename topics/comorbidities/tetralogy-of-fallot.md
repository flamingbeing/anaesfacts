# Tetralogy of Fallot

Commonest cyanotic congenital heart disease; anaesthesia centres on preventing and treating tet spells.

## Summary
- Avoid tet spells: premedicate to prevent stimulation and crying; titrated induction with vasopressors
- Know the anatomy; endocarditis prophylaxis for dental work
- Air bubble precautions on all lines

## Anatomy
- Associated with Down syndrome, DiGeorge and VACTERL
- 4 components
  - 1) Right ventricular outflow tract obstruction (RVOTO): pulmonary stenosis at any level, not only valvular
  - 2) Large VSD
  - 3) Aorta overriding the VSD
  - 4) Resulting right ventricular hypertrophy
- Severity of pulmonary stenosis sets the degree of right-to-left shunt and cyanosis

## Presentation
- Usually diagnosed
  - Antenatally
  - After a murmur is found in the first 2 months; cyanosis may not yet be obvious
  - A few present with severe cyanosis in the first days of life
- Features
  - Cyanosis; clubbing in older children
    - "Pink Fallot" if RVOTO is mild
  - Ejection systolic murmur at the left sternal edge from day 1 (pulmonary stenosis)
    - As the mainly subvalvular muscular obstruction worsens, the murmur shortens and cyanosis increases
  - During tet spells the murmur becomes inaudible as flow goes through the VSD instead of the RVOT
  - Single, loud S2

## Investigations
- ECG: RVH
- CXR
  - Boot-shaped heart: concave pulmonary bay plus upturned apex from RV enlargement
  - Reduced pulmonary vascular markings (oligaemic)
- 2D echo for the cardinal features

## Treatment
- Initially medical; treat tet spells
- Very cyanosed neonates may need
  - Modified Blalock-Taussig shunt (subclavian to pulmonary artery)
  - Balloon dilation of the RVOT
- Definitive repair at 6 to 9 months
  - VSD closed with an angled patch to correct the override
  - RVOTO relieved with a patch
  - Postoperative complication: ventricular arrhythmias
  - Postoperative murmur: pulmonary stenosis and regurgitation (to-and-fro), with a scar

## Hypercyanotic (tet) spells
- Dynamic RVOT obstruction (e.g. infundibular spasm); PVR/SVR balance shifts to less pulmonary flow and more right-to-left shunt
- Pathophysiology
  - Underfilled right heart: dehydration, tachycardia, increased contractility
  - High PVR: hypoxia, acidosis
  - Low SVR: dehydration
- Triggers
  - Sympathetic stimulation: pain, fever
  - Systemic vasodilation: fever, dehydration, exercise, hot bath
- Episodes of
  - Rapidly worsening cyanosis
  - Hyperpnoea (fast, deep breathing)
  - Murmur fades or disappears
- Usually under 2 years
- Complications: seizures, stroke, death

## Preventing tet spells
- Treat fever aggressively; keep well hydrated
- Propranolol and sedation as required
- Avoid adrenaline, dopamine, dobutamine: positive chronotropes and inotropes can worsen spells

## Managing a tet spell
- Calm the infant; remove triggers (e.g. pain)
- Knee-to-chest position: kinks femoral arteries to raise SVR and reduces return of hypoxic, acidic blood from the legs
  - Alternatives: squatting, or parent holds infant over their shoulder
- Sedation

## Managing a tet spell under anaesthesia
- Aims (like HOCM, plus lower PVR, correct acidosis, remove trigger)
  - Stop sympathetic stimulation
  - High preload: fluids
  - Low contractility
  - Slow heart rate
  - High SVR
  - Low PVR: correct acidosis
- Immediate measures
  - Stop triggers; deepen anaesthesia; top up analgesia
  - FiO2 1.0: lowers PVR and maximises dissolved oxygen (will not reverse cyanosis from intracardiac shunt)
  - Hyperventilate to reduce acidosis and PVR
    - Avoid high PEEP or airway pressures
  - Compress both femoral arteries to raise SVR
    - Compress the aorta if accessible during surgery
  - Fluid bolus up to 10ml/kg
  - Drugs
    - Phenylephrine 1-2mcg/kg to raise SVR
    - Propranolol 10-20mcg/kg test dose, then 0.05-0.1mg/kg over 10 mins, or
    - Esmolol 500 mcg/kg to relieve infundibular spasm
    - Bicarbonate 1-2 ml/kg slow IV bolus
  - Others: ketamine
- Emergency Blalock-Taussig shunt if intractable

## Advice to parents
- Minimise agitation and calm the child quickly
  - Fever is a common trigger: give regular antipyretics during fever
  - Keep well hydrated; see a doctor early if feeding poorly
- Spells can be life threatening
  - Calm the child and place in knee-chest position
  - If the spell has not ended after 5 mins, go urgently to the nearest emergency department
