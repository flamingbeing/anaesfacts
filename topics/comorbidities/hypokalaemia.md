# Hypokalaemia and potassium replacement

Serum K below 3.5 mEq/L; replace orally if mild, IV if symptomatic or below 3.0, and correct magnesium.

## Definition and targets
- Serum K less than 3.5 mEq/L
- Keep K above 4 in patients on digoxin or with arrhythmias

## Causes
1. Low dietary intake: rarely enough on its own, but contributes
2. Losses
   - 2A. Gastrointestinal (spot urine K:Cr less than 1)
     - Diarrhoea, laxatives, villous adenoma
     - Vomiting
   - 2B. Renal (spot urine K:Cr more than 1)
     - Normotensive or hypotensive
       - Diuretics (furosemide, hydrochlorothiazide)
       - Hypomagnesaemia (impairs tubular K reabsorption, so urinary K loss increases)
       - Renal tubular acidosis (type 1 and 2), inherited tubular disorders
       - Non-reabsorbable anions
       - Severe polyuria (usually psychogenic)
     - Hypertensive: excess mineralocorticoid activity
       - Primary (1°): Conn's syndrome
       - Secondary (2°) hyperaldosteronism: renovascular disease, renin-secreting tumour
       - Non-aldosterone mineralocorticoid (Cushing's, exogenous)
     - Gastric outlet obstruction can cause urinary K wasting
   - 2C. Other losses
     - Sweat
     - Dialysis, plasmapheresis
3. Shift into cells
   - Insulin (stimulates Na+/K+ ATPase), dextrose, refeeding syndrome
   - Catecholamines, β2 agonists (salbutamol/Ventolin), theophylline, tocolytics
   - Metabolic alkalosis (K+/H+ exchange): K falls by less than 0.4 mEq/L per 0.1 rise in pH
   - Head injury (β2 adrenergic activity stimulates Na+/K+ ATPase)
   - Uptake into new cells: vitamin B12 in pernicious anaemia, colony-stimulating factors raising white cell production
   - Hypokalaemic periodic paralysis
4. Spurious: many metabolically active cells in the sample (e.g. AML)
   - Prevent by separating plasma quickly after sampling, or storing blood at 4 degrees before assay

## Clinical features
- Often asymptomatic, especially when mild (3.0-3.5 mEq/L)
- Nausea and vomiting, constipation (ileus)
- Myalgia, cramps, generalised weakness (K below 3, ascending paralysis), rhabdomyolysis (K below 2.5), rarely paralysis with respiratory failure
  - Weakness is thought to come from reduced muscle membrane excitability
- Arrhythmias (tachy and brady) at variable K levels; more likely with digoxin, hypomagnesaemia or coronary disease
- Others: mild polyuria (concentrating defect, about 2-3 L per day), metabolic alkalosis

## Examination
- Check blood pressure: if high, consider hypertension treated with diuretics, Conn's, renal artery stenosis, hypercortisolism

## Investigations
- ECG: predicts the clinical picture better than serum K
  - Pattern looks as if the T wave is pushed down
  - T wave flattening or inversion
  - ST depression
  - U waves: the key sign (small wave after the T wave, best in V2 and V3)
  - QTc prolongation
  - Severe hypokalaemia: PR prolongation, wide QRS, ectopics and arrhythmias
- Serum Mg2+
- Urinary K excretion
  - Spot urine K:Cr: should be below 1.5 mEq/mmol when hypokalaemic, otherwise suspect renal loss (creatinine excretion is near constant, so corrects for urine volume)
  - 24 h urine K (most accurate): should be below 25-30 mEq/day when hypokalaemic, otherwise suspect renal loss
- Plasma renin and aldosterone
- Creatine kinase if muscle pain or weakness
- Pattern with acid-base status
  - Renal K wasting with metabolic acidosis: DKA, RTA
  - Renal K wasting with metabolic alkalosis: diuretics, vomiting, hyperaldosteronism
  - Non-renal loss with metabolic acidosis: diarrhoea
  - Non-renal loss with metabolic alkalosis: vomiting, diuretics (urine collected after the diuretic effect wears off)

## Management
- Treat the cause
  - Stop diuretics; K-sparing diuretics (triamterene, spironolactone, amiloride) can limit renal loss
  - Replace Mg2+ if low: hypokalaemia often does not correct until magnesium is replaced
- Oral replacement: asymptomatic, K above 3.0 and able to take oral drugs
  - Span-K 600 mg, 1-2 tablets once to twice daily (large tablet, cannot be crushed)
    - 8 mmol per 600 mg tablet, slightly less than 1 cycle
  - Mist KCl 5-10 ml three times daily (bitter, can go down a nasogastric tube), for a set time (e.g. 2 days)
    - 10 ml (1.34 mmol/ml) = 13.4 mmol, slightly more than 1 cycle
  - Potassium citrate: 10 ml = 28 mmol; 1 tablet = 10 mmol
  - Dose 40 to 100 mEq per day orally in 2 to 5 divided doses
  - Maximum single dose 40 mEq (or 1 mEq/kg)
  - Maximum daily dose 200 mEq (usual adult intake 50-100)
  - Recheck after 3 days (K rises slowly)
  - Oral allows larger replacement but absorption takes time
- IV replacement: symptomatic, ECG changes or K below 3.0 (severe)
  - KCl 100 ml bag (10 mmol in 100 ml or 20 mmol in 100 ml)
    - Each 10 mmol raises K by about 0.1 mmol/L
    - Maximum 10 mmol via a peripheral vein, 20 mmol via a central line
  - Potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4): 10 mmol K and 10 mmol PO4; use if phosphate is also low
  - Premixed infusions
  - Recheck symptoms, ECG and K 2 hrs later
  - Rate: general ward, non-urgent, maximum 10 mmol/hour; urgent, up to 40 mmol/hr with continuous cardiac monitoring
  - Give in saline; avoid dextrose (insulin release can worsen hypokalaemia)
- Estimating the deficit
  - About 10% of total body K is lost per 1 mEq/L fall in serum K
  - Even mild hypokalaemia (K 3 mEq/L) means a large deficit (175 mEq)
- Cautions
  - Impaired urine output or renal function: correct with great care
  - Avoid overcorrection, especially when transcellular shift is the cause
  - Keep K above 4 on digoxin
  - Peritoneal dialysis patients usually need regular supplements
- Critical hypokalaemia (below 2 mmol/l), ECG changes, or need for fast replacement (more than 10 mmol/hour)
  - Telemetry
  - High dependency care

## Flashcards
- Q: Maximum IV potassium rate on a general ward and when urgent?
  - A: 10 mmol/hour non-urgent; up to 40 mmol/hr with continuous cardiac monitoring
- Q: Why can hypokalaemia be refractory to replacement?
  - A: Coexisting magnesium deficiency; replace Mg2+ first
- Q: Most important ECG sign of hypokalaemia?
  - A: U waves (best seen in V2 and V3)
- Q: How much does 10 mmol of IV KCl raise serum K?
  - A: About 0.1 mmol/L
- Q: Target K in a patient on digoxin?
  - A: Above 4
