# Induction of Anaesthesia

Taking a patient from consciousness to a surgical plane of anaesthesia; drug choice and sequence depend on the airway plan and haemodynamics.

## Definition
- Starting a general anaesthetic ("putting the patient to sleep")
- Bringing a patient from consciousness to a surgical plane of anaesthesia
- Alternative in a difficult airway: awake intubation with airway topicalisation and/or sedation

## Goals
- Blunt airway reflexes (including laryngospasm)
- Blunt the haemodynamic response to airway instrumentation
- Avoid coughing and bucking
- Enough muscle relaxation for laryngoscopy
- Patient comfort

## Routine induction for intubation with neuromuscular blockade
- A version of the triad: hypnosis, analgesia, paralysis
  - Rapid-acting IV anaesthetic and opioid, then a neuromuscular blocking drug (NMBD)
- IV anaesthetic: propofol is most common unless haemodynamically unstable
  - Alternative: TCI propofol
- Opioid: fentanyl most common; remifentanil or alfentanil also used
  - May be omitted (e.g. some paediatric practice)
- Muscle relaxation improves intubating conditions by:
  - Easing laryngoscopy
  - Preventing reflex laryngeal and vocal cord closure
  - Preventing coughing

### Options
- IV induction agents: propofol, etomidate, thiopentone, ketamine
- Co-induction or pretreatment: fentanyl, lidocaine, midazolam
- Inhalational co-induction
- Muscle relaxant

## Choice of induction agent
- Propofol
  - Very effective at suppressing reflexes
- Ketamine
  - Indications: trauma; preserves spontaneous ventilation and airway tone
  - Contraindications: myocardial ischaemia (tachycardia), raised ICP
  - Emergence delirium
  - Interferes with BIS
  - Increases secretions
- Midazolam
  - Emergence delirium, delayed emergence
- Etomidate
  - Risk of adrenal suppression
- Thiopentone
  - May be used in pregnancy and ECT
  - Less effective at reducing muscle tone and reflexes
  - Higher risk of awareness

## Choice of opioid
- Fentanyl
- Remifentanil: chest wall rigidity, bradycardia
- Alfentanil

## Choice of muscle relaxant
- Atracurium
  - Indication: routine
  - Contraindication: asthma
- Rocuronium
  - Indications: RSI, myasthenia
  - Contraindication: renal failure
  - Immediately reversible with sugammadex 16mg/kg
- Suxamethonium
  - Indications: RSI (fastest onset), difficult airway (short acting)
  - Contraindications: MH, hyperkalaemia, crush injury, rhabdomyolysis
  - Fasciculations may raise oxygen consumption and shorten safe apnoea time
  - Wears off in 10min

## Traditional pretreatment and co-induction
- Lidocaine 1.5mg/kg IV
  - Sympatholytic; neuroprotection in head injury; reduces airway reactivity in asthma
- Defasciculating dose of a non-depolarising NMBD (e.g. rocuronium 0.1 mg/kg IV or vecuronium 0.01 mg/kg IV)
  - Prevents suxamethonium fasciculations (e.g. traumatic brain injury)
- Atropine 20 mcg/kg IV
  - Prevents bradycardia in children

## Sequence (by onset time)
- 1) Fentanyl first (unless RSI)
- 2) Propofol (with or without lidocaine)
- Attempt mask ventilation
- 3) NMBD once asleep, then wait and ventilate until it works (2-3mins for atracurium; 1min for rocuronium)

## Timing of paralysis
- Traditional teaching: hold the NMBD until mask ventilation is proven, so a preoxygenated patient could resume breathing or wake before desaturating
- In practice, waking the patient after failed mask ventilation is rarely done
- Relaxation makes mask ventilation easier; withholding it can create a difficult mask ventilation that would not otherwise occur

## Restoring spontaneous ventilation in unexpected airway difficulty
- Suxamethonium: breathing returns in 8–10 minutes; much longer with low plasma cholinesterase (elderly, pregnant, septic)
- Rocuronium: reversible within minutes with sugammadex 16mg/kg
- Induction agent effects (e.g. propofol) persist even after reversal of paralysis
- Restoring airway tone and spontaneous breathing soon after induction is very difficult, with or without paralysis: do not wait; front-of-neck access (FONA) is more reliable

## Induction for LMA insertion
- Less stimulating than laryngoscopy, so less opioid and induction agent needed
  - Still needs adequate depth to prevent laryngospasm
- No muscle relaxant needed
- Spontaneous ventilation can be the aim from the start

## Specific situations
### Haemodynamically unstable patient (cardiostable induction)
- Resuscitate and prepare before induction, balanced against surgical urgency
  - Fluid resuscitation
  - Arterial line, central line with vasopressors
  - Defibrillator pads, crash cart in theatre
- Slow, titrated induction
  - Haemodynamically neutral agents
  - Avoid high-dose opioids, which may remove sympathetic tone
  - Push-dose pressors (e.g. phenylephrine or ephedrine) with the induction agent; vasopressor infusions running

### Cardioprotective induction (blunting sympathetic response)
- High-dose opioids

### Rapid sequence induction
- See the RSI topic

### Patient already intubated (e.g. ICU)
- Can induce by inhalation down the tube instead of IV (potentially more stable)
- If already on propofol sedation, increase the infusion

### Cardiac arrest
- No drugs (except possibly paralysis)

### Paediatrics
- Higher propofol doses, e.g. 5mg/kg
- Have a flush drawn up

### Obstetrics (e.g. caesarean section under GA)
- Thiopentone was the traditional gold standard, but propofol is shown to be safe
- Avoid opioids until the baby is delivered

## Flashcards
- Q: Sugammadex dose for immediate reversal of rocuronium?
  - A: 16mg/kg
- Q: How long until spontaneous ventilation returns after suxamethonium?
  - A: 8–10 minutes; longer with low plasma cholinesterase (elderly, pregnant, septic)
- Q: Lidocaine pretreatment dose at induction?
  - A: 1.5mg/kg IV
- Q: Atropine pretreatment dose in children?
  - A: 20 mcg/kg IV
- Q: Onset time after giving atracurium versus rocuronium?
  - A: 2-3mins for atracurium; 1min for rocuronium
