# Airway Topicalisation and Airway Blocks for Awake Intubation

How to anaesthetise the nose, pharynx, larynx and trachea (topical local anaesthetic and nerve blocks) for awake tracheal intubation.

## Quick summary

- Maximum topical lidocaine: **9 mg/kg** in adults, 4.5 mg/kg in children
- Nose (anterior ethmoidal and palatine nerves): co-phenylcaine
- Oropharynx, glossopharyngeal territory: spray the tonsillar pillars
- Oropharynx, vagal territory: spray the pharyngeal walls
- Above the cords (superior laryngeal nerve): spray as you go (SAYGO)
- Below the cords (recurrent laryngeal nerve): SAYGO or transtracheal injection
- Trismus: intra-oral local anaesthetic and blocks may be impossible; use nebulised lidocaine or a transtracheal injection
- Clear blood, secretions or vomit from the airway first, or the drug cannot reach the mucosa

## Sensory innervation

- Upper airway = nasal and oral cavities, pharynx and larynx
- Supplied by the trigeminal (V), glossopharyngeal (IX) and vagus (X) nerves

1. Nasal cavity (trigeminal, V)
   - Anterior part and septum: anterior ethmoidal nerve (ophthalmic division, V1)
   - Posterior part and the rest: greater and lesser palatine nerves (maxillary division, V2)
   - Landmark: pterygopalatine ganglion, behind the middle turbinate
2. Pharynx and oropharynx (all three nerves)
   - Trigeminal (V): anterior two-thirds of the tongue (lingual nerve, mandibular division V3)
   - Glossopharyngeal (IX): posterior one-third of the tongue, vallecula, anterior surface of the epiglottis, tonsils and fauces (tonsillar nerve)
   - Vagus (X): posterior and lateral pharyngeal walls (pharyngeal nerve)
3. Larynx (vagus, X)
   - Above the cords: internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve (base of tongue, posterior epiglottis, aryepiglottic folds, arytenoids)
   - Cords and below: recurrent laryngeal nerve (subglottis and trachea)

## Drugs

### Lidocaine

- Main topicalisation agent; forms:
  - 1, 2 or 4% solution
  - 10% spray (10 mg per spray)
  - 5% spray within co-phenylcaine (5 mg per spray)
  - Gel
  - Paste
- About 25% is absorbed systemically from the upper airway
  - This limited uptake is why doses up to 9 mg/kg are tolerated

### Vasoconstrictors

- Needed for the nose: reduce bleeding and shrink the mucosa, making room for the scope and tube
- Xylometazoline or phenylephrine, often combined with lidocaine
  - Co-phenylcaine: each squirt gives 5 mg lidocaine (5%) and 0.5 mg phenylephrine
- Wait for onset before instrumenting

### Cocaine

- The only local anaesthetic that is itself a vasoconstrictor
- 5% or 10% solution, or paste
- Maximum recommended dose 1.5 mg/kg
- More cardiovascular side effects than lidocaine with phenylephrine
- Moffett's solution (10 mL): 2 mL 10% cocaine + 1 mL adrenaline 1:1000 + 2 mL sodium bicarbonate + 5 mL sodium chloride 0.9%

## Topical application techniques

- Options: nebulisation, spray from the container, direct contact (ribbon gauze or cotton applicators), SAYGO, McKenzie technique, mucosal atomisation device (MAD)
- Maximum topical lidocaine: 9 mg/kg adults, 4.5 mg/kg children

### Nebulisation

- 5 mL of 4% lidocaine, driven by oxygen, for up to 30 minutes
- Non-invasive and well tolerated
- Covers the upper airway down to the trachea
- Good for trismus, when atomisers cannot be placed
- Patient must sit up for a mask nebuliser to work

### Direct contact

- Ribbon gauze or cotton applicators soaked in local anaesthetic or lidocaine paste
- Gives targeted numbing

### Spray from container

- Spray the nasopharynx and oropharynx straight from the local anaesthetic container
- Use a long nozzle

### Spray as you go (SAYGO)

- Spray the mucosa and cords directly under vision
- Delivery: syringe with air, or a 16-gauge epidural catheter passed down the scope's working channel until its tip is just visible
- Lidocaine 2% or 4%

### McKenzie technique

- 20-gauge cannula connected by a three-way tap to oxygen bubble tubing; the tubing's other end goes to the oxygen source via a 1 ml syringe; flow 2–4 L/min
- Local anaesthetic given slowly from a 5-mL syringe on the cannula's top port
- Produces a jet spray that spreads the drug over a wide area; can be aimed at nasal and oral mucosa
- Keep the tubing–cannula join tight so no local anaesthetic leaks

### Mucosal atomisation device (MAD)

- Commercial atomiser fitted to a syringe; gives a mist like the McKenzie technique
- Nasal or oral use
- Also used for intranasal premedication

## Complications of topicalisation

- Blunts cough and swallow reflexes; aspiration risk
- Pharyngeal topicalisation can cause transient obstruction at the glottis, from loss of reflex control of airway calibre

## Landmark nerve blocks

- Benefit: better airway anaesthesia than topical alone
- Risks: intravascular injection, nerve injury
- Several nerves needed, so time consuming; technically harder
- Oropharynx and larynx: glossopharyngeal, superior laryngeal and translaryngeal blocks
- Nose (palatine and anterior ethmoidal nerves): still needed for awake nasal intubation, usually done topically (inhalation, spray, soaked applicators)

### Glossopharyngeal nerve block

- Nerve supplies the posterior third of the tongue and vallecula and is the sensory limb of the gag reflex; block mainly abolishes gag
- Easiest site: where it crosses the palatoglossal arch
- Spray, soaked gauze or pledgets, or injection around the nerve
- Alone it is not enough for awake fibreoptic intubation
- Intraoral approach
  - Needs enough mouth opening to see the base of the posterior tonsillar pillar (palatopharyngeal arch); the anterior pillar can be injected instead
  - After lidocaine spray, retract the tongue medially (laryngoscope blade or tongue depressor)
  - 22- or 25-gauge needle; 2 mL 2% lidocaine submucosally after negative aspiration
  - Site: caudal end of the posterior pillar, about 0.5 cm lateral to the tongue edge where it meets the floor of the mouth; repeat other side
  - Risk: carotid injection
  - Alternative: hold soaked gauze firmly over the site for a few minutes; no intravascular risk but less effective
- Peristyloid approach
  - Target: just behind the styloid process, where the nerve lies; internal carotid artery close by
  - Supine, head neutral; styloid process at the midpoint of a line from the angle of the jaw to the mastoid tip (deep palpation, may be uncomfortable)
  - Needle perpendicular to skin to contact the styloid (usually 1–2 cm deep)
  - Redirect posteriorly and walk off the styloid; inject 5–7 mL 2% lidocaine after negative aspiration; repeat other side

### Superior laryngeal nerve block

- Anatomy
  - Supplies laryngeal structures above the cords
  - Divides below the greater cornu of the hyoid into internal and external branches
  - Internal branch pierces the thyrohyoid membrane about 2–4 mm below the greater cornu, then runs submucosally in the piriform recess
  - External branch stays outside the membrane, descending deep to sternothyroid
- External approach
  - Supine with some neck extension to find the hyoid
  - Push the hyoid towards the block side; 25-gauge needle from the lateral neck towards the greater cornu
  - Contact the greater cornu, walk off inferiorly, inject 2 mL 2% lidocaine: blocks internal and external branches
  - Advance a few millimetres through the thyrohyoid membrane ("give"): injection here blocks only the internal branch
  - Aspirate carefully first; carotid artery is close
  - If the hyoid is hard to find: use the superior cornu of the thyroid cartilage (follow the upper edge back from the thyroid notch; small round structure just below the greater cornu of the hyoid); walk cephalad off it and inject
  - If the membrane is pierced, give 2 mL there and another 2 mL while withdrawing, to improve the chance of blocking both branches
- Internal approach
  - Soaked gauze or pledgets in the piriform fossae with Krause's forceps
  - Leave for 5-10 minutes

### Translaryngeal (transtracheal) block for the recurrent laryngeal nerve

- Recurrent laryngeal nerve supplies the cords and trachea; runs in the tracheo-oesophageal groove; motor to all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid
- Never block it directly: motor and sensory fibres run together, so bilateral cord palsy and obstruction can follow
- Technique
  - Supine, neck extended; find the midline and move down to the cricoid; cricothyroid membrane sits just above it
  - Stabilise the larynx at the thyroid cartilage with thumb and third finger
  - 22 or 20 gauge needle, perpendicular, through the membrane in the midline, aspirating continuously
  - Air bubbles = tip in the trachea; stop advancing at once to avoid puncturing the posterior wall
  - Inject 5 mL of 4% lidocaine rapidly and withdraw; the cough spreads the drug

### Sphenopalatine ganglion block (nose)

- Ganglion lies in the posterior nose between the upper and middle turbinates
- Topical: soaked cotton-tipped applicator or atomiser spray
- Slow onset over several minutes

## Ultrasound-guided techniques

- Improves success: more accurate deposition at the greater cornu for superior laryngeal nerve block; locates the cricothyroid membrane for translaryngeal block
- Visible structures: hyoid bone, thyroid cartilage, thyrohyoid membrane, superior laryngeal artery, superior laryngeal nerve

### Superior laryngeal nerve block at the greater cornu

- Useful when landmarks are hard to feel, e.g. obesity
- Sagittal probe to find the greater cornu, then rotate transverse to see the upper lateral thyrohyoid membrane; nerve lies superficial to the membrane
- Internal branch runs with the superior laryngeal artery just below the greater cornu
- Alternative: find the hyoid in the midline (bright curved line), slide laterally; greater cornu is a bright structure medial to the superior laryngeal artery
- In-plane needle aimed just below the greater cornu; 1–2 mL local anaesthetic after negative aspiration
- Success over 90%; failures put down to variable nerve position relative to the hyoid

### Superior laryngeal nerve block at the thyrohyoid membrane

- Probe parasagittal over thyroid cartilage and hyoid to find the space
- Out-of-plane needle
- Feel the pop; inject deep to the thyrohyoid membrane

### Translaryngeal block

- Longitudinal midline probe: tracheal rings, then (moving cranially) the cricoid (larger, more superficial, elongated), then thyroid cartilage
- Cricothyroid membrane lies between the lower border of thyroid cartilage and upper border of cricoid
- Centre the membrane on screen and mark the skin, then do the block
- Real-time option: tilt to parasagittal keeping the cricoid in view; enter just cranial to the cricoid; aspirated air confirms tracheal position
- Visible: tracheal rings, cricoid cartilage, thyroid cartilage, cricothyroid membrane
