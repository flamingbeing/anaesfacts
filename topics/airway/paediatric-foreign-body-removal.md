# Paediatric Foreign Body Removal

Anaesthesia for removal of inhaled or swallowed foreign bodies by laryngoscopy, rigid oesophagoscopy and/or rigid bronchoscopy.

## Background
- Most common at 1-3 years of age; can be life threatening
- Inhaled objects are usually organic (peanuts common); few are radio-opaque
- Organic material, especially peanut, causes airway hyperreactivity
- Coins usually lodge in the upper oesophagus

## Where objects lodge
- Large (>20 mm): oropharynx
  - An oesophageal object can cause respiratory distress by compressing the trachea
- Medium (8-15 mm) or irregular: larynx
- Small, smooth, linear: bronchi
  - Objects passing the cords rarely block the trachea
  - Distribution: right main bronchus 50%, left 45%, trachea 5%
  - Ball-valve effect: air in on inspiration but not out, causing hyperinflation

## Presentation
- Cough, choking, drooling, wheeze, dyspnoea or cyanosis
- Sometimes unnoticed: fever and chest infection days later, or atypical respiratory symptoms months later
- Signs: airway obstruction, wheeze, reduced breath sounds

## Investigations
- CXR may be normal; radio-opaque objects visible
- Other findings: lobar or lung collapse, mediastinal shift, pneumothorax
- End-expiratory film may show one-sided emphysema or hyperinflation on the affected side
- Examination and removal under GA are required
- After removal, laryngeal, subglottic or tracheal mucosal oedema may develop

## Preoperative assessment
- Respiratory distress varies; many children are calm
- Urgency depends on symptoms and degree of distress
- Fasting is usually possible
- Partial obstruction, hypoxia or marked distress: proceed even if not fasted (delay is riskier than aspiration)

## Laryngoscopy
- Used when the object is in the oropharynx or near the laryngeal inlet
- No sedative premedication
- Aim: patent airway with spontaneous breathing throughout
- Inhalational induction with halothane or sevoflurane in 100% oxygen
- Upper airway obstruction slows induction; gentle CPAP helps
- When deep enough, site IV cannula and give atropine 20 mcg/kg or glycopyrronium 5-10 mcg/kg
  - Dries secretions and protects against bradycardia from instrumentation or high halothane concentrations

## Bronchoscopy
- Supine with a roll under the scapulae to extend the neck and bring the trachea forward
- Spray the cords with lidocaine (up to 4 mg/kg); dense laryngeal topicalisation prevents coughing and breath holding
- T-piece circuit attached to the side arm of the Storz bronchoscope
- Maintenance: volatile in 100% oxygen; spontaneous ventilation preferred
  - Gentle assisted ventilation if hypoventilating, too light or hypoxic
- Inserting the telescope narrows the lumen and can greatly increase work of breathing, especially in infants
  - May need to withdraw the telescope intermittently to ventilate
- After removal, intubate with a standard ETT 0.5-1 mm smaller than the age-based size; extubate awake
- Dexamethasone 0.25 mg/kg IV often given to reduce oedema
- Significant airway oedema (stridor, accessory muscle use, oxygen-requiring hypoxia): keep intubated and ventilated in ICU

## Oesophagoscopy and OGD
- Paralyse and intubate: RSI
- Extubate awake
- Risk of oesophageal perforation after rigid oesophagoscopy
  - Suspected perforation: nil by mouth and IV antibiotics
  - Look for pneumomediastinum on CXR
  - Watch for mediastinitis: tachycardia, chest pain, pyrexia, subcutaneous emphysema

## Postoperative care
- Most go back to a general ward
- ICU if ventilated; HDU observation if stridor
- Nebulised adrenaline 400 mcg/kg of 1:1000 relieves stridor from mucosal oedema but lasts only 2-3 hours; repeat regularly
- Dexamethasone may be continued at 100 mcg/kg IV 6-hourly for 24 hours
- IV maintenance fluids until drinking
- Paracetamol plus an NSAID
- Antibiotics if late presentation with infection; CXR required

## Flashcards
- Q: Peak age for foreign body inhalation or ingestion?
  - A: 1-3 years.
- Q: Where do inhaled foreign bodies past the cords end up?
  - A: Right main bronchus 50%, left 45%, trachea 5%.
- Q: Atropine and glycopyrronium doses before airway instrumentation?
  - A: Atropine 20 mcg/kg; glycopyrronium 5-10 mcg/kg.
- Q: Maximum lidocaine dose sprayed on the cords before bronchoscopy?
  - A: Up to 4 mg/kg.
- Q: Nebulised adrenaline dose for post-extraction stridor and how long it lasts?
  - A: 400 mcg/kg of 1:1000; lasts 2-3 hours.
