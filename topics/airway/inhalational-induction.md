# Inhalational Induction and Co-induction

Volatile (usually sevoflurane) induction: needle-free and keeps spontaneous breathing, but slow and poor at blunting airway reflexes.

## Advantages and indications
- Common in paediatrics: painless, needle-free
  - Also avoids propofol injection pain (thiopentone is an IV alternative)
- Keeps spontaneous ventilation, e.g. difficult airway
- Depth can change gradually, with cardiovascular stability
- Volatile agents and propofol are both potent bronchodilators

## Disadvantages
- Children may fear the mask; unfamiliar smell can cause anxiety
- Slower and possibly less smooth induction
- Blunts airway reflexes less than propofol: more laryngospasm, bronchospasm, severe coughing and desaturation
- Not for RSI or full stomach
- Airway obstruction risk in OSA

## Technique
- Sevoflurane is the usual agent: not pungent, low blood:gas solubility, smooth induction
  - Can give airway management conditions with or without NMBDs or opioids
- Tidal volume induction: normal breathing through the face mask
  - Can start at a lower concentration and step up for tolerance
- Vital capacity (single-breath) induction: exhale to residual volume, then one vital capacity breath from the mask
  - Uses high delivered sevoflurane, 8%
- Nitrous oxide with either method speeds induction (second-gas effect)
- Suitable for LMA insertion or intubation
  - Intubation on sevoflurane alone needs deep anaesthesia, typically MAC 2.0, so hypotension is more likely
  - Propofol, fast-onset opioids, NMBDs or ketamine improve intubating conditions at lower end-tidal sevoflurane

## Co-induction
- Combining drugs for a greater effect than each alone
- Useful when haemodynamically unstable or when spontaneous ventilation is wanted
- Example: sevoflurane 4%, then reduced-dose propofol 0.75 mg/kg
- Advantages
  - More gradual haemodynamic change
  - Lower IV anaesthetic doses
  - Propofol suppresses airway reflexes better than volatile alone

## Inhalational induction for the difficult airway
- Historically the technique of choice; still important in children, questioned in adults
- Advantage: spontaneous ventilation, and so oxygenation, more likely to be kept
- Problems
  - Obstruction stops volatile washout, so the hoped-for rapid wake-up does not happen
  - Negative inspiratory pressure can collapse the upper airway as consciousness is lost
  - Slow to reach depth: longer in stage 2 and laryngospasm; slower still with obstruction
- Evidence
  - NAP4: in 16 inhalational inductions, the airway was compromised in 12 and spontaneous ventilation lost in 11
  - DAS 2025 recommends against it

## Flashcards
- Q: What sevoflurane concentration is used for vital capacity induction?
  - A: 8%.
- Q: What MAC of sevoflurane is typically needed for airway management with sevoflurane alone?
  - A: MAC 2.0.
- Q: Give an example co-induction regimen.
  - A: Sevoflurane 4% followed by propofol 0.75 mg/kg.
- Q: NAP4 outcome of inhalational induction in difficult airways?
  - A: Of 16 cases, the airway was compromised in 12 and spontaneous ventilation lost in 11.
- Q: Why does inhalational induction not guarantee rapid wake-up in an obstructed airway?
  - A: Obstruction prevents off-gassing of the volatile agent.
