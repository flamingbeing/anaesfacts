# Surgical Tracheostomy

Insertion of a tracheal tube through a neck incision; the main anaesthetic risks are airway fire and hypoxia during the tube change.

## Indications

- Acute upper airway obstruction that an oral or nasal tracheal tube cannot bypass
- Long-term positive pressure ventilation
  - Neurological disease
  - Neuromuscular disease
  - OSA
  - Subglottic stenosis
- Airway maintenance and lung protection when pharyngeal or laryngeal reflexes are impaired, or after major head and neck surgery (e.g. laryngectomy)
- Long-term control of heavy bronchial secretions, especially with reduced consciousness
- Weaning from ventilation: less sedation needed (better tolerated than a tracheal tube) and less anatomical dead space

## Benefits

- Less risk of glottic trauma
- Better ventilatory mechanics: less dead space and airway resistance
- Better suctioning; easier oral hygiene
- Less sedation
- Earlier mobilisation
- More comfortable
- Oral feeding possible
- Communication possible (speaking valve)

## Complications

- Mortality below 1%, but overall complication rate up to 40%; higher in ICU and emergency patients
- Immediate
  - Haemorrhage
  - Tube misplacement (e.g. into a main bronchus)
  - Tube occlusion by cuff herniation
  - Tube tip occluded against the carina or tracheal wall
  - Pneumothorax
- Delayed
  - Tube blocked by secretions, sudden or gradual (rare with good humidification and suction)
  - Stoma infection
  - Cuff overinflation: tracheal ulceration and dilatation
  - Mucosal ulceration from high cuff pressure, uneven cuff inflation or tube migration
- Late
  - Tracheal granulomas causing breathing difficulty after decannulation
  - Persistent sinus at the stoma
  - Tracheal dilatation
  - Tracheal stenosis at the cuff site (scarring)

## Surgical tracheostomy in an intubated patient

### Main issues

- Airway fire
- Hypoxia during the tube change

### Preoperative assessment

- Intubated patient: check fitness for tracheostomy (acceptable FiO2 and PEEP)
- Non-intubated patient: before induction have all equipment ready, including a cricothyroidotomy kit, with the ENT surgeon scrubbed

### Intraoperative management

- Supine, shoulder roll (or head dropped), head ring, head up; neck extended
- GA with ETT and IPPV
  - Change a DLT to a single-lumen tube first
  - Consider filling the cuff with saline
- Drape so the airway stays accessible
- Lowest safe FiO2, ideally below 0.3
  - Avoid nitrous oxide (supports combustion)
  - Some preoxygenate with FiO2 1.0, but this carries a high airway fire risk
- Loosen the ETT ties
- Preoxygenate
- When the surgeon is ready: deflate the cuff and withdraw the ETT slowly until the cuff sits just below the cords (the surgeon says when to stop)
  - Surgeon opens the trachea with a scalpel (not diathermy), inserts the tracheostomy tube and inflates the cuff
  - Ventilation may be paused at this point
- Alternative: advance the ETT before the trachea is opened, then withdraw it
  - Puts the cuff beyond the stoma so it is not cut; going endobronchial does not matter
  - Ventilation continues while the stoma is made, so the surgeon is not rushed
  - Useful in unstable patients with poor oxygenation or high PEEP, and with difficult anatomy
  - Once the stoma is made and the tracheostomy tube is ready, pull the ETT back above the stoma
- Connect the tracheostomy to the circuit via a sterile catheter mount
- Confirm position
  - Capnography: confirms tracheal placement but not correct position
  - Airway pressures; chest rise and auscultation for endobronchial placement (hard in practice unless you get behind the surgeon)
  - Cuff pressure and leak: no leak at normal cuff pressures (20–30 cmH2O); needing more suggests a malpositioned or herniated cuff
- Reduce FiO2 again so the surgeon can achieve haemostasis
- With or without fibreoptic check
  - Through the tracheostomy tube: tip position relative to the carina, not abutting the tracheal wall
  - From above via the larynx (e.g. through the partly withdrawn ETT): whole cuff through the anterior tracheal wall and sitting correctly
  - Keep the neck neutral during endoscopy (the position the tube normally sits in)
- If there is a problem: remove the tracheostomy tube and advance the ETT down the trachea
- After position is confirmed, the surgeon secures haemostasis and sutures the tube in place

### Postoperative management

- CXR
- Persistent coughing sometimes: morphine, benzodiazepines or low-dose propofol
- Humidified gases
- Analgesia

## Tracheostomy under local anaesthesia for severe airway obstruction

- Covered under partially obstructed airway management; awake fibreoptic intubation is the alternative

## Patient with a previous tracheostomy

- Difficult airway
  - Underlying pathology that needed the tracheostomy
  - Fibrosis and scarring tether the trachea, limit neck extension and make rescue front-of-neck access (FONA) harder
- Complications: check preoperative CT or bronchoscopy; consider awake fibreoptic intubation
  - Subglottic or tracheal stenosis (cuff ischaemia, stoma granulation, chondromalacia): the ETT may not pass even with a Grade 1 view; have smaller tubes ready
  - Tracheomalacia: dynamic airway collapse, especially at induction or after extubation
- False passage: old stoma tracts can form blind pockets causing ETT malposition or surgical emphysema; bronchoscope on standby
- Granulation tissue at the old stoma may bleed

## Flashcards

- Q: Target FiO2 during surgical tracheostomy, and why?
  - A: Lowest safe FiO2, ideally below 0.3, to reduce airway fire risk; avoid nitrous oxide
- Q: Normal cuff pressure to check for a leak after tracheostomy insertion?
  - A: 20–30 cmH2O; needing more suggests a malpositioned or herniated cuff
- Q: Mortality and complication rates of surgical tracheostomy?
  - A: Mortality below 1%; complications up to 40%
- Q: What should the surgeon use to open the trachea?
  - A: A scalpel, not diathermy
- Q: Does capnography confirm correct tracheostomy tube position?
  - A: No; it confirms tracheal placement only
