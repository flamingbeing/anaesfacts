# Anaesthesia for Diagnostic Radiology

Remote-site work with radiation, bulky equipment and poor access to the patient and airway.

## Key issues
- Remote location
- Ionising radiation
- Poor access to the patient
- Demand for anaesthesia in scanning and interventional radiology is growing

## Radiation safety
- Shielding for staff and patient (cover the genitals in children)
- Keep total exposure time short
- Staff keep distance from the source (inverse square law)

## Equipment
- Large specialised radiology kit; C-arms or O-arms may need to move
- MRI is the hardest setting: most standard pumps, laryngoscope blades, stethoscopes, ventilators and monitors are not magnet-safe (see MRI anaesthesia)

## Access to the patient
- Patient is in another room and inside the scanner
- Use line extensions
- Airway:
  - Main risk with sedation is respiratory depression in the scanner with an unprotected airway
  - Moderate sedation can slide into unplanned GA
  - Have a clear airway rescue plan
  - Use continuous capnography for sedation; avoid deep sedation where possible
  - If heavy sedation is needed, GA with an LMA or ETT may be safer

## Remote site factors
- Limited anaesthetic equipment: TIVA may be needed
- Get to know the staff, resources and equipment before the patient goes into the scanner

## Sedation for CT
- Non-anaesthetists usually give IV midazolam

## Flashcards
- Q: What monitoring is essential when sedating a patient in a scanner?
  - A: Continuous capnography
- Q: Three ways to cut radiation exposure for staff?
  - A: Shielding, less time, more distance (inverse square law)
- Q: When should GA be chosen over sedation in the scanner?
  - A: When the patient needs heavy sedation; use GA with an LMA or ETT
