# Phaeochromocytoma Surgery

Removal of one or both adrenals, or an extra-adrenal tumour: alpha then beta block, avoid catecholamine release, expect hypertension on handling and hypotension after vein ligation.

## Summary
- Preoperative: alpha block (phenoxybenzamine) first, then beta block; check adequacy with Roizen criteria; correct dehydration; TTE
- Before induction: arterial line, CVP, FloTrac, vasopressors and vasodilators ready
- Useful drugs: remifentanil, dexmedetomidine, magnesium
- Avoid catecholamine release:
  - Sympathetic stimulation, e.g. laryngoscopy
  - Indirect agents, e.g. ephedrine
  - Histamine release, e.g. atracurium, morphine
- Phentolamine 1mg bolus for hypertensive surges
- Tumour handling causes hypertension; ligation of the adrenal vein causes hypotension
- Management is similar to carcinoid syndrome and tumours
- Steroids for bilateral adrenalectomy

## Condition
- Tumour of chromaffin cells
- Secretes catecholamines, from most to least common: noradrenaline, adrenaline, dopamine
- 95% in abdomen or pelvis (usually the adrenals); 10% bilateral
- Most are benign; less common in children
- Can be part of MEN2A (thyroid cancer) or MEN2B (thyroid cancer with marfanoid features), from a chromosome 10 defect
  - So with thyroid disease always consider phaeochromocytoma
- Also seen in neurofibromatosis and von Hippel-Lindau syndrome

## Diagnosis
- Biochemical confirmation: metanephrine and normetanephrine are breakdown products of adrenaline and noradrenaline
  - 24hr urine fractionated metanephrines and catecholamines
    - Sensitivity 98 percent, specificity 98 percent
    - Measure urinary creatinine too (confirms an adequate collection) and 3-methoxytyramine (picks up dopamine-only secretion)
  - Plasma fractionated metanephrines
    - Sensitivity high, slightly above the urine test; a negative result virtually excludes the tumour
    - Easier to collect
    - Specificity poor (85 to 89 percent)
  - If normal but spells continue: repeat during a spell
- Imaging to locate the tumour
  - MRI or CT abdomen and pelvis: adrenals or a para-aortic mass
  - Consider 123I-MIBG if mass over 10cm, to look for metastases or multiple tumours
- If the adrenals look normal:
  - Whole-body MRI
  - 123I-MIBG radioisotope scan
  - Indium-111 pentetreotide scan
  - PET scan
- Once found:
  - Genetic testing: 40% familial
  - Alpha blockade first, then beta blockade
  - Surgical resection

## Surgical approach
- No single intraoperative technique has been shown to be better
- Increasingly laparoscopic: faster recovery, but haemodynamic instability is no less
- Large or invasive adrenal masses and most paragangliomas usually need open surgery
- Laparoscopic routes: transabdominal or retroperitoneal, both in the lateral position
  - Retroperitoneal: needs a marked table break to open up the small working space for port placement
  - Transabdominal: now preferred (familiar anatomy, faster access to the adrenal vein); table break not needed but may still be asked for out of habit

## Preoperative assessment
- Goals of optimisation:
  - Control arterial pressure, heart rate and arrhythmias
  - Reverse chronic circulating volume depletion
  - Assess and optimise myocardial function
  - Correct glucose and electrolyte disturbances
- History: palpitations, sweating, headache, hypertension, anxiety, nausea and vomiting, weakness and lethargy, MI, acute pulmonary oedema, stroke
  - If present perioperatively, mortality can reach 50%
- Examination: mass, hypertension
- Investigations:
  - Plasma and urinary catecholamines and metanephrines
  - Imaging: CT, MIBG radioisotope scan (taken up by chromaffin tissue), MRI; search the abdomen first, then widen
  - Echo: catecholamine cardiomyopathy is rare but possible
  - Blood glucose

## Blood pressure control: alpha blockade
- Key to reducing noradrenaline effects and intraoperative hypertensive crisis, and to restoring blood volume
  - Without it: severe intraoperative hypertension, stroke, arrhythmias, myocardial ischaemia, LV failure, refractory hypotension after resection
- Start at least 7–14 days before surgery; longer for cardiomyopathy or resistant hypertension
- Phenoxybenzamine: non-selective, non-competitive, long-acting
  - Non-competitive block may blunt catecholamine surges but is linked to catecholamine-resistant hypotension after resection
  - So stop 24–48 h before surgery because of its long half-life
  - Also blocks presynaptic alpha-2 receptors: removes noradrenaline negative feedback, so more noradrenaline from cardiac sympathetic nerves leads to reflex tachycardia via beta-1
  - Central alpha-2 block: somnolence, headache, nasal congestion
- Selective alpha-1 blockers, e.g. doxazosin: competitive
  - Do not inhibit presynaptic noradrenaline re-uptake, so avoid the tachycardia seen with non-selective block
  - Some studies show less postoperative hypotension
- Calcium channel blockers, e.g. nicardipine
  - Block noradrenaline-driven calcium entry into smooth muscle; no effect on tumour noradrenaline release
  - Not for monotherapy, except very mild hypertension or severe orthostatic hypotension on alpha blockers
  - Mainly an add-on for patients already alpha blocked
- Check adequacy of alpha and sympathetic block, ideally with ambulatory BP monitoring
  - Roizen criteria (1982):
    - BP below 160/90 for 2 days before surgery
    - Mild orthostatic hypotension, but standing BP above 80/45 mmHg
    - ECG free of ST-T changes and PVCs for 2 weeks
  - Limits of Roizen criteria:
    - Current targets are tighter: seated BP below 130/80 mm Hg
    - Orthostatic hypotension is not required
    - ST or T wave changes may be inverted Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, not ischaemia

## Heart rate and arrhythmia control
- Tachyarrhythmias come from adrenaline- or dopamine-secreting tumours, or follow alpha blockade
- Selective beta-1 blockers preferred, e.g. atenolol, metoprolol
- Start beta blockers only after complete alpha blockade
  - Otherwise loss of beta-2 vasodilation leaves alpha vasoconstriction unopposed, risking hypertensive crisis
  - Negative inotropy of beta block further depresses myocardial function

## Volume, myocardial function and metabolic correction
- Circulating volume should recover on its own once alpha blockade is adequate
  - High sodium diet and fluid intake help
  - Monitor with serial haematocrits
- ECG: LVH, tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischaemia
- TTE (consider for all): looking for cardiomyopathy
  - Most have some diastolic dysfunction; about 10% have LV systolic dysfunction
  - Function may improve once catecholamine levels normalise
- Hyperglycaemia can occur; treat as usual
  - Mechanisms: more glycogenolysis (alpha-1), less insulin release (alpha-2), lipolysis (beta-1), more glucagon and peripheral insulin resistance (beta-2)
- Electrolytes: pick up catecholamine-induced renal impairment
- Hypercalcaemia: suggests an associated parathyroid adenoma, e.g. MEN 2A

## Risk factors for intraoperative haemodynamic instability
- High plasma noradrenaline before induction
- Large tumour
- Marked postural drop after starting alpha blockade
- MAP above 100 mm Hg before induction

## Intraoperative management
- Expect and manage haemodynamic instability
  - Arterial line, CVP, vasopressors and vasodilators
    - Phentolamine, esmolol
    - GTN, SNP
    - Phenylephrine
    - Vasopressin (works for catecholamine-resistant vasoplegia), noradrenaline
  - Hypertension during tumour handling
  - Hypotension after tumour devascularisation
  - Watch for hypoglycaemia
- Avoid catecholamine release
  - Anaesthetic and surgical triggers: laryngoscopy, pneumoperitoneum, coughing
  - Drug triggers:
    - Indirect sympathomimetics, e.g. ephedrine
    - Histamine releasers, e.g. atracurium (use remifentanil and rocuronium instead)
    - Sympathetic stimulants: ketamine, desflurane
    - Metoclopramide
    - Suxamethonium: fasciculations can trigger a hypertensive crisis
  - Dexmedetomidine and magnesium are useful
- Bilateral adrenalectomy: hydrocortisone

## Critical periods
- Induction and intubation: haemodynamic instability
- Abdominal insufflation: hypertension
- Tumour manipulation: hypertension
- Venous ligation: BP falls, blood glucose falls

## Technique
- GA
  - Remifentanil: blunts responses to intubation and pain very well, but alone does not prevent hypertension from tumour handling
  - Dexmedetomidine: central sympatholysis markedly lowers plasma noradrenaline
- Epidural can help blunt sympathetic stimulation
- Lines and monitoring:
  - Arterial line before induction
  - CVP usually after induction
  - Large-bore IV access: to treat volume depletion; bleeding risk higher on the right side from venous anatomy
  - Vasopressors and vasodilators ready
  - FloTrac for cardiac output monitoring
- Induction: instability likely; remifentanil, noradrenaline

## Managing hypertensive episodes
- Phentolamine 1mg boluses
  - Reversible non-selective alpha blocker; short-acting; may show tachyphylaxis
  - Good for surges while other infusions are being titrated
- Magnesium
  - Reduces adrenal catecholamine release and alpha receptor sensitivity
  - Mainly arteriolar dilator: lowers LV afterload, keeps preload; antiarrhythmic
  - Prophylaxis: 2-4g before induction, then 1-2g/hr infusion
  - Treatment: 2g bolus as needed
- Dexmedetomidine
- Titrate remifentanil
- SNP (very titratable), GTN
- Labetalol if tachycardia as well
- Nicardipine
- Esmolol

## Venous ligation and tumour removal
- BP falls over several minutes
- Give a fluid preload
- Low MAP may be from:
  - Low cardiac output: low-dose adrenaline
  - Low SVR: phenylephrine, or vasopressin if needed
- Usually resolves before the end of surgery unless other problems
- End of surgery: ensure full reversal if magnesium given

## Postoperative management
- HDU or ICU
  - Haemodynamic monitoring with arterial line for at least 24hrs
  - Most can be extubated
- Monitor glucose: risk of severe hypoglycaemia
  - Rebound hyperinsulinism once noradrenaline no longer suppresses insulin release
  - Beta blockade may mask the signs; check glucose regularly and titrate dextrose
- Both adrenals removed: hydrocortisone 100mg in theatre, continue then wean

## Unexpected phaeochromocytoma during surgery
- Suspect with severe unexpected hypertension (by far the commonest sign), tachyarrhythmia, or unexplained acute pulmonary oedema
- If possible, abandon surgery to allow blockade
- Otherwise manage on the same principles as elective cases
- Hypertension:
  - Vasodilate: GTN
  - Give fluid, even with pulmonary oedema, as circulating volume is often very low
  - Phentolamine
  - Labetalol
- Low MAP: acute heart failure from profound vasoconstriction
  - Very hard to diagnose; very high mortality
  - Try to avoid catecholamines; cautious treatment as for hypertension

## Pregnancy
- Phenoxybenzamine and metoprolol are safe
- Diagnosed before mid-trimester: resect
- Diagnosed later: elective LSCS, with or without resection at the same time

## Flashcards
- Q: How long before surgery should alpha blockade start, and when is phenoxybenzamine stopped?
  - A: At least 7–14 days before; stop phenoxybenzamine 24–48 h before surgery
- Q: What are the Roizen criteria?
  - A: BP below 160/90 for 2 days; standing BP above 80/45 mmHg; ECG free of ST-T changes and PVCs for 2 weeks
- Q: Magnesium dosing for phaeochromocytoma surgery?
  - A: 2-4g before induction, then 1-2g/hr; 2g bolus for surges
- Q: Why start beta blockers only after full alpha blockade?
  - A: Loss of beta-2 vasodilation leaves alpha vasoconstriction unopposed and risks hypertensive crisis
- Q: Key metabolic risk after tumour removal?
  - A: Severe hypoglycaemia from rebound hyperinsulinism; may be masked by beta blockade
