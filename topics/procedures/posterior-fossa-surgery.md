# Posterior Fossa Surgery

Long neurosurgery near the brainstem: control ICP and CPP, manage awkward positions and air embolism, and watch the airway after surgery.

## Anatomy
- Boundaries
  - Superior: tentorium cerebelli and tentorial incisura (opening to the supratentorial space)
  - Inferior: occipital bone and foramen magnum (opening to the spinal canal)
  - Anterior: dorsum sellae and basilar occipital bone (clivus)
  - Posterior: occipital bone
  - Lateral: petrous and mastoid temporal bone
- Contents
  - Cerebellum
  - Brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla): main motor and sensory tracts, lower cranial nerve nuclei, cardiorespiratory centres
  - CSF outflow from the ventricles, including the cerebral aqueduct
  - Vessels and venous sinuses
- Raised posterior fossa pressure leads to
  - Obstructive hydrocephalus
  - Reduced consciousness
  - Hypertension and bradycardia
  - Respiratory depression
  - Loss of protective airway reflexes

## Operations
- Tumour excision or debulking
  - Glioma (often from the 4th ventricle)
  - Medulloblastoma (often from the cerebellar vermis, mostly children)
  - Acoustic neuroma (often from CN8 at the cerebellopontine angle, usually benign)
  - Meningioma
  - Metastases
- Vascular
  - Haemangioblastoma
  - Aneurysms of the superior cerebellar, posterior inferior cerebellar and vertebral arteries
- Foramen magnum decompression for Arnold-Chiari malformation

## Key considerations
- Patient
  - Raised ICP: lower GCS, Cushing's reflex (hypertension, bradycardia), bulbar palsy with weak airway reflexes and aspiration risk, respiratory depression
  - Effects of metastases
  - Cardiac or respiratory disease may worsen when the lesion affects cardiorespiratory centres and nerves
- Surgical
  - Long operation: careful fluids, normothermia (temperature probe, warmed fluids, forced air and underbody warmers), thromboprophylaxis (TED stockings, pneumatic compression), pad pressure areas, avoid nerve traction
  - Position: sitting, lateral or prone
  - Slack brain for access: smooth induction, adequate depth, smooth emergence
  - Lumbar CSF drain may be used: better surgical conditions but more postoperative CSF leak
- Anaesthetic
  - Limited access to the airway
  - Prevent ICP rises and keep CPP: normotension, avoid hypoxia, hypercarbia, hypothermia and acidosis, avoid hypotonic fluids
  - Avoid extreme neck flexion or rotation, which hinders cerebral venous drainage
- General neuro points: BP control at intubation and pinning, keep CPP, mannitol may be needed for ICP, seizure prophylaxis, bleeding risk (group and crossmatch with blood on standby, large-bore IV)

## Surgical complications
- Brainstem handling: haemodynamic instability (bradycardia, cardiac arrest), respiratory depression, cranial nerve palsy (4.8%)
  - Tell the surgeon at once to stop retraction
- Cerebellar handling: cerebellar mutism
- Obstructive hydrocephalus (usually at the 4th ventricle or cerebral aqueduct)
- Pneumocephalus: air trapped in the subarachnoid space expands after dural closure, causing delayed waking and persistent deficits
- Venous air embolism
- CSF leak (13%)
- Bleeding
- Meningitis (9.2%), wound infection (7%)
- Death (2.6%)

## Positioning
- Options: supine, prone, sitting, lateral, park bench
- Prone: good access to midline structures
- Sitting: now rare because of high risk of venous air embolism and pneumocephalus
  - Improves access as gravity drains blood and CSF and lowers ICP
  - Posterior fossa surgery is a common reason for the sitting position
- Lateral: gravity drains blood and CSF; good for one-sided procedures
- Park bench: better midline access; in selected patients avoids prone
- Acoustic neuroma and cerebellopontine angle tumours: may be done supine, head turned away, sandbag under the same-side shoulder

## Preoperative assessment
- History
  - Raised ICP from hydrocephalus or the lesion: may need an EVD first
  - Lower GCS
  - Weak airway reflexes or bulbar palsy: silent aspiration
  - Respiratory depression from reduced drive or vagal dysfunction
  - Dehydration and electrolyte upset from vomiting or diuretics: hypotension at induction or on sitting
  - Hyperglycaemia from steroids
  - Cardiac and respiratory disease
- Examination
  - Vital signs: bradycardia and hypertension of Cushing's reflex
  - Airway, including gag reflex
  - Existing neurological deficits
- Investigations
  - FBC, PT/PTT, group and crossmatch
  - Urea, electrolytes and creatinine: hyponatraemia or hyperkalaemia from vomiting or diuretics, hyperglycaemia from steroids
  - ECG, CXR
  - CT or MRI: size, site and vascularity set the length and difficulty of surgery
- Premedication: avoid sedatives
- Counselling: fasting, anaesthetic plan, invasive lines, possible HD or ICU and ventilation after surgery, reassurance

## Intraoperative management
- Monitoring
  - Standard ASA: ECG, NIBP, SpO2, ETCO2, end-tidal agent
  - Arterial line; central line for CVP, vasopressors or inotropes and aspiration of air in venous air embolism
  - Neurophysiology: SSEP, BAEP, EMG
  - Venous air embolism: praecordial Doppler ultrasound
  - NGT if postoperative bulbar dysfunction is likely, urinary catheter, temperature probe
- Thromboprophylaxis: TED stockings, pneumatic compression devices
- Induction (GA with IPPV)
  - Smooth, avoiding sudden BP or CPP changes
  - Thiopentone 3-5mg/kg IV or propofol 2-3mg/kg IV, given slowly
  - Blunt the pressor response to intubation: lignocaine 1.5mg/kg IV; fentanyl 5mcg/kg or remifentanil 0.2-0.5 mcg/kg/min; labetalol 5-10mg IV or esmolol 10-20mg IV
  - Armoured ETT to prevent kinking; secure with tape, as ties may obstruct venous return
- Maintenance
  - TIVA/TCI propofol (3-6 mcg/ml) with remifentanil, or sevoflurane/isoflurane < 1 MAC
  - Avoid N2O: raises CMRO2 and CBF, enlarges venous air emboli and residual intracranial air (postoperative pneumocephalus)
  - Avoid neuromuscular blockers if neurophysiological monitoring is used
    - Remifentanil 0.15-0.25 mcg/kg/min avoids the need for them
    - Alternatives: fentanyl 25-50 mcg boluses, alfentanil 25-50 mcg/kg/h
  - Pressor response to the Mayfield 3-point pin holder: local anaesthetic at pin sites, propofol 0.5-1mg/kg, fentanyl 25-50mcg or remifentanil 0.5-1 mcg/kg
  - Bradycardia or hypertension: tell the surgeon; gentler retraction or dissection usually fixes it
    - Atropine or beta-blockers can mask later BP and HR changes
  - Lumbar CSF drain if needed: better surgical conditions, fewer postoperative CSF leaks
  - May need neuromonitoring
- Fluids
  - Restrict to 30ml/kg/day if cerebral oedema is present
  - Avoid glucose-containing or hypotonic fluids: hyperglycaemia worsens outcome after brain injury; low plasma osmolality increases cerebral oedema
  - Replace blood loss with colloid or blood
- Ventilation: mild hypocapnia in tumour surgery (PaCO2 30-34mmHg)
- Seizure prophylaxis: phenytoin as needed, load 15mg/kg then 3-4mg/kg once each morning
- Emergence
  - Most patients can be extubated
  - Smooth extubation; labetalol 5-10mg IV or esmolol 10-20mg IV for the pressor response; avoid coughing

## Postoperative care
- HD or ICU monitoring
- Keep intubated to ICU if: poor preoperative GCS, extensive resection, marked cerebral oedema, intraoperative problems (bleeding, nerve injury)
- Watch conscious level and neurology
- Watch for airway obstruction from
  - Macroglossia
  - Vagus nerve injury impairing diaphragm function
  - Medullary injury reducing respiratory drive
  - Excessive cervical flexion: airway and facial oedema, quadriplegia
- Analgesia
  - Morphine during dural, bone flap and scalp closure; may need more in PACU
  - Ward: paracetamol 1g and codeine phosphate 60-90mg for mild to moderate post-craniotomy pain
  - NSAIDs with caution: reduce opioid use but prolong bleeding time (risk of intracranial haematoma) and risk renal injury in hypovolaemic patients with vomiting or diuretics

## Flashcards
- Q: Why avoid N2O in posterior fossa surgery?
  - A: Raises CMRO2 and CBF, and enlarges venous air emboli and residual intracranial air (pneumocephalus)
- Q: What to do for sudden bradycardia during brainstem manipulation?
  - A: Tell the surgeon at once to stop retraction; avoid reflex atropine or beta-blockers that mask further changes
- Q: Remifentanil rate that avoids neuromuscular block during neuromonitoring?
  - A: 0.15-0.25 mcg/kg/min
- Q: Phenytoin loading dose for seizure prophylaxis?
  - A: 15mg/kg, then 3-4mg/kg once daily
- Q: Fluid restriction if cerebral oedema is present?
  - A: 30ml/kg/day, avoiding glucose-containing and hypotonic fluids
