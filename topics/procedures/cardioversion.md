# Direct Current Cardioversion

R-wave synchronised shock to restore sinus rhythm; usually brief sedation or short GA, with attention to anticoagulation, electrolytes and implanted devices.

## Procedure
- **DC shock synchronised to the patient's own cardiac activity**
  - Timed to the R wave of the QRS on the ECG
  - Avoids shocking near the T-wave peak, the vulnerable period when VF can be triggered (end of refractoriness, with neighbouring fibres at different stages of repolarisation)
  - Defibrillation, by contrast, is an unsynchronised shock for VF
- A brief shock (< 4ms) depolarises cardiac cells at once, letting the sinus node take over again
- In re-entrant arrhythmias (e.g. PSVT, VT) it breaks the circuit and restores sinus rhythm

## Contraindications (Class III, LOE C)
- Digitalis toxicity with hypokalaemia
  - Digoxin sensitises the heart, so shocks can trigger further arrhythmias, especially VT or VF
  - Supraventricular arrhythmias:
    - Unstable: lowest energy likely to work; consider rapid atrial pacing for flutter
    - Stable: correct digoxin toxicity and normalise levels first
  - Ventricular arrhythmias:
    - Unstable: prophylactic lignocaine 1mg/kg (max 100mg) before the shock; lowest energy likely to work
- Repeated DCC is not advised when AF keeps relapsing after short spells of sinus rhythm despite several attempts and antiarrhythmic prophylaxis

## Factors affecting success (75-95%)
- Electrodes: internal electrodes do better than external chest wall ones in obesity and obstructive lung disease
- Current density (set by capacitor voltage, waveform, pad size and position, thoracic impedance):
  - Waveform: biphasic (gold standard) beats monophasic
    - Higher success (94% vs 84%)
    - Fewer shocks, about 50% less energy
    - Shorter procedure, less sedation
    - Fewer skin burns and less muscle damage, fewer post-shock arrhythmias, shorter myocardial stunning
  - Higher energy is more successful
  - Best pad diameter roughly matches the cross-sectional area of the heart
  - Anterior-posterior (sternum and left scapula) beats anterior-lateral (apex and right infraclavicular)
  - Thoracic impedance: lung impedes current, so shocking in expiration or with chest pressure delivers more energy to the heart
- Heart disease:
  - Longer AF duration lowers success
  - Larger left atrium lowers success
- Drug enhancement:
  - Pretreatment with flecainide, propafenone, ibutilide, amiodarone or sotalol can improve success and prevent AF recurrence (Class IIa, LOE B)
  - If AF relapses after successful DCC, consider repeating it after antiarrhythmic prophylaxis (Class IIa, LOE C)
  - Out-of-hospital antiarrhythmics may be considered once shown safe for that patient (Class IIb, LOE C)

## Complications
- AF recurrence
  - Most within 1mth
  - Higher risk if AF > 1y or underlying heart disease
- Thromboembolism
  - 1-2% with prophylactic anticoagulation
  - 2-5% without anticoagulation before cardioversion
  - Higher risk if AF > 48h
- Arrhythmias
  - Ectopics, bradycardia and sinus arrest usually settle on their own
  - VT or VF with hypokalaemia, digitalis toxicity or poor synchronisation
- Myocardial damage or dysfunction
  - Transient hypotension
  - Pulmonary oedema: rare, from transient left atrial standstill or LV dysfunction
- Skin burns (20-25%)

## Compared with drug cardioversion
- Higher success (biphasic DCC)
- Shorter procedure
- Less proarrhythmia
- Needs conscious sedation or anaesthesia

## Preoperative assessment
- Emergency or elective?
  - Emergency: need for anaesthesia depends on the situation and conscious level; conscious sedation in urgent cases
  - Elective: conscious sedation or short-acting GA
- Acute or chronic AF?
- Thromboembolic risk and anticoagulation:
  - Conventional: warfarin for 3wks, synchronised DCC, then warfarin for 4wks
  - TEE-guided:
    - No thrombus: synchronised DCC, then warfarin for 4wks
    - Thrombus: warfarin for 4wks, repeat TEE; if thrombus persists, another 4wks of warfarin
  - Haemodynamically unstable:
    - AF < 48h: cardiovert at once without waiting for anticoagulation (Class I, LOE C)
    - AF > 48h or unknown: cardiovert at once with heparin unless contraindicated (Class I, LOE C)
      - Heparin bolus (5000IU or 80IU/kg), then infusion (usually 16-18IU/kg/h by ideal body weight) to aPTT 1.5-2x control
      - Continue heparin until warfarin reaches INR 2-3
      - Warfarin for at least 4wks after cardioversion, INR 2-3; alternatives dabigatran, rivaroxaban
      - Little evidence for SC enoxaparin (Clexane) here
  - Haemodynamically stable:
    - AF < 48h: anticoagulation before and after depends on thromboembolic risk (Class IIa, LOE C)
      - Low stroke risk: UFH, LMWH, NOAC, or no antithrombotic
      - High stroke risk: UFH, LMWH, or factor Xa or direct thrombin inhibitors
    - AF > 48h or unknown: warfarin for at least 3wks before and 4wks after electrical or drug cardioversion, INR 2-3 (Class I, LOE B)
  - TEE to exclude left atrial or appendage thrombus is a reasonable alternative to prior anticoagulation (Class IIa, LOE B)
    - TEE under propofol sedation if anticoagulation is contraindicated
    - No thrombus:
      - Cardiovert straight after heparin
      - Heparin bolus (5000IU or 80IU/kg), then infusion (usually 16-18IU/kg/h by ideal body weight) to aPTT 1.5-2x control
      - Continue heparin until warfarin reaches INR 2-3
      - Warfarin for at least 4wks after, INR 2-3
      - Little evidence for SC enoxaparin (Clexane) here
    - Thrombus seen:
      - Warfarin for at least 3wks before and 4wks after cardioversion, INR 2-3 (Class IIa, LOE C)
      - Longer anticoagulation may be needed even after success, as risk stays high (Class IIa, LOE C)
- Arrhythmia risk:
  - Ventricular rate < 60bpm suggests a conduction defect: consider prophylactic transvenous or transcutaneous pacing, especially if sick sinus syndrome suspected
  - Optimise electrolytes, especially potassium
  - Check thyroid function
  - Prophylactic antiarrhythmics (flecainide, propafenone, ibutilide, amiodarone, sotalol) improve success and maintenance of sinus rhythm
  - Continue digoxin unless toxic
- Pacemaker or ICD:
  - Devices are shielded against external shocks, but programming can be altered by surges
  - Current along the lead can injure endocardium and raise the pacing threshold, causing loss of ventricular capture
  - Interrogate the device before and after
  - Place pads as far from the device as possible (at least 15cm), ideally anterior-posterior
- Standard GA assessment: airway, CNS, fasting

## Intraoperative management
- Standard ASA monitoring (NIBP, SpO2, continuous ECG, ETCO2, end-tidal agent)
  - Shock synchronised to the R wave
  - Pick an ECG lead that shows atrial activity
- Pads or paddles anterior-lateral or anterior-posterior with conductive gel
  - Even ideally, only 10-30% of the current reaches the heart
- Initial energy (up to max 360J) per AHA ACLS Guidelines 2010:
  - AF: biphasic 100-200J, monophasic 200J
  - Atrial flutter: biphasic 50-100J, monophasic 100J
  - VT with pulse: biphasic 100J, monophasic 200J
  - Pulseless VT or VF: biphasic 120-200J, monophasic 360J
- Location (remote anaesthesia):
  - Emergency DCC usually in ED
  - Elective DCC may be in the cardiac lab; move to theatre if possible
- Technique:
  - Conscious sedation, or
  - Short-acting day-case GA:
    - Adequate fasting and preoxygenation
    - Slow titrated induction: propofol, midazolam; consider etomidate if unstable
    - Airway by facemask or LMA; RSI and ETT if aspiration risk
    - Drugs allowing rapid recovery
    - Opioids and muscle relaxants not needed
    - Obese or difficult-airway patients can be shocked in the lateral position
- Safety: follow ACLS protocol; remove oxygen during the shock
- Arrhythmias after DCC:
  - Commonly ectopics, bradycardia, transient sinus arrest
  - VT or VF with hypokalaemia or digitalis toxicity
  - Severe bradycardia, complete heart block or asystole with underlying conduction disease (e.g. slow ventricular response in AF without rate-control drugs)
- Ischaemic changes: transient ST elevation with raised CK-MB or troponin can occur without real cardiac damage

## Postoperative management
- Recovery position
- Supplemental oxygen
- Monitor haemodynamics and ECG in recovery

## Flashcards
- Q: Why is cardioversion synchronised to the R wave?
  - A: To avoid shocking near the T-wave peak, the vulnerable period for VF
- Q: Initial biphasic energy for AF and for atrial flutter (AHA 2010)?
  - A: AF 100-200J; flutter 50-100J
- Q: How long is warfarin needed around elective DCC for AF > 48h?
  - A: At least 3wks before and 4wks after, INR 2-3
- Q: How far should pads be from a pacemaker or ICD?
  - A: At least 15cm, ideally anterior-posterior
- Q: What prophylaxis is given before shocking an unstable ventricular arrhythmia with digoxin toxicity?
  - A: Lignocaine 1mg/kg (max 100mg), with the lowest effective energy
