# Paediatric Induction of Anaesthesia

Choosing and conducting IV or inhalational induction in children.

## Choosing the method
- Explain the 2 methods to the parent and ask for any preference
- Inhalational if:
  - Needle phobia or uncooperative child
  - Spontaneous ventilation needed for a difficult airway
- Intravenous if:
  - URTI or asthma
  - Aspiration risk
  - OSA

## Intravenous induction
- Cannula already in place: test that it is patent
- No cannula: use nitrous oxide for cannulation if the child agrees
- Propofol injection pain: consider adding lignocaine, diluting with normal saline, and/or giving small aliquots instead of one bolus

## Inhalational induction: when used
- A core paediatric technique, used often
- Clinically indicated: suspected epiglottitis or another potentially difficult airway
- Requested by child or parents, especially with repeat procedures
- Anaesthetist preference: poor venous access (e.g. chubby toddler, ex-premature child with many past cannulae)
- Site an IV cannula after induction, ideally before airway instrumentation or surgery

## Inhalational induction: agents
- Usually sevoflurane in oxygen; nitrous oxide often added for the second gas effect
- Many prefer oxygen and volatile alone so the child is well oxygenated if the airway obstructs, especially when problems are expected
- Halothane: used less than sevoflurane but more potent; often chosen for an anticipated difficult airway
- Sevoflurane is not irritant, so a high concentration can be given quickly or from the start
- Halothane must be stepped up gradually to avoid distress (rule of thumb: 0.5% every 4 breaths)

## Inhalational induction: conduct
- Most children accept nitrous oxide first, then volatile added gradually
- Child may hold the mask, or the circuit is held in a cupped hand near the face; apply the mask and gentle chin lift as the child gets drowsy
- Scented masks or lip balm on the mask: benefit is mainly ritual and the child's sense of control rather than hiding the smell
- Be patient: stimulation at too light a plane can cause breath holding, coughing, laryngospasm and obstruction
  - Avoid early oral airways
  - Gentle chin lift and jaw thrust
- Once deep enough, reduce the inspired volatile concentration (cardiovascular, and to a lesser degree respiratory, depression)
- Avoid "bagging" with a high concentration: this removes the built-in safety of gas induction (overdose causes hypoventilation) and leads to cardiovascular depression

## Cautions
- May be unsuitable with limited cardiovascular reserve: severe hypovolaemia, cardiac failure, right to left shunt, fixed cardiac output
  - Myocardial depression and vasodilatation can cause profound hypoperfusion and cardiac arrest
- Pollutes the anaesthetic room; staff report more fatigue after several in one day
- A prolonged difficult gas induction is often remembered by children as very unpleasant

## Flashcards
- Q: Name three reasons to prefer IV over inhalational induction in a child.
  - A: URTI or asthma, aspiration risk, OSA.
- Q: Rule of thumb for increasing halothane during gas induction?
  - A: 0.5% every 4 breaths.
- Q: Why avoid bagging a child with a high volatile concentration at induction?
  - A: It bypasses the safety of hypoventilation with overdose and causes cardiovascular depression.
- Q: Which children may not tolerate an inhalational induction?
  - A: Limited cardiovascular reserve: severe hypovolaemia, cardiac failure, right to left shunt, fixed cardiac output.
