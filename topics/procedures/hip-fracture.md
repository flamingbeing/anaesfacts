# Hip Fracture

Frail elderly patients with high mortality: operate early, optimise only what can be fixed quickly, keep haemodynamics stable.

## Background
- High morbidity and mortality; predicted mortality similar to emergency laparotomy

## Preoperative assessment
- Thorough assessment with a multidisciplinary team: surgeons, orthogeriatricians, anaesthetists
- Confirm injury and planned operation: fixation or femoral head replacement, chosen by fracture type, surgeon preference, prior mobility and life expectancy
- Fluid resuscitation
  - Blood loss may be significant: start resuscitation on admission
  - Dehydration is common from poor oral intake: start IV fluids on admission
- Find the cause of the fall
- Comorbidities: usually elderly (average age 83), reduced reserve, much comorbidity
- Discuss risks with the patient and family; document resuscitation status

## Timing of surgery
- Early repair gives analgesia, less delirium, faster mobilisation, and lower mortality and complications
- Aim for the earliest possible daytime list
- Standards
  - English BPT and Scottish Hip Fracture Audit: within 36 h of admission
  - Irish Hip Fracture Database and Fragility Fracture Network: within 48 h
  - Stricter groups: under 24 h; repair after 24 h is linked to a 9.4% relative rise in 30-day mortality
  - Delay beyond 48 h: more mortality, morbidity and longer stay
- Delay only if optimisation gives a real benefit
  - Little can be optimised while the patient is in pain and immobile
  - Fixable problems are usually quick to fix (e.g. transfusing preoperative anaemia)
- Weigh the risk of operating against non-operative care: not operating is often riskier and means weeks of painful immobility; decide as a team
- Active cardiac or chest disease may need specialist input
  - Mild chest infection will not improve in a bed-bound patient
  - Frank pneumonia with sepsis and dyspnoea may respond to fluids, antibiotics and physiotherapy
  - Try to control AF preoperatively to avoid severe hypotension
- Undiagnosed murmur: about 25% of hip fracture patients have one
  - A prompt preoperative echo is reasonable
  - Do not delay surgery waiting for echo: proceed with invasive BP monitoring and careful cardiovascular stability (lower anaesthetic doses, IV fluids, vasoactive drugs)
  - Systolic murmur: treat as at least moderate aortic stenosis
    - Arterial line, slow heart rate, keep sinus rhythm or rate-control AF, avoid large falls in SVR
    - Avoid spinal
- Correct anaemia and electrolytes while waiting for theatre

## Anaemia and transfusion
- Preoperative anaemia is common, worsened by blood loss from fracture and surgery
- Extracapsular fractures and complex surgery cause larger haemoglobin falls
- Association of Anaesthetists targets
  - Fitter, younger patients may tolerate lower haemoglobin
  - Frail patients: 9 g/dl
  - Ischaemic heart disease, or unable to mobilise on day 1 from fatigue or dizziness: 10 g/dl

## Analgesia before surgery
- Start early; pain is often severe
- Multimodal to reduce opioid needs
- Fascia iliaca block, femoral nerve block and 3-in-1 block help but are incomplete: the hip is supplied by both lumbar and sacral plexuses

## Surgery
- Fracture fixation
  - Cannulated screws: quick, small incision, little blood loss
  - Dynamic hip screw (Richards screw and plate): intermediate
  - Position: supine on a hip table
    - Groin post; table supports only the upper body; feet strapped into boots; table raised for X-ray screening
    - Check pressure points: these patients get pressure injuries easily
  - Duration: 45 min
- Bipolar hemiarthroplasty
  - Longer with more blood loss, like the femoral part of a 1° hip replacement
  - Position: lateral on an ordinary table
  - Duration: 60-90 min
- Femoral head excision (Girdlestone procedure)
  - Femoral head removed; no prosthesis
  - Duration: 30–45 min
  - Pain: significant
  - Blood loss: under 400 ml
  - Position: supine
  - Bigger incision but no prosthesis, so quicker than arthroplasty
  - Mobility is limited afterwards

## Reducing blood loss
- Loss varies; much measured loss is old haematoma, but real haemorrhage needing transfusion can occur
- Tranexamic acid: consider if at higher risk of bleeding or anaemia complications; routine in some centres
- Avoid hypothermia: monitor temperature, active warming, cover the head, wrap the chest and lower abdomen
- Operate promptly and keep surgery short

## Anaesthetic technique
- Neuraxial or GA: no convincing evidence either is better
  - Neuraxial advantages: less blood loss, lower VTE risk, less postoperative delirium
- Spinal
  - Single antiplatelet therapy, including clopidogrel, is not a contraindication
  - Dual antiplatelet therapy: spinal may suit patients unfit for GA, on risk-benefit
  - Vitamin K antagonists: spinal once INR is 1.5 or less
  - Operative side up; plain bupivacaine
  - Trend to low-dose spinal to limit hypotension, balanced against surgical time
  - ASAP standard: bupivacaine 10 mg or less; if an intrathecal opioid is used, fentanyl only
  - Sedate only when needed: any sedative is unpredictable in the elderly and adds delirium risk
- GA with opioids
  - Avoid hypotension and deliriogenic drugs where possible
  - Consider invasive monitoring; cardiostable induction
  - Glycopyrrolate rather than atropine
  - LMA is usually fine, but keep a low threshold for intubation
    - History of aspiration risk may be unavailable
    - Trauma and opioids slow gastric emptying
    - Airway access is limited by positioning
    - ETT may be better in the lateral position
- Regional block (e.g. fascia iliaca) before induction
  - Or a small IV dose of ketamine or fentanyl to cover moving and turning
- Cemented hemiarthroplasty: blood pressure, ETCO2 and heart rate may fall sharply at cement insertion; take the same precautions as for total hip replacement (bone cement implantation syndrome)

## Postoperative care
- Analgesia
  - Fracture pain falls but persists on rolling and turning
  - Multimodal, with or without a regional technique
  - Avoid NSAIDs: GI and renal complications
- Low threshold for high dependency care, given high morbidity and mortality

## Flashcards
- Q: Within what time should hip fracture repair happen under the English BPT standard?
  - A: Within 36 h of admission.
- Q: Haemoglobin targets for hip fracture patients (Association of Anaesthetists)?
  - A: 9 g/dl if frail; 10 g/dl with ischaemic heart disease or failure to mobilise on day 1.
- Q: ASAP standard for spinal bupivacaine dose in hip fracture?
  - A: 10 mg or less, with fentanyl as the only intrathecal opioid.
- Q: At what INR can a spinal be done on a vitamin K antagonist?
  - A: 1.5 or less.
- Q: Why is a fascia iliaca block incomplete for hip fracture?
  - A: The hip is supplied by both lumbar and sacral plexuses.
