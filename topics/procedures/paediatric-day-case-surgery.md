# Paediatric day case surgery

Children suit day surgery (target 50-70% of elective cases); select by patient, procedure, anaesthetic and social factors; rely on regional blocks plus paracetamol and NSAIDs; benchmark admission rate 1-2%.

## Overview
- Children are excellent day case candidates
- Recommendation: 50-70% of elective paediatric surgery done as day cases
- Needs careful selection, experienced multidisciplinary team, suitable facilities, and clear protocols for analgesia, discharge and follow-up

## Selection criteria
- Criteria should be clear and unit-specific, covering four areas: patient, procedure, anaesthetic factors, social circumstances

### Patient
- Suitable:
  - Healthy, no chronic disease
  - Stable or well-controlled systemic disease (e.g. asthma, epilepsy, malignancy, cerebral palsy)
  - Asymptomatic uncomplicated cardiac lesions (e.g. small VSD) or well children after corrective cardiac surgery, with endocarditis prophylaxis as appropriate
- Not suitable:
  - Insulin-dependent diabetes or inborn errors of metabolism (difficult perioperative glucose control)
  - Infants: term babies >1 month may be day cases for some procedures (especially examination under anaesthesia, no surgery); many units set a lower limit of 3 months
  - Ex-preterm babies <50 weeks postconceptual age: risk of postoperative apnoea, need monitoring
    - Risk may persist to 60 weeks or more, especially with chronic lung disease
- Respiratory infection:
  - Moderate or severe: postpone
  - Mild: reasonable to proceed

### Procedure
- Ideally superficial, short, with pain controlled by oral analgesics
- Ideally no body cavity entry and low risk of postoperative bleeding
- Ideally no longer than 1 hour (longer anaesthesia delays recovery and increases complications such as PONV)
- Adenotonsillectomy as a day case is debated; may become common as techniques reduce bleeding

### Anaesthetic factors
- Previous anaesthetic problems or difficult airway may exclude day surgery; depends on the child and the expertise of the anaesthetist and unit

### Social circumstances
- Family needs a telephone and transport back to hospital if required

## Facilities
- Accepted quality standards include:
  - Integrated admission plan with efficient pre-admission system
  - Specific written advice for parents
- Designated day case area and staff; not mixed with acutely ill inpatients
- Not admitted or treated alongside adults
- Child-friendly environment meeting child safety standards
- Complete key documentation before discharge for aftercare and follow-up

## Preoperative assessment
- Streamlined advance assessment improves the service
  - Structured questionnaires; nurse-led pre-admission clinic with surgeon and anaesthetist input
  - Telephone assessment: confirms attendance, identifies problems early, reinforces fasting advice
- Clear verbal and written fasting instructions; liberal clear fluids reduce distress and improve behaviour
- Sedative premedication in selected children: oral midazolam 0.5 mg/kg, max 20 mg
  - May cause postoperative drowsiness, especially after short cases

## PONV prevention
- Give an antiemetic if an opioid is used
- Give adequate fluids

## Analgesia
- Good analgesia is essential
- Local and regional techniques wherever possible: topical, infiltration, peripheral nerve blocks, caudal
  - Penile block for circumcision; ilioinguinal block for inguinal herniotomy; great auricular nerve block for otoplasty
  - Caudal: many procedures below the umbilicus
    - Dilute LA reduces leg weakness
    - Adjuncts prolong block: ketamine 0.5 mg/kg or clonidine 1 mcg/kg (clonidine may cause sedation and transient hypotension)
- Paracetamol and NSAIDs improve analgesia
  - Paracetamol: oral load 20 mg/kg preoperatively, or rectal 30-40 mg/kg after induction
  - IV paracetamol 15 mg/kg may be better than rectal (erratic absorption)
  - NSAIDs: ibuprofen 5-10 mg/kg or diclofenac 1 mg/kg, oral preoperatively or rectal/IV after induction
- Single perioperative opioid doses useful in some cases: fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg or codeine phosphate 1 mg/kg
  - Procedures needing repeated opioid doses are not suitable for day surgery

## Postoperative care
- Home analgesia mainly paracetamol and NSAIDs
- For some procedures, 3 or 4 doses of a weak opioid (e.g. codeine phosphate) may be supplied for parents to give, with written advice

## Discharge criteria
- Airway reflexes intact; no respiratory distress or stridor
- Stable vital signs; conscious level appropriate for the child
- No bleeding or surgical complications
- No or minimal pain and PONV
- Mobility appropriate for the child
- Drinking is desirable but not essential
- Written and verbal instructions with clear hospital contact
- Escorted home by a responsible adult by private car or taxi

## Audit and quality
- Commonest causes of unplanned admission: PONV and severe pain
- Benchmark admission rate: 1-2%
- Audit cancellations, unplanned admissions, morbidity, and parent and child satisfaction

## Flashcards
- Q: Below what postconceptual age are ex-preterm babies unsuitable for day surgery?
  - A: 50 weeks (risk may extend to 60 weeks or more with chronic lung disease)
- Q: Oral midazolam premedication dose in children?
  - A: 0.5 mg/kg, maximum 20 mg
- Q: Caudal adjunct doses of ketamine and clonidine?
  - A: Ketamine 0.5 mg/kg; clonidine 1 mcg/kg
- Q: Ideal maximum duration of a paediatric day case procedure?
  - A: 1 hour
- Q: Benchmark unplanned admission rate after paediatric day surgery?
  - A: 1-2%
