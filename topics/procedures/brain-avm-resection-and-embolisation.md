# Brain AVM Resection and Embolisation

Managed much like cerebral aneurysm surgery: strict BP and CO2 control, readiness for major bleeding, and watch for cerebral steal and normal perfusion pressure breakthrough.

## Background
- Rare congenital vascular malformation; can cause intracranial haemorrhage and epilepsy; usually presents in young adults
- Tangle of arteries and veins with no capillary bed between them
  - May supply eloquent cortex, which can be mapped before surgery with selective barbiturate injection in the awake patient
- Harms brain tissue by mass effect, haemorrhage and steal from surrounding tissue
- Anaesthesia needed for angiography with or without embolisation, surgical excision, or stereotactic radiosurgery (gamma knife)
- Often combined treatment
  - Endovascular occlusion of feeding arteries first; high-risk AVMs usually have serial embolisation before surgery to cut operative bleeding
  - Then surgical resection
- Cognard classification (dural AV fistulas): grades venous drainage pattern, which tracks a more aggressive clinical course and higher haemorrhage risk

## Key considerations
- As for aneurysm: strict BP control, control CO2 and other parameters
- Rupture or haemorrhage
  - Prepare for rapid major bleeding: arterial line, large-bore IV, group and crossmatch; blood in theatre for high-risk cases
  - Intraoperative rupture is much less likely than with an aneurysm
  - But 20 to 25 percent of AVMs carry aneurysms: avoid hypertension meticulously
- Cerebral steal (ischaemia before treatment)
  - Proposed cause of focal deficits in AVM patients
  - Can occur with hypotension as perfusion pressure falls
- Normal perfusion pressure breakthrough (hyperaemia after treatment)
  - Once the AVM is removed, blood that was shunted now perfuses surrounding tissue, which may not cope: rapid cerebral oedema, haemorrhage, raised ICP
  - Traditional approach: mild fluid restriction (or diuresis) and strict BP control, lowering BP after surgery
  - More recently, keeping normotension is favoured
  - With postoperative haemorrhage, always exclude residual AVM before blaming breakthrough

## Surgical resection
- Usually not urgent unless there is pressure effect
- Can cause massive blood loss
- No vasospasm risk, so hypotension can be used if needed
- Children
  - AVMs can cause high-output heart failure from intracerebral shunting
  - Excision can also precipitate heart failure

## Endovascular embolisation
- Used to obliterate the AVM or shrink it before surgery
- Liquid polymer or glue
- Reduces bleeding while keeping arterial supply to normal brain
- Staged procedures common with rapid flow, multiple fistulae, complex feeding and draining vessels, or associated aneurysms

### Practical points
- Long cases, usually about 2-8hrs: urinary catheter, forced-air warming
- Induction as for coiling
- Maintenance
  - Falling BP can cause intracerebral steal
  - Raised ICP from haemorrhage can push BP up sharply
- The femoral artery may be punctured several times, so a closure device is not used: needs smooth prolonged anaesthesia to avoid coughing and pressure on the puncture

### During embolisation
- The interventionalist may ask for hypotension (or adenosine-induced asystole) during injection so the glue sets and does not pass into draining veins or the systemic circulation; in practice such requests are uncommon
- Balloon inflation (to stop flow during embolisation) can be stimulating; patients may desaturate
- The team announces glue injection: the patient must not move (deep, fully relaxed)
- Embolic agent can migrate through the AVM
  - Occluding distal veins, or reaching the right heart and lungs with pulmonary hypertension
  - Inadvertent occlusion of normal vessels causes ischaemia
  - Pulmonary embolism from shunted particles
- Onyx (ethylene vinyl alcohol polymer, in DMSO)
  - Irritates the meninges; can cause tachycardia and hypertension
  - Leaves a distinctive smell on the patient that may last into the next day
- Sources of bleeding
  - Incomplete embolisation
  - Perforated arterial feeder
  - Ruptured associated aneurysm
  - Hyperperfusion (shared venous drainage leading to oedema and raised ICP)

## Flashcards
- Q: What proportion of brain AVMs have associated aneurysms?
  - A: 20 to 25 percent, so hypertension must be avoided
- Q: What is normal perfusion pressure breakthrough?
  - A: Oedema or haemorrhage after AVM removal as previously shunted blood hyperperfuses surrounding brain
- Q: What causes cerebral steal in AVM patients?
  - A: Hypotension lowering perfusion pressure to tissue around the AVM
- Q: Why might the interventionalist ask for hypotension or adenosine asystole?
  - A: To let the glue set and stop it passing into draining veins
