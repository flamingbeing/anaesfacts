# Paediatric Emergency Neurosurgery

Emergency paediatric neurosurgery is mostly blocked VP shunts, with fewer head injuries needing resuscitation, CT, surgery and intensive care.

## Blocked ventriculoperitoneal shunt
### Background
- Most non-elective paediatric neurosurgery involves VP shunts
- Median shunt survival before revision: 2 years if inserted under two years of age; 8-10 years if over two
- Blockage or fracture is fairly common: revise one part or replace the whole shunt
  - Commonest: proximal ventricular end blocked by cells, choroid plexus or debris
  - Also kinking, disconnection, fracture or migration of the distal end
  - Blockage often lies distal to the cranial valve, so aspirating CSF from the reservoir gives temporary relief
- Signs of blockage or fracture:
  - Headache, vomiting, drowsiness
  - Bulging fontanelle, cranial nerve palsies (especially sixth)
  - More seizures
- Confirmed by CT head or shunt tap
- Children are usually well known to the unit with many previous anaesthetics; parents usually recognise raised ICP

### Preoperative assessment
- Raised ICP is usually relieved beforehand by draining CSF through the access device
  - Child arrives with normal or near-normal ICP; surgery waits until fasted
  - IV access usually already placed on the ward
- Occasionally a child with raised ICP needs emergency anaesthesia

### Anaesthetic technique
- As for VP shunt insertion
  - Appropriately sized ETT, taped to the side
- Duration hard to predict: ventricular end may be replaced without retunnelling; full replacement takes 1-2 hours
- Minimal blood loss; give fasting deficit and maintenance as isotonic crystalloid
- Wound infiltration with local anaesthetic plus adrenaline for haemostasis and analgesia
  - Add fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV and paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV or 40 mg/kg rectal
- Extubate awake

### Postoperative care
- Neurological observations for the first 24 hours; escalate any deterioration or coma scale drop of two points or more
- Maintenance fluids until drinking
- Analgesia:
  - Paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 6-hourly
  - Ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly
  - Oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly if needed

## Head injury: initial resuscitation
### Background
- Commonest single cause of trauma death in children aged 1-15 years
  - 40% of injury-related deaths
  - Children: road traffic accidents commonest, then falls
  - Infants: non-accidental injury commonest

### Initial management
- Follow ATLS for TBI
- Fundoscopy: papilloedema suggests raised ICP; retinal haemorrhages raise suspicion of non-accidental injury in a young child, especially with other unexplained injuries

### Severe head injury
- Goal: prevent secondary brain injury after the primary insult
- Target MAP to maintain CPP by age:
  - <3 months: 40-60 mmHg
  - 3-12 months: 45-75 mmHg
  - 1-5 years: 50-90 mmHg
  - 6-11 years: 60-90 mmHg
  - 12-14 years: 65-95 mmHg

## Evacuation of intracranial haematoma
### Background
- Usually after trauma; emergency surgery to lower ICP, keep CPP and limit secondary injury
- Extradural, subdural or intracerebral haematoma
- Already assessed and stabilised by APLS ABC approach
- Early care for intracerebral haematoma: anaesthesia, intubation, ventilation, morphine and midazolam infusions, IV fluids to support CPP and oxygenation
- May need transfer to a paediatric neurosurgical centre

### Preoperative assessment
- Often brief and incomplete
- If already intubated: record ETT size and length, drugs given, allergies, vascular access
- Mannitol may have been given and will cause a diuresis
- Look for other injuries; clarify cervical spine status

### Anaesthetic technique
- Principles: keep CPP (lower ICP, support blood pressure), oxygenate and ventilate, protect lungs from aspiration
- Low MAP despite haemorrhage control and resuscitation: noradrenaline 0.1-0.5 mcg/kg/min
- Transfer to the theatre table carefully (possible cervical spine injury); log-roll if turning
- Not yet anaesthetised: RSI with armoured ETT usually preferred
  - Manual in-line stabilisation if cervical spine not cleared
- Moderate hyperventilation (arterial pCO2 4.0-4.5 kPa) is normal practice
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen and air, or oxygen and nitrous oxide
- Analgesia:
  - Morphine 100 mcg/kg or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg boluses, or remifentanil infusion 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min
  - With remifentanil, give morphine about 30 min before the end if extubation planned
  - Surgeon can infiltrate long-acting local anaesthetic at the wound
- Monitoring: capnography, SpO2, NIBP, ECG; arterial line (often femoral), but do not delay emergency surgery for it
- Temperature: skin or rectal probe, warming mattress
- Blood loss variable:
  - Crystalloid first for maintenance and losses
  - Group and save before surgery; urgent crossmatch if needed
  - Monitor haemoglobin with point-of-care Hb (HemoCue) or blood gas; transfuse if Hb falls below 7 g/dL
  - Major loss: urgent coagulation screen, then FFP or platelets guided by results
- Maintenance fluids restricted to 50% of normal, isotonic (e.g. Hartmann solution)
- Keep blood glucose normal
- Consider phenytoin loading 18 mg/kg if any seizure activity

### Postoperative care
- Awake before surgery with isolated extradural haematoma: usually woken and extubated
- More often: ITU with ventilation and sedation, especially after long surgery, raised ICP or other injuries
- Check U&E and FBC if not done
