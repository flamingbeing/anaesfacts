# Prostatectomy (Open, Laparoscopic, Robotic)

Removal of the prostate, usually for cancer; robotic surgery means steep head-down and long pneumoperitoneum, open surgery means bleeding and more pain.

## Summary (robotic)

- Steep head-down; prolonged pneumoperitoneum
- Robotic: paralyse; poor access
- Bleeding risk

## Procedure

- Open radical prostatectomy: removes the whole prostate, seminal vesicles, ejaculatory ducts and part of the bladder neck; usually for prostate cancer
- Pelvic lymph node dissection may be added for staging
- Laparoscopic and robotic approaches are increasingly used as they are less invasive

## Robotic laparoscopic prostatectomy

### Advantages

- To the surgeon
  - 3D vision
  - Tremor filtering
  - Motion scaling
  - Greater range of movement inside the patient
  - Stable, comfortable position
- To the patient
  - Less blood loss
  - Less pain
  - Shorter length of stay
  - Possibly less incontinence and urinary regurgitation

### Intraoperative management

- Robot
  - Team must know the equipment and be able to undock rapidly in an emergency
  - Little access once started; arms tucked: good running IV, arterial line
  - No patient movement at all (seriously disturbs the robot): keep paralysed
- Very steep head-down
  - Raised ICP
  - Raised intraocular pressure
  - Airway oedema
  - Lower limb compartment syndrome
- Prolonged pneumoperitoneum: surgical emphysema

## Open simple and radical prostatectomy

- Open excision of the prostate, with or without pelvic lymph nodes, with anastomosis

### Preoperative assessment

- Elderly men, as for TURP
- Radical surgery more often in younger men with fewer comorbidities
- Comorbidities: IHD, COPD, smoking
- Renal function (U+E)
- HDU bed for radical surgery

### Intraoperative management

- Supine, head down
- GA (ETT) with or without epidural
  - Epidural should reach T6-8
- Remifentanil infusion works well for intraoperative analgesia
- Moderate to large blood loss (usually below 1 litre)
  - Large-bore IV access
  - Consider an arterial line
  - Consider a CVP line: urine output does not reliably reflect volume status
  - Blood warmer; forced-air warming and heating blankets
  - Use the epidural cautiously until bleeding is controlled
  - Cell salvage helps in radical surgery
- Dyes after the urethral reconstruction (methylene blue or indigo carmine) may be requested
  - Methylene blue bolus: hypotension, false pulse oximeter readings
  - Indigo carmine: hypertension via D-agonist effects
- Complications: major blood loss, fluid shifts causing coagulopathy or anaemia, air embolism from head-down positioning
- Laparoscopic
  - Less invasive
  - Retroperitoneal insufflation reported to increase CO2 absorption and reduce urine output, which can lead to excess fluid being given

### Postoperative management

- Severe pain: epidural, or rectus sheath catheters with PCA and simple analgesics
- Urine output hard to measure because of irrigation
- Air embolism is a recognised complication

## PROSPECT 2020 recommendations

- Basic analgesia: paracetamol plus NSAID or COX-2 selective inhibitor
- Laparoscopic or robotic radical prostatectomy: bilateral TAP block is the first-choice regional technique
- Open surgery: consider IV lidocaine

## Flashcards

- Q: What epidural level is needed for open prostatectomy?
  - A: Up to T6-8
- Q: Usual blood loss in open prostatectomy?
  - A: Moderate to large, usually below 1 litre
- Q: First-choice regional technique for robotic radical prostatectomy (PROSPECT 2020)?

- Q: Effects of methylene blue and indigo carmine?
  - A: Methylene blue: hypotension and false pulse oximetry; indigo carmine: hypertension
