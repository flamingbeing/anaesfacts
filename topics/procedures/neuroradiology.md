# Interventional Neuroradiology

Remote-site neuroanaesthesia for diagnostic and endovascular brain procedures: tight BP and ICP control, a still patient, anticoagulation and radiation.

## Procedures
- Cerebral angiography and angioplasty
- Aneurysm coiling
- Functional testing of arteriovenous malformations
- Preoperative embolisation of vessels feeding intracranial lesions
- Preoperative assessment of carotid collateral flow
- Endovascular thrombectomy for ischaemic stroke

## Core considerations
- Neuroanaesthetic goals
  - Blood pressure: keep within a range set by ICP, evolving stroke, aneurysm status and baseline hypertension, agreed with the neurology team
    - Arterial line
    - Esmolol, labetalol or GTN for hypertension
  - ICP: control via PaCO2, venous return, sedation and paralysis
  - Neuroprotection: maintain CPP, PaO2, PaCO2, normoglycaemia and normothermia
  - Delicate work: the patient must be still and cooperative; movement risks injury and spoils images
- Heparin, antiplatelets and thrombolytics
  - Monitor ACT
  - Have protamine, platelets, FFP or plasmapheresis available
- Be ready to manage specific intraoperative crises
- Remote location
  - Unfamiliar room, dim light, radiology staff new to anaesthesia, help far away
  - Ensure a skilled assistant, dedicated recovery area and staff, resuscitation and difficult airway equipment in the suite
  - Transfers between CT, MRI, radiology, theatre and other hospitals: watch clinical state, temperature, lines, tubes, ventricular drains and equipment
- Bulky mobile imaging equipment
  - Table slides back and forth often: leave slack in lines, airway and monitor cables
  - Extensions on IV lines, arterial pressure tubing and the breathing circuit
  - Place the anaesthetic machine opposite the neuroradiologist, towards the feet, so imaging can move around the head
  - Poor access to airway and IV, since equipment sits over the head: make connections secure
  - Keep monitors and circuit out of the X-ray field; BIS may interfere with images
- Radiation: shielding, minimise time, keep distance (inverse square law)
- Contrast and flushes
  - Non-ionic low-osmolar agents cause fewer reactions
  - Watch for hyperosmolarity, hypervolaemia and then dehydration

## Anaesthetic technique
- Most procedures under GA
  - GA with ETT especially for therapeutic embolisation and coiling: these need a completely still field and carry more risk
  - Controlled ventilation gives normocapnia and so helps control ICP
- Local anaesthesia with or without sedation is possible
  - Be ready to convert rapidly to GA (e.g. aneurysm rupture, embolisation of a critical feeding vessel)
  - Some diagnostic tests need the patient awake and responsive, e.g. temporary vessel occlusion or intra-arterial barbiturate injection; sedation must be titratable
- Mostly little pain apart from femoral artery access

## Airway
- ETT generally preferred: long procedures, restricted access, and spontaneous breathing prevents control of EtCO2
- Exchange to an LMA can smooth extubation and emergence

## Postoperative care
- Consider leaving the arterial sheath in; press on the groin if it is removed
- Bed rest usually 6hr, as the leg cannot be bent
- Do not wake the patient until the pressure dressing is on; coughing can cause bleeding

## AVM embolisation
- Aims to obliterate the nidus, or shrink it before surgery or radiosurgery
- Hyperventilation can redistribute flow: it reduces flow to normal brain while flow in the nidus is maintained
- Cyanoacrylate glues are most used: low-viscosity liquid monomers that polymerise on contact with ionic blood
- Worst complication: acute cerebral haemorrhage when glue passes into the draining vein
  - Mild hypotension during injection may help

## Carotid angioplasty and stenting
- CREST: no significant overall difference between stenting and endarterectomy in a composite of periprocedural stroke, MI or death plus ipsilateral stroke over four years of follow-up
  - ACC/AHA therefore accept stenting as an alternative to endarterectomy for symptomatic carotid stenosis
- Neurological function must be assessed during the procedure
- Balloon or stent stretch of the carotid triggers baroreceptor reflexes: rapid swings in BP and heart rate
  - Severe bradycardia or asystole from carotid body stretch
  - Treat with IV atropine (0.5-1 mg) or glycopyrrolate (1 mg)
- Risk of cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome
- Usually under MAC: GA is avoided because neurology cannot be assessed

## Flashcards
- Q: Why is GA with ETT preferred for coiling and therapeutic embolisation?
  - A: They need a completely still field, carry higher risk, and controlled ventilation keeps normocapnia for ICP control.
- Q: Why can hyperventilation help during AVM embolisation?
  - A: It reduces flow to normal brain while flow to the nidus is maintained.
- Q: Most serious complication of glue embolisation and its BP strategy?
  - A: Acute cerebral haemorrhage from glue entering the draining vein; mild hypotension may help.
- Q: Treatment of bradycardia or asystole during carotid stenting?
  - A: IV atropine 0.5-1 mg or glycopyrrolate 1 mg.
- Q: Why is carotid stenting usually done under MAC?
  - A: Neurological function can be assessed throughout.
