# Lower Limb Arthroscopy

Mostly knee (some ankle) arthroscopy in younger day-case patients: aim for early mobilisation and discharge.

## Procedures
- Examination under anaesthesia (EUA)
- Meniscal surgery and loose body removal
- Synovectomy
- Ligament reconstruction (e.g. ACL)

## Preoperative assessment
- Patients are usually younger than joint replacement patients
- Smaller cases are day cases: technique must allow early walking and discharge
- Tourniquet often used
- EUA with or without washout can be done under intra-articular and infiltration local anaesthetic alone
- Nerve blocks: limited by long duration and failure to cover the thigh tourniquet site

## Intraoperative management
- Premedication: paracetamol and an NSAID
- Standard day-case GA with LMA plus IV opioid, e.g. fentanyl 1 mcg/kg
- Low-dose IV ketamine (0.15 mg/kg) suggested to improve analgesia
- Avoid IV morphine if possible: high PONV rate in day cases

## Postoperative care
- Discharge with NSAIDs and strong oral analgesics
- Many surgeons inject 10–20 ml of 0.5% bupivacaine, with or without morphine 10 mg, into the joint
- Nerve blocks slow postoperative mobilisation

## Flashcards
- Q: What low dose of IV ketamine has been suggested to enhance analgesia for arthroscopy?
  - A: 0.15 mg/kg.
- Q: What intra-articular local anaesthetic do surgeons often instil after knee arthroscopy?
  - A: 10–20 ml of 0.5% bupivacaine, with or without morphine 10 mg.
- Q: Why avoid IV morphine in day-case arthroscopy?
  - A: High incidence of PONV.
- Q: Why are nerve blocks of limited use for knee arthroscopy?
  - A: Long duration, they miss the tourniquet site and they hinder mobilisation.
