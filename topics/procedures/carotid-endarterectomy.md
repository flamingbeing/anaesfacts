# Carotid Endarterectomy

Vascular patients having carotid plaque removed: keep cerebral perfusion up during cross-clamp, monitor the brain (awake patient is best), then prevent hypertension, haematoma and hyperperfusion.

## Main considerations
- Patient:
  - Vascular disease throughout
  - Record baseline neurological deficits so a new perioperative stroke can be recognised
- Surgical:
  - Carotid cross-clamp:
    - Maintain perfusion: induced hypertension, shunt
    - Neurological monitoring: NIRS
    - General neuroprotection: CO2 and FiO2 control
  - Afterwards avoid hypertension to prevent cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome (CHS)
  - Airway oedema, haematoma, cranial nerve injury
- Anaesthetic:
  - GA or regional
  - Early neurological assessment

## Carotid stenosis

### Anatomy
- Internal carotid arteries (right and left): 80-90% of cerebral blood flow (CBF)
- Vertebrobasilar arteries (right and left): 10-20% of CBF
- Carotids and basilar artery join at the circle of Willis, which protects against occlusion of one vessel

### Causes and pathophysiology
- Atherosclerosis (90%)
- Rare: dissection, arteritis, fibromuscular dysplasia, radiation
- Risk factors: hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes, smoking
- Plaque with a central lipid core, arterial remodelling with abnormal adhesion molecules, endothelial dysfunction
- Unstable plaque ruptures, leading to thrombosis and embolism

### Clinical presentation
- Transient ischaemic attack (TIA): short reversible deficits (e.g. monocular blindness, limb weakness, sensory change, speech disturbance)
- Prolonged reversible ischaemic neurological deficit (PRIND): deficit lasting longer than 24 hours but resolving within 1 to 3 weeks
- Ischaemic stroke

### Best medical therapy
- Antiplatelet: aspirin or clopidogrel
- High-intensity statin to stabilise plaque
- Risk factors: tight BP control, stop smoking, diabetes control

### Surgical options
- Carotid endarterectomy: clamp ECA, ICA and CCA, open the ICA lengthwise, remove plaque, close directly or with a patch (Gore-Tex or vein)
- Carotid artery stenting: endovascular, by interventional radiology
- Stenting versus CEA (CREST): stenting has higher perioperative stroke risk but lower perioperative MI risk; equal protection from ipsilateral stroke over 4–10 years

### Indications and timing
- Symptomatic means ipsilateral ischaemic stroke or TIA
- Symptomatic >70% extracranial stenosis: clear indication
  - Best done within 2 weeks of the index event
- Symptomatic 50-69%: consider if surgical risk is low
- Asymptomatic: controversial; consider at 70%
  - Operate if stroke prevention outweighs risk (intermediate-risk surgery; combined mortality and major stroke 2-5%)
- <50% stenosis: no benefit, contraindicated
- NICE 2008: 50-99% by NASCET equals 70-99% by ECST; NASCET is the usual standard now

## Preoperative assessment
- Combined perioperative mortality and major stroke: 2-5%
- Full vascular work-up
- Full neurological assessment:
  - Document existing deficits (stroke, TIA)
  - CT or MRI brain: site and severity of stroke
  - Carotid ultrasound: site and severity of stenosis
- Neck range of movement:
  - Head neutral for angiography, extended for CEA
  - Patients with carotid or vertebral insufficiency may not tolerate extreme flexion or rotation
- Group and crossmatch

## Preoperative optimisation
- Aim BP < 160/90 mmHg
  - Delay surgery if DBP > 110 mmHg: high risk of cardiovascular complications and postoperative CHS
- Continue aspirin after checking with the surgeon

## Intraoperative management

### Monitoring
- Arterial line before induction, in the contralateral arm (subclavian occlusion on the operative side can lose the trace)
- Central line usually not needed: little blood loss, and risk of puncturing the other carotid
- Neuromonitoring: NIRS

### Haemodynamic control
- Hypotension: vasopressors
- Hypertension: GTN, SNP
- Before cross-clamp: avoid hypotension; keep BP within 20% of baseline to protect CPP; cardiostable induction
- During cross-clamp: induced hypertension, BP at or above baseline to maintain CPP
- After unclamping: avoid hypertension; keep BP at baseline
  - Low BP risks stroke or MI; high BP risks cerebral hyperperfusion or haematoma
  - Smooth emergence and extubation
- Carotid sinus handling can cause bradycardia, hypotension or asystole
  - Stop surgery, atropine IV 0.6-2.4mg, local anaesthetic infiltration around the carotid sinus
  - Rebound catecholamine release may follow: hypertension, tachycardia

## Regional anaesthesia technique
- Patient preparation: counselling, self-positioning, urinary catheter
- Block options:
  - Superficial or deep cervical plexus block at Erb's point (midpoint of sternocleidomastoid)
    - Superficial block needs about as much supplemental local anaesthetic as deep
  - Mandibular branch block: infiltrate along the jawline for the marginal mandibular nerve (facial nerve, CN VII) and the mandibular nerve (V3); reduces pain from submandibular retraction
  - Carotid sheath infiltration by the surgeon: the glossopharyngeal (CN IX) and vagus (CN X) nerves supply the carotid sinus and body, so this blunts the baroreceptor reflex (sudden bradycardia or hypotension)
  - Surgeon's local anaesthetic top-ups during dissection
  - Cervical epidural (rare): good conditions; risks dural puncture, epidural vein puncture, respiratory muscle paralysis
- Sedation:
  - During block and dissection only
  - None during cross-clamp, so neurology can be assessed continuously
  - Propofol; remifentanil (can be sole agent); dexmedetomidine (conscious sedation without respiratory depression, blunts the hypertensive response to clamping)
- Cross-clamp:
  - Heparin before clamping; stop sedation
  - Consider a 2-minute test clamp before arteriotomy
  - Watch for ischaemia:
    - Clinical: mental state, speech, contralateral motor power; often the same symptoms as the original TIA
    - Surrogate: NIRS (<60% or 20% drop)
  - Deficits appear in 3 ways:
    - Profound unconsciousness straight after clamping
    - Subtle immediate deficit (confusion, dysphasia, slow answers)
    - Delayed deficit, usually from relative hypotension
- Deficit after cross-clamp:
  - Raise BP with drugs: improves collateral flow across the circle of Willis
  - Supplemental O2
  - Shunt if BP augmentation fails
    - Shunt complications: intimal tear, dissection, haematoma, particulate or gas embolism, thrombosis, late restenosis, infection
  - Consider conversion to GA
- Unresponsive patient after cross-clamp (emergency):
  - Tell the surgeon to stop; call for skilled help
  - Airway: open it; breathing: bag-mask with 100% high-flow O2 (may improve neurology)
  - Raise BP 20% above baseline
  - Release the clamp; consider a shunt
  - Consider GA if still unresponsive (possible thrombosis or major embolus)
    - High risk: poor airway access
    - Protect the brain: avoid hypotension and hypoxia
- End of surgery:
  - Valsalva (strain or blow into a syringe) and BP augmentation to test haemostasis
  - Protamine usually avoided (risk of acute thrombosis on the raw endarterectomy surface)

## General anaesthesia technique
- Induction: GA with ETT; smooth, haemodynamically stable
- Maintenance:
  - Maintain BP and CPP
  - Neuroprotection: normotension, normoxia, normocarbia, normothermia, normoglycaemia
    - Hypocarbia causes cerebral vasoconstriction; hypercarbia causes steal
  - Avoid N2O: raises CMRO2 and enlarges air emboli in the distal circulation during clamping
- Cross-clamp:
  - Heparin before clamping
  - Consider thiopentone IV 250mg before clamping for neuroprotection
  - Monitor for ischaemia: NIRS most common now
  - Ischaemia: raise MAP to 20% above baseline, supplemental O2, shunt if BP augmentation fails
- End of surgery: Valsalva and BP augmentation
- Emergence:
  - Smooth, controlled emergence to avoid neck haematoma and CHS
    - Remifentanil infusion at extubation to stop coughing on the tube
  - Early neurological assessment: aim for an awake, cooperative patient at the end
    - Short-acting drugs with little hangover (desflurane, remifentanil)
    - Superficial cervical plexus block reduces opioid need

## Postoperative management
- Watch the airway (neck haematoma)
- Avoid hypotension and hypertension
- Neurological observations

### Airway emergency
- Voice change or hoarseness can herald obstruction from haematoma or oedema
- Stridor or distress: open the skin clips and the deep (investing) fascia at the bedside straight away
- Expect a difficult airway: laryngeal oedema, midline shift

### New neurological deficit after CEA
- Differential diagnosis:
  - Local: vessel thrombosis, intimal flap, distal embolism; neck haematoma (usually airway signs first)
  - Systemic: CHS, intracranial haemorrhage
  - Mimics: hypoglycaemia, post-ictal state
- Management:
  - ABC and augment blood pressure
  - Full neurological examination and wound check
  - Tell the surgeon at once; then:
    - Bedside ultrasound, CT brain or CT angiogram, especially if unclear
    - Distal embolic occlusion (e.g. MCA) on CTA: involve interventional radiology for EVT
    - Immediate return to theatre for re-exploration

## GA versus regional anaesthesia

### Neurological
- Cerebral monitoring:
  - Regional: awake patient is the gold standard
  - GA: relies on surrogate monitors
- Cerebral perfusion and oxygenation:
  - Regional: autoregulation kept, but harder to manipulate physiology if ischaemia occurs
  - GA: volatiles uncouple autoregulation, but BP, FiO2 and CO2 (normocarbia) are easy to control
- CMRO2:
  - Regional: discomfort (poor block, pain, anxiety, claustrophobia, full bladder, unable to lie flat) causes tachycardia, hypertension, myocardial ischaemia and higher CMRO2; no neuroprotective drugs
  - GA: lowers CMRO2; neuroprotective drugs possible (e.g. thiopentone)
- Shunting: fewer shunts with regional; GA may lead to unnecessary shunts
- Outcome: GALA trial found no difference in 30day mortality, stroke or MI

### Surgical
- Conditions: regional needs cooperation (unsuitable with movement disorders or dysphasia); GA gives a still field
- Wound bleeding: less with regional, more with GA

### Anaesthetic
- Airway:
  - Regional: no airway handling or its haemodynamic surges; but airway uncontrolled, risk of obstruction and hypoventilation with oversedation, and emergency GA is hard with limited access
  - GA: airway handled with surges at laryngoscopy, emergence and extubation; but early airway and ventilation control
- Haemodynamics: GA is less stable, with surges at laryngoscopy and emergence
- Cervical plexus block risks:
  - Carotid, IJV or vertebral artery puncture and intravascular injection
  - Epidural or intrathecal spread (deep block): total spinal
  - Phrenic nerve palsy (deep block), important in COPD
  - Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity
  - Conversion to GA (2.5%)

## Neuromonitoring and shunting
- Why it matters: false positives cause needless shunts; false negatives miss strokes
- Types:
  - Flow: stump pressure, transcranial Doppler (TCD)
  - Oxygenation: jugular bulb oximetry, NIRS
  - Function: EEG, SSEP, awake testing

### 0) Awake testing (regional only)
- Simple tasks, e.g. squeeze the contralateral hand
- Gold standard: direct test of function, no surrogate

### 1a) Stump pressure
- Pressure in the distal ICA after clamping CCA and ECA: back-pressure from the circle of Willis
- Normal = 60-70 mmHg
- Pros: high sensitivity and NPV for ischaemia (97-98%); a high stump pressure (typically >50mmHg) means collateral flow is enough and a shunt is not needed
- Cons:
  - Poor specificity (30-50%), so unnecessary shunts; cut-off uncertain, <50mmHg used to shunt
  - Static: ignores metabolic demand and emboli

### 1b) Transcranial Doppler
- Probe over the petrous temporal bone measures MCA flow velocity
- Pros: measures flow, not just pressure; only monitor that detects microemboli (high-intensity transient signals, HITS), so the surgeon can reduce handling or flush the shunt
- Cons: operator dependent; no acoustic window in 10-20% of patients; probe may get in the surgeon's way

### 2a) Jugular venous bulb oximetry (SjvO2)
- Rarely used now; replaced by NIRS
- Catheter passed up the IJV to the bulb; SjvO2 reflects global supply-demand balance
- Pro: global oxygenation
- Cons: cannot detect focal hypoperfusion; errors from tip position, protein build-up, aspirating too fast

### 2b) Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS)
- Regional cerebral saturation (rSO2), a mix of arterial, venous and capillary blood
- Pros: continuous, non-invasive, regional; high sensitivity and NPV (good for ruling out)
- Cons:
  - Lower specificity and PPV: falls with extracranial flow change, hypoxia or hypocapnia
  - Frontal sensors poorly placed for MCA territory
  - Interference from ambient light and scalp flow
  - Cannot detect emboli
  - Cut-off uncertain: rSO2 < 60% or a fall > 20% from baseline prompts a shunt

### 3a) EEG (raw or processed)
- Pros: functional; high specificity for cortical ischaemia (where most strokes occur)
- Cons: misses deeper subcortical activity; affected by GA drugs; cannot detect emboli; needs expertise

### 3b) Somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP)
- Cortical response to peripheral nerve stimulation
- Pros: functional; covers cortical and deeper subcortical structures; less affected by anaesthetics than EEG, so useful when baseline EEG is abnormal
- Cons: only sensory pathways, so motor strokes may be missed; still some drug effect; cannot detect emboli; needs kit and expertise
- Combining modalities (e.g. EEG with SSEP) is often better

### Shunting
- Plastic tube from CCA to ICA to keep flow when the other carotid is stenosed or the circle of Willis is incomplete
- No Level 1 evidence: never, selective (guided by monitoring) or routine shunting all practised
- Complications:
  - Vascular: intimal tear, dissection, haematoma, air or plaque embolism, late restenosis
  - Other: nerve injury, infection

## Complications

### Early
- Airway obstruction:
  - Airway oedema from dissection near the airway (GA or regional)
  - Neck haematoma (5-10%): remove skin sutures to drain
  - Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury
- Haemodynamic instability:
  - Carotid sinus hypersensitivity after plaque removal: hypotension
  - Post-CEA hypertension: from carotid sinus denervation, more common after GA
    - Risks: myocardial ischaemia, reperfusion injury, cerebral haemorrhage or ischaemia
    - Chronic hypoperfusion beyond the stenosis abolishes autoregulation, so flow becomes pressure-dependent and surges after CEA
    - Treatment: vasodilators (GTN preferred; hydralazine is slower and causes tachycardia; SNP dilates normal vessels); beta-blocker labetalol IV 5-10mg
  - MI (2%)
- Neurological:
  - Cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome (1-3%)
  - Stroke (3-4%): mostly thromboembolic, not from hypo- or hypertension
- Cranial nerve injuries: hypoglossal, recurrent laryngeal, superior laryngeal, marginal mandibular branch of facial, greater auricular, phrenic
- Regional anaesthesia: phrenic palsy, recurrent laryngeal palsy, total spinal, local anaesthetic toxicity

### Late
- Restenosis
- Infection

### Cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome (CHS)
- Within 1wk of surgery
- Brain previously shielded by a tight stenosis loses autoregulation and is now exposed to high pressure: oedema and haemorrhage
- Often when cerebral perfusion rises by more than 100% over baseline
- Risk factors:
  - Hypertension before or after surgery
  - Poor cerebrovascular reserve
  - Recent ipsilateral non-haemorrhagic stroke
  - Previous cerebral infarction
  - Surgery for > 90% ipsilateral stenosis
  - Intraoperative ischaemia or emboli
  - Hyperperfusion lasting more than a few hours
- Features: hypertension, ipsilateral headache, seizures, focal deficits; oedema, intracerebral or subarachnoid haemorrhage, coma, death
- Management:
  - High index of suspicion
  - Tight BP control, aim SBP <140mmHg (perfusion is pressure-dependent)
  - Labetalol, hydralazine
  - Avoid direct vasodilators that dilate cerebral vessels (calcium channel blockers, nitroprusside, GTN)
  - Osmotic diuretics, steroids

## Key trials

### Symptomatic stenosis
- NASCET 1991 (North American)
- ECST 1998 (European)
- Rothwell pooled analysis (Lancet 2004): 6902 patients from ECST, NASCET and Veterans Affairs
  - Stenosis > 70% (not near-occlusion): highly beneficial, 16% absolute reduction in ipsilateral stroke or death at 5y, NNT = 6.3
  - 50-69%: marginal, 4.6% absolute reduction, NNT = 22
  - 30-49%: no benefit; < 30%: harmful

### Asymptomatic stenosis
- ACAS 1995
- ACST 2004: patients < 75 years with > 60% stenosis; CEA halved 5-year stroke risk (~12% to ~6%)
  - Caveat: best medical therapy has improved since

### CEA versus stenting
- CREST 2010: RCT, 2502 patients
  - No difference in composite stroke, MI or death
  - Stroke higher with stenting (4.1% vs 2.3%); MI higher with CEA (2.3% vs 1.1%)
  - Older patients (> 70) do better with CEA; younger may favour stenting (less MI)
- SAPPHIRE: stenting with distal protection versus CEA in high-risk patients
  - No significant difference in stroke, MI and death at 3y; more restenosis after stenting; long-term benefit unknown

### GA versus local anaesthesia
- GALA 2008: RCT, 3,526 patients
  - No difference in 30-day stroke, MI or death (4.8% GA vs 4.5% LA) within 30days
  - Local: fewer shunts, less bleeding
  - Limitation: high-risk patients often excluded

## Flashcards
- Q: What BP target applies during carotid cross-clamp?
  - A: At or above baseline; with ischaemia raise MAP 20% above baseline.
- Q: What NIRS change prompts a shunt?
  - A: rSO2 < 60% or a fall > 20% from baseline.
- Q: What stump pressure is used as the shunt threshold?
  - A: <50mmHg.
- Q: When does CHS occur and what is the SBP target?
  - A: Within 1wk; SBP <140mmHg.
- Q: What did GALA show?
  - A: No difference in 30-day stroke, MI or death (4.8% GA vs 4.5% LA); regional gave fewer shunts and less bleeding.
