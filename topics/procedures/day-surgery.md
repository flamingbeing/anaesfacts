# Day Surgery Anaesthesia

Choose patients well, use short-acting drugs, prevent pain and PONV aggressively, and discharge the same day.

## Definition
- Planned procedure or investigation with discharge within 24h

## Advantages
- For patients
  - Less hospital-acquired infection
  - Less VTE, thanks to early walking
  - Lower hospital-stay costs
  - Less time away from home and work; less social disruption
  - More convenient for patient and family
  - Shorter separation from family: less anxiety, especially for children
- For hospitals
  - Better efficiency, cost-effectiveness and use of resources
  - Lower inpatient workload and nursing need

## Safety
- 30-day perioperative mortality: day surgery 0.08%, cataract surgery 0.02%, all surgery 0.8%

## Key principles
- Emphasis on efficiency and throughput
- SAMBA (Society for Ambulatory Anesthesia) S.A.F.E. principle: short-acting, fast-emergence anaesthesia
- Goals
  - Rapid emergence
  - Prevent and treat pain and PONV quickly
  - Fast-track patients through recovery
  - Theatre efficiency
  - Patient convenience
- The 4 As: alertness, analgesia, ambulation, alimentation

## Patient selection: surgical factors
- Will the operation need inpatient monitoring or care?
- Short duration: no absolute cut-off, but best under 2hrs
- Low risk of serious complications
  - Airway compromise
  - Haemodynamic instability, big fluid shifts, major bleeding
  - Unsuitable if invasive monitoring or transfusion is expected
- Low pain: manageable with oral or rectal analgesia and local anaesthetic techniques, little opioid
- Postoperative needs can be met at home
  - No special nursing: unsuitable with drains for bleeding, NG tube, complex dressings
  - No IV drugs or analgesia
  - Quick return to eating and drinking
  - Expected to pass urine
  - No prolonged immobility: should mobilise before discharge (full mobility not always needed)
- Typical day procedures
  - Excision of local lesions (cyst, melanoma, breast biopsy, mastectomy)
  - Orthopaedics without major fractures
  - Basic ENT (sinus, tonsillectomy, tympanoplasty, cochlear surgery, thyroid lobectomy)
  - Limited plastics (blepharoplasty, scar revision)
  - Limited urology (cystoscopy, biopsy, vasectomy, circumcision)
  - Ophthalmology (not vitrectomy or enucleation)
  - Limited gynaecology (hysteroscopy, D&C, cone biopsy, tubal ligation)

## Patient selection: patient factors
- Comorbidity: even a minor procedure in a complex patient may need admission
- Previous anaesthetic problems: difficult airway, PONV, postoperative cognitive dysfunction, poor pain control
- Children: risk factors for postoperative apnoea
  - Gestational or post-conceptual age (PCA), and anaemia
    - Term infants under 1 month (under 44 weeks PCA)
    - Ex-preterm under 60wks PCA, or Hct under 30%; incidence of postoperative apnoea under 0.05%
  - History of ventilation or apnoea: infants on a home apnoea/bradycardia monitor must be off it and apnoea-free for 6mths
  - Strong family history of sudden infant death syndrome
  - Cardiac, respiratory or other significant disease
- Adults
  - No upper age limit
    - Age over 85y independently predicts admission and death after day surgery; all parties should know and plan for this
  - ASA I and II; stable chronic disease (e.g. diabetes, asthma, epilepsy) may do better as day cases (less routine disruption)
  - Medically stable ASA III or IV: possible after anaesthetic consultation
    - Ischaemic heart disease: intermediate cardiac risk patients not having high-risk surgery may proceed
    - Severe heart failure
    - Poorly controlled asthma: check symptom control and past admissions or intubations
    - Severe OSA
      - Consider severity, sleep study and treatment
      - Type of surgery: unsuitable for airway surgery, tonsillectomy in children under 3 years, upper abdominal surgery
      - Anaesthetic plan: GA versus regional or local; postoperative opioid need
      - Well-treated OSA, low-risk surgery under local or regional with little or no sedation and minimal opioid: suitable
    - Morbid obesity (BMI over 35 kg/m2): look at complications; obesity alone may not exclude day surgery
      - Complications rise with BMI in both day and inpatient surgery
      - Obese patients gain from short-acting techniques and early mobilisation
    - Significant neurological disease (e.g. myasthenia); well-controlled epilepsy is not a contraindication
  - Difficult airway

## Patient selection: social factors
- Responsible carer to take the patient home and ideally stay for 24-48h, able to make decisions for them
  - Transport home by private car or taxi
  - Follows postoperative instructions (written advice, including when a child can return to normal activity)
  - Watches for complications
- Can get medical help: telephone access, and can reach hospital within 1h
- Patient willing to go home the same day

## Preoperative assessment
- Ideally in a preoperative clinic well before surgery, so problems can be found and optimised
- Goals: routine fitness assessment and optimisation, suitability for day surgery, and education on the day surgery pathway
- Testing guided by patient factors; some stand-alone day units do no routine tests

## Premedication
- Avoid sedative premedication where possible (prolongs recovery, especially after short cases)
- Pre-emptive analgesia: oral paracetamol 1g and an NSAID (e.g. oral diclofenac 50-100mg), peak effect at 1-2h
  - Few side effects; useful for hernia repair, laparoscopic cholecystectomy, arthroscopy, mastectomy

## Choice of technique
- GA, regional, combined, and monitored sedation all allow quick discharge
- GA
  - For: faster induction, shorter recovery stay, earlier mobilisation
  - Against: more pain and analgesic need (multimodal analgesia needed), more PONV, slower return to eating
- Regional or local
  - For: avoids GA and its risks (including the difficult airway); less PONV, so earlier eating and discharge; better analgesia with less opioid; may bypass first-stage recovery; earlier mobilisation; cost-effective
  - Against
    - Needs a cooperative patient
    - Takes time: discussion, block performance (list planning), slow onset with long-acting local anaesthetic, failed block needing supplementation or GA
    - Prolonged block can delay discharge: spinals must have worn off fully before walking
    - Discharge with a numb limb lacking protective reflexes is debated; give written care instructions (e.g. sling after brachial plexus block)
    - Neuraxial problems that delay discharge: PDPH, transient neurological symptoms, prolonged motor block, urinary retention

## Intraoperative management
- Monitoring: processed EEG (BIS, SedLine) may help avoid overdose and slow recovery
- Airway
  - ETT: secure airway, especially in shared-airway surgery (e.g. dental); but needs IPPV, relaxant and reversal; more trauma and sore throat
  - LMA: spontaneous breathing, no relaxant or reversal, less trauma; but unprotected airway, aspiration risk
- Induction: fast on and off drugs; propofol preferred to thiopental
- Volatile (sevoflurane, desflurane)
  - Clinically similar when depth is titrated; desflurane (low blood solubility) may help some groups, e.g. morbid obesity
  - For: rapid emergence; less awareness if MAC over 0.8
  - Against: more PONV, more hangover
- Propofol TIVA
  - For: less PONV, safe in malignant hyperthermia, airway instrumentation without relaxant
  - Against: slower recovery after long infusions, possibly more awareness, needs TCI equipment, more expensive
- Relaxants: short-acting preferred (e.g. mivacurium, atracurium); suxamethonium myalgia may delay return to normal activity
- IV fluids 15ml/kg reduce PONV and dizziness and speed recovery

## Analgesia
- Multimodal, to cut opioid need and its side effects (sedation, PONV, respiratory depression)
  - Local anaesthetic to the wound; regional techniques
  - Paracetamol PR or IV 15-20mg/kg
  - NSAIDs: diclofenac PR or IV 1mg/kg; or COX-2 inhibitors such as parecoxib
  - Tell patients to take analgesia before a single-shot block wears off
- Opioids: short-acting (remifentanil, alfentanil, fentanyl); avoid high-dose long-acting opioid (morphine over 0.1mg/kg raises admission rates)
- Adjuncts: low-dose ketamine (NMDA antagonist), gabapentin, pregabalin
- Continuous peripheral nerve catheters (see below)

## PONV prevention
- A leading cause of delayed discharge and unplanned admission
- Non-drug measures
  - Local or regional rather than GA
  - If GA: TIVA, avoiding volatile, nitrous oxide, opioids and anticholinergics
  - Short fluid fast (2h) and good hydration (IV fluid)
- Drugs
  - A single 5HT3 antagonist (e.g. ondansetron) is cheap, safe and reasonable for most
  - Higher risk: combine agents
    - High risk: previous PONV, motion sickness, female non-smokers, ear, eye, gynaecological or abdominal surgery
    - Scopolamine patch, low-dose dexamethasone, 5HT3 antagonists and metoclopramide sedate less than droperidol, prochlorperazine or promethazine

## Regional techniques
- Peripheral nerve blocks
  - Brachial plexus block as sole technique: plan the list around onset time
  - Femoral nerve block: debated in adults because it hampers mobilisation
- Low-dose spinal
  - 25/26G pencil-point needle to cut PDPH risk
  - Low-dose local anaesthetic (bupivacaine 3.5-7mg, lidocaine 10-30mg, ropivacaine 5-10mg) plus opioid (fentanyl 10-25mcg, sufentanil 5-10mcg)
  - Opioid improves block quality and allows less local anaesthetic; similar onset; less motor block, so earlier discharge (4h vs 6h)
- Epidural: less suitable, block takes 10-15min
- Caudal: dilute solution (e.g. bupivacaine 0.125%); add ketamine 0.5mg/kg or clonidine 1mcg/kg to prolong block up to 24h

## Local anaesthesia with sedation
- Small doses of short-acting sedatives give good amnesia with few after-effects; sedation does not rescue a poor block
- Propofol TCI 1-1.5mcg/ml
- Midazolam IV 1-2mg boluses
- Fentanyl
- Remifentanil IV 0.05mcg/kg/min
- A dedicated person other than the surgeon should give and monitor sedation

## Recovery and fast-tracking
- Patients and families want quick discharge
- Pain and ongoing nausea or vomiting (with poor oral intake) are the two commonest causes of delay: treat aggressively
  - Treat PONV with a different class from the prophylactic drug
  - Treat pain with fast IV analgesia, then move to oral
- Phase I recovery: active issues (BP, pain, hypoxia); Phase II: nearly ready to go, apart from voiding, walking or eating
- Fast-tracking: from theatre straight to Phase II, bypassing Phase I
  - Needs an adapted technique so most patients reach a modified Aldrete score over 12 in theatre
  - Short-acting drugs titrated with monitoring such as BIS for quick emergence
  - Pain and PONV prophylaxis
- Limitations
  - Safety must come first; audit to check benefits outweigh risks
  - Pressure to use costlier drugs or stretch eligibility
  - Risk of awareness if anaesthetists cut doses to fast-track
  - Savings are hard to measure

## Continuous peripheral nerve block at home
- Disposable elastomeric pump
- For: sustained analgesia, opioid-sparing, better rehabilitation, fewer recovery and unplanned admissions
- Against: numb limb at home, catheter migration, local anaesthetic toxicity, infection
- Safety: dilute solution, careful selection, 24h access to anaesthetic services, clear written and verbal instructions, daily phone follow-up

## Discharge home
- Still meets recovery discharge criteria: stable airway, breathing, circulation and consciousness; surgical issues settled; pain and PONV controlled
- Reasonable pain control on oral drugs
- Tolerating oral intake without vomiting; most units ask for a light snack and drink
- Voiding before discharge: required in some units; many waive it unless at high risk of retention (neuraxial block, pelvic surgery), with home support and transport to emergency care if needed
- Extra criteria after regional
  - Spinal: full motor power and proprioception back; passed urine
  - Brachial plexus block: some motor recovery; understands how to protect the limb
  - Lower limb block: some motor recovery; understands limb protection; safe on crutches
- Escort home and carer at home essential
  - At-own-risk discharge forms signed after, or in anticipation of, GA are not legally valid
  - No escort: cancel the case
- Discharge checklist
  - IV cannula out; wound checked
  - Written and verbal advice on anaesthetic and surgical aftercare, including care of a numb limb
  - Discharge drugs (mainly analgesia) with instructions
  - Suture removal date if needed
  - Clinic appointment or letter to GP
  - Emergency contact number
  - Collected by a responsible adult
- Advice
  - Analgesia and rescue plan, verbal and written
    - 20-40% of patients may have moderate to severe pain at home, lasting 2-4days
    - Regular round-the-clock analgesia; start before the block wears off
  - Drowsiness and fatigue are common for several days
    - No driving or cycling for at least 24h
    - No heavy machinery
  - Follow-up phone call on postoperative day 1

## Factors delaying discharge
- Preoperative: female sex, older age, heart failure
- Intraoperative: long surgery, GA, spinal
- Postoperative: PONV, pain, drowsiness, no escort

## Unplanned admission (incidence 0.5-2%)
- Surgical: pain, bleeding, surgical complications, need for more extensive surgery
- Anaesthetic: PONV, drowsiness or dizziness, cognitive dysfunction, cardiovascular events, laryngospasm, aspiration

## Flashcards
- Q: 30-day mortality after day surgery versus all surgery?
  - A: 0.08% versus 0.8% (cataract surgery 0.02%)
- Q: Which ex-preterm infants are at risk of postoperative apnoea?
  - A: Under 60 weeks PCA, or Hct under 30%; term infants under 44 weeks PCA
- Q: Morphine dose above which admission rates rise after day surgery?
  - A: Over 0.1 mg/kg
- Q: Low-dose spinal bupivacaine dose and fentanyl addition for day surgery?
  - A: Bupivacaine 3.5-7 mg with fentanyl 10-25 mcg; discharge at about 4h vs 6h
- Q: How many day patients have moderate to severe pain at home, and for how long?
  - A: 20-40%, lasting 2-4 days
