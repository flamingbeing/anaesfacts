# Middle Ear Surgery

Microsurgery on the ear needing a bloodless field, a still patient, an intact graft and facial nerve monitoring, with a high PONV risk.

## Key points
- Poor airway access
- Optimise surgical field and minimise bleeding
- Protect the tympanic membrane (avoid coughing)
- Facial nerve monitoring
- PONV prophylaxis
- Possibly laser and microscope

## Procedures
- Mastoidectomy: removes infected mastoid air cells, usually from cholesteatoma
  - Simple: drainage through a cortical opening, via the ear or a post-auricular incision
  - Radical: removes the tympanic membrane and middle ear structures except the stapes (so a hearing aid can be used later)
  - Modified radical: keeps the tympanic membrane and ossicle remnants to preserve hearing
- Myringoplasty: isolated repair of the tympanic membrane
- Tympanoplasty: larger tympanic membrane repair plus repair of chronic middle ear changes; usually a temporalis fascia graft
- Ossiculoplasty (ossicular chain reconstruction): repair or partial replacement of one or more ossicles, e.g. stapedectomy
- Cochlear implant: electrode array placed in the cochlea

## Surgical complications
- Altered taste and transient facial palsy (more likely with large volumes of infiltrated LA)
- Rare: CSF leak, meningitis, persistent facial palsy

## Major considerations

### Airway access
- Head is far from the anaesthetist
- Airway covered and hard to reach

### Surgical field
- Bleeding obscures anatomy and is a risk factor for cholesteatoma recurrence
- Bleeding is mainly from the microvasculature: arterial pressure, small-vessel tone and venous pressure all matter
- Methods
  - Induced hypotension, e.g. remifentanil
  - Lower venous pressure: 15–20° head-up tilt; avoid tight tube ties and extreme neck rotation (jugular obstruction)
  - Avoid excess fluid, PEEP and hypercarbia

### Protecting the tympanic membrane
- Avoid coughing on emergence: TIVA, remifentanil
- Avoid nitrous oxide

### Facial nerve (CN VII) monitoring
- Nerve can be injured by sharp or blunt trauma, or by heat from high-speed burrs without contact
- Careful surgery is the main protection; monitoring is thought to lower the risk further
- EMG from orbicularis oris and oculi via intramuscular electrodes
  - No maintenance paralysis; short-acting neuromuscular blocker at induction only
- Monopolar probe stimulates the nerve directly to confirm position, function or continuity

### PONV
- High incidence
  - Contributors: younger patients, long procedures, drilling near the inner ear stimulating the vestibular system, suction-irrigation (caloric stimulation)
  - Retching or vomiting can disrupt the repair
- Aggressive multimodal prophylaxis: ondansetron plus dexamethasone (better combined)
  - Vestibular symptoms (common after cochlear implant or middle ear surgery): betahistine may help (histamine analogue, H1 agonist and H3 antagonist)
- TIVA reduces early PONV

### Lasers and microscope
- Laser (e.g. laser stapedectomy, cholesteatoma surgery): full laser precautions
- Microscope work on the ossicles needs an immobile patient: deep anaesthesia

## Preoperative assessment
- Children: usually older and healthy; often many previous anaesthetics and may be anxious
  - Sedative premedication if needed or requested
- Adults: usually young and fit
  - Exclude contraindications to hypotensive anaesthesia: cardiovascular disease, sickle cell disease, pregnancy, infancy <1 year, carotid stenosis
- Hearing loss can make communication and consent hard; may need a sign language interpreter

## Intraoperative management
- Position: slight head-up, head turned to the opposite side
- Airway: ETT or LMA
  - Intubate for: secure airway with poor head access, long procedure, controlled ventilation to avoid hypercapnia
  - Supraglottic airway for: less coughing on emergence, no neuromuscular blocker (facial monitoring), faster list turnover
  - ETT options: reinforced tube (less kinking) or preformed RAE tube
- Maintenance
  - TIVA for smoother emergence
  - Limit bleeding: avoid hypertension; 15-20° head-up and mild hypotension
    - Remifentanil infusion gives moderate hypotension, cuts volatile needs and avoids relaxant
    - Consider dexmedetomidine; alternatives magnesium or beta-blockers
    - Arterial line if hypotensive anaesthesia is used
    - Avoid excess fluid, PEEP and hypercarbia
    - Surgeon infiltrates LA with adrenaline superficially for vasoconstriction
  - Facial monitoring: no neuromuscular blocker; remifentanil
  - Avoid nitrous oxide: high PONV, and contraindicated in tympanoplasty because N2O diffuses into the middle ear, expanding it and risking membrane rupture or graft displacement
  - Two prophylactic antiemetics
- Emergence must be smooth with no rise in venous pressure
  - Minimise coughing, which can damage the repair; remifentanil may help

## Postoperative care
- PONV can disrupt grafts and prostheses: multimodal control
- Pain usually mild
- Children: oral paracetamol 15 mg/kg 4-hourly, ibuprofen 10 mg/kg 6-hourly, and an oral opioid (codeine phosphate 1 mg/kg 4-hourly or morphine sulphate 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly), plus an antiemetic

## Cochlear implant
- Usually done before two and a half years of age to help speech development
- Preoperative
  - Airway: a congenital syndrome may cause the deafness (e.g. Stickler, Klippel–Feil), often with a difficult airway
  - ECG: congenital sensorineural deafness can come with long QT, alone or as Jervell and Lange–Nielsen syndrome; screen with an ECG
- Intraoperative
  - Implant function is tested after insertion; little stimulation at this stage
  - TIVA does not interfere with elicited stapedius reflex threshold (ESRT) testing of the implant

## Flashcards
- Q: Why avoid nitrous oxide in tympanoplasty?
  - A: It diffuses into the middle ear, expanding it and risking tympanic membrane rupture or graft displacement
- Q: How much head-up tilt reduces bleeding in middle ear surgery?
  - A: 15-20°
- Q: Why avoid maintenance neuromuscular blockade in middle ear surgery?
  - A: Facial nerve EMG monitoring
- Q: Which ECG finding should be screened for before cochlear implant in congenital deafness?
  - A: Long QT (e.g. Jervell and Lange–Nielsen syndrome)
