# Intraoperative bleeding and patient blood management

Planning for and managing surgical blood loss: blood volume and allowable loss, estimating loss, transfusion triggers, massive transfusion and blood conservation.

## Quick summary
- Allowable blood loss (ABL): blood volume males 70 ml/kg, females 65 ml/kg
- Prepare for bleeding: large-bore access (RIC line, Mahurkar, PA sheath); rapid infuser (Hotline, Belmont)
- Massive ongoing bleeding: avoid hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy; TXA; cell saver

## Basic science

### Normal blood volume
- Conventional total blood volume (TBV): 70 ml/kg
- By sex: males 70 ml/kg, females 65 ml/kg
- By age (common simplified values)
  - Premature neonates: 95 ml/kg
  - Term neonates: 85 ml/kg
  - Infants: 80 ml/kg
  - Young children: 75 ml/kg
  - Teenagers and adults: 70 ml/kg

### Maximum allowable blood loss
- ABL = [EBV x (Hi - Hf)] / Hi
  - EBV: estimated blood volume
  - Hi: initial haemoglobin
  - Hf: final (lowest acceptable) haemoglobin

## Surgery with moderate to high bleeding risk

### Main concerns
- May need transfusion
- May become haemodynamically unstable

### Preoperative assessment
- Check the procedure-specific bleeding risk
- Blood conservation: check Hb and correct anaemia; check bleeding risk (platelets, PT/PTT)
- Make sure there is a valid group and crossmatch, with or without packed cells or other products on standby

### Intraoperative management
- Prepare for instability: good vascular access, arterial line, with or without CVP
  - Large-bore IV, or RIC line, Mahurkar or PA sheath
  - Warmers and rapid infusers: Barkey, Hotline, Level One or Belmont
- Blood conservation: avoid the lethal diamond (hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy, hypocalcaemia); TXA; cell saver
  - Consider ROTEM if blood loss is significant
  - Maximise tissue oxygen delivery (patient blood management principle)
- Monitor closely for bleeding
- Treat bleeding: colloids, transfusion

### Postoperative care
- Risk of instability: high dependency care for postoperative haemodynamic monitoring

## Estimating blood loss in theatre

### In practice
- Clinical: watch the field, listen to the sucker
- Chart losses: gauze, suction bottles
- Baseline and serial ABG for Hb

### Watching the surgical field for ongoing loss
- Look at the field
- A sucker running a lot means more bleeding
- If the surgeon is worried, the loss is usually significant

### Adding up all sites for total loss
- Soiled gauze
  - Visual estimate: most used but least accurate
    - Fully soaked 10x10 Raytec gauze: about 10 ml
    - Fully soaked 30x30 large swab ("penny towel"): about 100 ml
  - Gravimetric: weigh swabs and packs before and after use (used in transplant surgery)
  - Photometry
- Suction bottles and collection bags
  - Note how much washout was used and allow for it; irrigation makes estimates harder
- Hidden losses under drapes and on the floor
  - Tip: tape a green sheet to the floor to help count fluid

### Serial haematocrit or haemoglobin
- The fall from preoperative to postoperative haematocrit can estimate loss, as a % of TBV (like ABL)
- Limits
  - It reflects the cell:plasma ratio, not loss itself; fluid shifts and IV replacement alter it
  - Ongoing loss may not show immediately
  - Make sure the patient is well filled before taking the ABG
- A visual guide to blood in gauze was published in Trauma Monthly 2016

## Managing intraoperative bleeding

### Replacing loss before the transfusion trigger
- First replace with crystalloid or colloid to keep normovolaemia
  - Crystalloid: old teaching 3 ml per 1 ml blood; newer teaching 1.5 ml per 1 ml blood
  - Colloid: 1 ml per 1 ml blood (1:1)
  - Accepting early haemodilution lowers transfusion rates (and their incompatibility and infection risks)

### Transfusion trigger
- Transfuse when the risk of anaemia outweighs the risk of transfusion: Hb 7-10 g/dl (haematocrit 21-30%)
- General triggers
  - Fit and healthy: Hb 7
  - Renal failure, neurological disease, IHD: evidence unclear, so Hb 8
  - Ongoing bleeding: Hb may lag behind
- Trigger depends on
  - Comorbidity: higher with IHD
  - Type of surgery
  - Ongoing bleeding: choose a higher threshold if more loss is suspected (e.g. 9 instead of 8 in IHD); from then on, replace further loss with red cells to hold Hb at that level
- Volume to transfuse = (Hct desired - Hct current) x EBV / Hct of transfused blood
- Signs of poor oxygen delivery from anaemia are hard to spot under GA

### Transfusion
- Call for blood, check it, keep it on standby in the theatre fridge
- Warm blood with a Barkey warmer; consider Hotline or Belmont
- Consider keeping BP lower while bleeding continues

## Massive transfusion

### Definitions
- Most accepted: 10 or more units (≥10) of red cells in the first 24 hours
- Ultra-massive: 20 or more units (≥20) of red cells in 24 or 48 hours
- Other definitions of massive haemorrhage (LITFL)
  - More than 1 blood volume in 24 h
  - More than 50% of blood volume lost in 3 h
  - More than 150 ml/min blood loss

### Causes
- Trauma (ABC score predicts need for massive transfusion)
- GI bleeding
- Obstetric haemorrhage

### Massive transfusion protocols (MTPs)
- Vary between institutions; often FFP:platelets:red cells of 1:1:1 or 1:1:2
- Help keep to ideal haemostatic resuscitation, a 1:1:1 ratio of FFP, platelets and packed red cells

### Complications of massive transfusion
- Dilutional coagulopathy: treat with FFP and platelets, guided by platelet count and PT/PTT
  - Aim for haemostatic resuscitation: 1:1:1 ratio, ROTEM guided
  - ROTEM after the first or second MTP pack
  - Check PT, aPTT and platelets, or a viscoelastic test, after every five to seven units of red cells
  - Trauma: damage control resuscitation
- Hypothermia: warm all blood products
- Hypocalcaemia: citrate in stored blood binds calcium, which is a cofactor in both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways; consider calcium replacement

### Other haemostatic agents
- Tranexamic acid (TXA)
- Fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate
- Prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC)
- NovoSeven: recombinant factor 7 (VIIa)

## Crisis management: massive haemorrhage in theatre
- Call for help and the crash trolley
- Confirm the surgeon is actively controlling bleeding
- Secure the airway (ETT if needed); ventilate with 100% O2 until resolved
- Get 2 large-bore peripheral IV lines; run warmed fluid fast; actively warm the patient
- Pressure bag or rapid infuser; consider starting the cell saver
- Call the blood bank to activate the MTP and crossmatch early
- Consider antifibrinolytics
- Set up monitoring: arterial line, CVP, TEG, point-of-care blood gas (iSTAT)

## Preparing theatre for massive ongoing haemorrhage
- E.g. polytrauma, ruptured AAA
- Personnel: extra anaesthetic doctor and extra anaesthetic assistant
- Equipment
  - Large-bore IV or RIC line; Swan-Ganz sheath or Mahurkar
  - Arterial line before induction; CVC usually not needed
  - 2x ultrasound machines
  - Belmont rapid infuser
  - ROTEM in theatre
  - Cell saver
- Drugs
  - Cardiostable induction: ketamine, midazolam
  - Phenylephrine infusion; peripheral noradrenaline
  - TXA
  - Fibrinogen
  - Blood in the theatre fridge
- Goals
  - Avoid the lethal diamond
  - TXA, cell saver
  - BP control

## Patient blood management (PBM)
- Anaemia, bleeding and transfusion are three independent risk factors for poor outcome
- Three pillars
  - Optimise erythropoiesis (red cell mass and iron stores)
  - Minimise surgical blood loss and coagulopathic bleeding
  - Optimise physiological tolerance of anaemia while treatment starts
- Three phases: before (preoperative), during (intraoperative) and after (postoperative) treatment
- Most important in
  - Surgery with major blood loss (more than 1000 ml)
  - Patients who cannot receive blood: Jehovah's Witnesses, rare blood groups, multiple alloantibodies
  - Pre-existing anaemia
  - Bleeding disorders

## Blood conservation strategies
- Ways to reduce allogeneic transfusion; indicated e.g. in multilevel spine surgery such as scoliosis

### Preoperative
- Screen for and treat anaemia and coagulopathy
- Valid group and crossmatch, products on standby: prepare for possible transfusion
- Preoperative autologous donation (PAD): rarely used
  - Serial collection of 1–4 units of the patient's whole blood 2–5 weeks before surgery; last collection more than 72 hours before induction
- With or without acute normovolaemic haemodilution (ANH), especially for Jehovah's Witnesses

### Intraoperative
- Avoid the lethal diamond: coagulopathy, hypothermia, acidosis, hypocalcaemia
- Antifibrinolytics: tranexamic acid, aprotinin
- With or without cell salvage: if expected loss is more than 1000 ml
- With or without controlled (hypotensive) anaesthesia, e.g. MAP 60-65
- Operation-specific
  - Hip: regional anaesthesia where possible
  - Spine: avoid venous congestion at the surgical site (low PEEP, low intrathoracic pressure, avoid hypercarbia)
- Surgical: meticulous haemostasis, topical haemostatic agents, tourniquets

## Procedure-specific bleeding risk

### When to group and crossmatch
- Potential blood loss more than 500 ml (adults) or more than 10 ml/kg (children)
- Hb less than 10 g/dl
- Bleeding disorder

### Estimated surgical blood loss categories
- Minimal
- Low (less than 500 ml): minimally invasive surgery with low blood loss (under 200 ml)
- Moderate (500–1000 ml): moderately invasive surgery with fluid or blood loss up to 1 l, or moderate procedure-related morbidity
- High (more than 1 l): highly invasive surgery with loss over 1 l, or risk of major procedure-related morbidity or mortality

## Paediatric bleeding and transfusion

### Blood volume
- Premature newborn: 95 ml/kg
- Term newborn: 85 ml/kg
- Infants: 80 ml/kg
- Young children: 75 ml/kg
- Teenagers and adults: 70 ml/kg
- Calculate ABL as above

### Blood products
- Packed red cells: 4 ml/kg raises Hb by 1 g/dl
  - Higher targets in neonates, e.g. 8-12 g/dl
- Platelets: 5-10 ml/kg raises the count by 30-50 x 10^9/l
  - Aim above 50k (50 x 10^9/l) for surgery
- FFP: 10 to 20 ml/kg
  - Aim INR >1.5, PT/aPTT less than 1.5x normal (as in the note)
- Cryoprecipitate: 5 ml/kg, or 1-2 units per 10 kg
  - Aim fibrinogen more than 1 g/l

### Massive transfusion in children
- Defined as more than 40 ml/kg transfused (blood volume in children over 1 month old is about 80 ml/kg)
