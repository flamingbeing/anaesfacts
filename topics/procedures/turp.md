# Transurethral Resection of the Prostate (TURP)

Endoscopic prostate resection with continuous irrigation; main risks are TURP syndrome, bleeding and hypothermia.

## Procedure
- Usually for benign prostatic hypertrophy causing urethral compression and obstructive symptoms
- Resectoscope passed via a cystoscope cuts and coagulates tissue protruding into the prostatic urethra
- Continuous irrigation creates the risk of TURP syndrome
  - Monopolar resection with glycine is being replaced by bipolar resection with saline
- Very large prostate (>100g): simple open prostatectomy may have fewer complications
- Complications rise with resection time >1hr
  - If likely to exceed an hour, consider resecting one lobe only and doing the other later
- Bipolar TURP lowers overall complications, transfusion and TURP syndrome

## Preoperative assessment
- Often elderly, with comorbidities and polypharmacy
- Check creatinine and serum sodium
  - Consider postponing if sodium is markedly low: irrigant absorption will lower it further
- Fluid absorption puts heart failure patients at particular risk: optimise beforehand
- Assess mental state and communication: spinal is hard if the patient is confused or deaf

## Anaesthetic technique
- General anaesthesia
  - Positive pressure ventilation raises venous pressure and may reduce irrigant absorption
  - Muscle relaxation or deep anaesthesia may be preferred: coughing or movement risks prostatic capsule rupture
  - Intraoperative fentanyl or morphine plus multimodal analgesia (paracetamol, NSAID, tramadol or codeine) is usually enough; postoperative opioids rarely needed
- Regional anaesthesia
  - Needs a T10 level
  - Awake patient: changes in mental state and fluid overload are easier to spot, so TURP syndrome may be detected earlier
  - Some, not all, studies show less blood loss

## Intraoperative management
- Careful fluids; watch for TURP syndrome; never use glucose
  - If spinal causes hypotension, consider a colloid
  - Replace blood loss with colloid; be ready to transfuse if Hb falls below the trigger
- Blood loss is hard to judge
  - Can in theory be calculated from Hb and the volume of discarded irrigant
  - Usually judged by the colour and volume of fluid, which can mislead
  - Bedside Hb (e.g. HemoCue) is useful
  - Related to weight of tissue resected (usually 15–60g), resection time and surgeon experience
- Hypothermia with large irrigant volumes: warm fluid to 37°C
- Antibiotic prophylaxis usually needed: ceftriaxone
- Lithotomy position

## Postoperative management
- Saline bladder irrigation via a three-way catheter for about 24hr until bleeding settles
  - Accidental slowing of irrigation can cause clot retention
- Usually little pain; catheter discomfort or bladder spasm can be troublesome
  - Severe pain suggests clot retention or bladder perforation
  - Clot retention: tense, painful bladder and vagal symptoms; needs washout, sometimes under anaesthesia
- Bleeding may continue and need resuscitation and return to theatre
- Check FBC, creatinine and electrolytes the next day

## Postoperative complications
- TURP syndrome (15 mins to 24 hours from start)
- Haemorrhage
- Clot retention
- Bladder spasm

## TURP syndrome
- Fluid overload with iso-osmolar hyponatraemia from absorbed irrigant (via prostatic venous sinuses or visceral perforation)
- Incidence 1–8% (more for mild cases, often masked by GA); severe cases have 25% mortality

## Irrigation fluids
- Glycine: clear, cheap, non-conductive, easy to sterilise, non-haemolytic
- Saline: conducts electricity but otherwise ideal; not a problem with bipolar diathermy

## Pathophysiology
- Hypo-osmolality: main cause of CNS features
  - Glycine 1.5% is hypotonic at 220 mOsmol/kg
- Glycine toxicity: inhibitory neurotransmitter; visual disturbance and seizures
- Fluid overload: acute pulmonary oedema and reflex bradycardia
- Secondary effects: natriuresis (worsens hyponatraemia), hyperammonaemia, coagulopathy

## Clinical features
- Timing: 15 minutes to 24 hours from start
- CNS: restlessness, headache, burning sensation in face and hands, visual disturbance, seizures, coma
- Cardiovascular and respiratory: bradycardia, early hypertension then late hypotension, tachypnoea, hypoxia, pulmonary oedema
- Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

## Risk reduction
- Surgical
  - Limit resection time (ideally <60 mins)
  - Irrigation bag height <70cm to limit hydrostatic pressure
  - Bipolar diathermy allows 0.9% saline irrigation, removing glycine toxicity and hypo-osmolality
- Anaesthetic
  - Spinal (gold standard)
    - Awake monitoring for early CNS signs
    - Early recognition of bladder perforation as new referred pain (shoulder or periumbilical)
    - Needs a T10 level for bladder distension, although prostatic pain is sacral
  - GA: common in practice, but masks features until severe cardiovascular collapse or delayed emergence

## Acute treatment
- Stop irrigation and finish surgery quickly
- Support airway, breathing and circulation; treat seizures with benzodiazepines or magnesium
- Furosemide for fluid overload or pulmonary oedema
- Hypertonic saline (3%) for severe hyponatraemia (<120 mmol/L) or neurological crisis

## Holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP)
- Lower morbidity and short hospital stay
- Useful for large prostates
- Penetration only 0.5–1 mm, so tissue damage is more superficial: much lower risk of bleeding and TURP syndrome
- TURP syndrome with HoLEP and other enucleation methods is only anecdotal, as saline is the irrigant

## Flashcards
- Q: Above what resection time do TURP complications increase?
  - A: More than 1 hour.
- Q: What block height does spinal for TURP need?
  - A: T10.
- Q: What is the osmolality of 1.5% glycine?
  - A: 220 mOsmol/kg (hypotonic).
- Q: What irrigation bag height limits absorption?
  - A: Below 70 cm.
- Q: When is 3% hypertonic saline given in TURP syndrome?
  - A: Severe hyponatraemia (<120 mmol/L) or neurological crisis.
