# Shoulder and Humerus Surgery

Often arthroscopic, often day case, very painful: GA (LMA often enough) plus interscalene block, with care in the beach-chair position.

## Procedures
- Most common: acromioplasty (subacromial decompression), stabilisation, adhesiolysis (frozen shoulder release), rotator cuff repair; usually arthroscopic
  - Arthroscopy uses two or three ports
  - Posterior port (below and medial to the posterolateral acromion) is the main viewing port
  - Anterior and/or lateral ports for instruments, placed according to the operation
- Open procedures: glenohumeral arthroplasty (total or partial), open stabilisation (e.g. Latarjet-Bristow), open cuff repair, most fracture surgery

## Preoperative assessment
- Find the cause of the shoulder problem; look for rheumatoid arthritis

## Positioning
- Supine with head-up tilt, lateral, or beach-chair
- Head-up, sitting or beach-chair
  - Risk of cerebral hypoperfusion: consider arterial line transduced at the external auditory canal, or cerebral oximetry; aim for a higher MAP
  - Venous return falls; in poor cardiovascular reserve, sit the patient up slowly
  - Watch for the Bezold-Jarisch reflex: bradycardia, hypotension, even cardiac arrest
  - Phenylephrine infusion often needed
  - Risk of air embolism
- Head is away from the anaesthetist, so secure the airway well
  - Often easier to intubate (south-facing RAE or armoured tube); LMA for shorter cases
  - Long breathing circuit and gas sampling lines needed

## Anaesthetic technique
- Usually GA; LMA often enough, even in beach-chair
- Regional anaesthesia is a useful adjunct; interscalene block is the block of choice
  - Interscalene alone can be the sole technique, especially for arthroscopic surgery

## Lines and access
- IV access in the opposite arm (long extension) or ankle/foot
- If BP cuff is on the same arm as the IV cannula (other arm is operative), use an anti-reflux valve and stop the cuff before giving propofol

## Postoperative care
- Arthroscopic (especially cuff repair and stabilisation) and open surgery both cause moderate to severe pain; among the most painful day-case operations
  - Unpredictable, may last days, worst in the first 48hr
  - One fifth of patients rate the pain "the worst pain imaginable" once the block wears off
- Preoperative interscalene block is often the most useful
  - Interscalene catheter for prolonged analgesia after major arthroplasty
  - If interscalene is impractical: surgical LA infiltration around the joint; a subacromial catheter can give further LA after surgery (works well for Bankart and capsular shift)
- Usually day surgery

## Shoulder innervation
- Sensory supply from the cervical plexus (C3,4) and brachial plexus (C5,6)
- Joint: mainly suprascapular nerve (upper trunk) and axillary nerve (posterior cord)
  - Minor: lateral pectoral, musculocutaneous and subscapular nerves (BJAEd 2019, ATOTW 2021)
- Skin of the shoulder cape, upper chest and part of the AC and SC joints: supraclavicular nerves (cervical plexus, C3,4)
- Skin distal to the glenohumeral joint: superior lateral cutaneous nerve of arm (axillary), medial cutaneous nerve of arm (medial cord), lateral cutaneous branch of the second intercostal nerve

## Regional options
- Brachial plexus block
  - Interscalene (ISB): gold standard and most used
    - Main problem is phrenic nerve block, hence the alternatives
    - Contraindicated in respiratory compromise
  - Superior trunk block: more distal, lower phrenic risk
  - Supraclavicular brachial plexus block has been used, but may miss the suprascapular nerve, which leaves the plexus more proximally
- Selective nerve blocks (analgesia only, not a sole anaesthetic as not all nerves are covered)
  - "Shoulder block": combined suprascapular and axillary nerve block (CSANB)
    - Inferior to ISB; mainly for when ISB is contraindicated or declined
    - Very few block-specific complications reported so far
  - Suprascapular block alone: worse than ISB, better than intra-articular infiltration
- Supraclavicular nerve block
  - Adds skin cover over the shoulder cape and ACJ
  - Used to be covered by proximal spread from interscalene injection; lower ultrasound-guided volumes spread less
  - After ISB, withdraw the needle in the same plane to between SCM and scalenus medius and inject

## Bankart and capsular shift (recurrent dislocation)
- Usually less painful than cuff repair or open acromioplasty; may hurt more in large, muscular patients

## Rotator cuff repair
- Often extremely painful: interscalene block useful
- Consider PCA; give a morphine loading dose during surgery
- Consider an interscalene catheter with LA infusion

## Total shoulder replacement
- Time: 2-3hr
- Pain: severe (+++ to ++++)
- Blood loss: 250-500ml
- Position: supine, head-up, beach-chair (deck-chair)
- Technique: ETT and IPPV, interscalene block
- Surgery
  - Anterior deltopectoral incision from the coracoid towards the deltoid tuberosity
  - Beach-chair improves access and lowers venous pressure and bleeding; lateral allows arm traction
- Preoperative assessment
  - Often elderly; severe rheumatoid disease common
  - Ask about respiratory reserve if planning ISB (some diaphragm function lost for several hours)
  - Check airway and neck movement, especially in rheumatoid; some need fibreoptic intubation
- Intraoperative management
  - Consider ISB before induction
  - IV and BP cuff on the opposite arm, long extension
  - Preformed south-facing ETT
  - Hypotension common when sitting up
  - With ISB, anaesthesia is usually uneventful; breakthrough may occur in the glenoid phase (fibres from T2 not always blocked)
  - Without ISB: load with morphine, surgeon infiltrates LA (20–30mL of 0.25% bupivacaine)
  - Antibiotic prophylaxis
- Postoperative care
  - Pain worst in the first 24hr; PCA or intermittent morphine usually enough
  - NSAIDs useful

## Flashcards
- Q: Which two nerves supply most of the shoulder joint?
  - A: Suprascapular nerve and axillary nerve
- Q: Main drawback of interscalene block?
  - A: Phrenic nerve block; contraindicated in respiratory compromise
- Q: Where should the arterial transducer be levelled in the beach-chair position?
  - A: At the external auditory canal (to reflect cerebral perfusion)
- Q: LA infiltration for total shoulder replacement without ISB?
  - A: 20–30mL of 0.25% bupivacaine by the surgeon, plus morphine loading
- Q: Expected blood loss and duration of total shoulder replacement?
  - A: 250-500ml over 2-3hr
