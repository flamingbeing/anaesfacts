# Paediatric plastic surgery

Lacerations, otoplasty, cleft lip and palate, craniofacial surgery and free flaps in children: shared or difficult airways, PONV after ear surgery, major bleeding in craniofacial work, and "warm and wet" physiology for flaps.

## Lacerations
- Head, limb and fingertip lacerations are a large part of on-call paediatric work
- Surgery: explore for deeper damage, debride, clean and suture
- LA alone is often poorly tolerated, especially by young children, so GA is often preferred

### Preoperative assessment
- Usually fit and healthy
- Delayed until fasted, often on a planned trauma list; evening cases are usually for organisational reasons
- IV cannula may already be in for antibiotics or analgesia

### Anaesthetic technique
- IV or gas induction
- Spontaneous ventilation via LMA; volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
- Digital or ring block works very well for digits (some surgeons do it); otherwise surgical LA infiltration; add NSAID and/or paracetamol
- Tourniquet often used; minimal blood loss
- May become a long, complex nerve or tendon repair if deep damage is found

### Postoperative care
- Low analgesic needs: paracetamol and NSAID, with or without a weak opioid
- Usually home later the same day or next morning, unless complex or needing IV antibiotics

## Otoplasty
- For prominent ears (bat ears) and microtia, the two commonest congenital ear malformations; either can be unilateral or bilateral
- Prominent ears: usually isolated; antihelical fold reshaped through an elliptical incision behind the ear
- Microtia: may be part of hemifacial microsomia (asymmetric mandible, possible airway problems); reconstruction often uses harvested costal cartilage
- Complications: haematoma, asymmetry, infection, prominent scar
- Age 6 years and over
- Male to female 2:1

### Preoperative assessment
- Mostly healthy
- A few microtia patients are hard to intubate conventionally (most have an easy airway) and may need fibreoptic intubation
- Sedative premedication if needed

### Anaesthetic technique
- Older children: IV induction unless airway problems expected
- Airway: armoured LMA or south-facing preformed RAE tube; ETT needed if costal cartilage is harvested
- IPPV or spontaneous ventilation
- Volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide; avoid nitrous oxide with costal cartilage harvest (small pneumothorax risk)
- High PONV incidence: ondansetron 100 mcg/kg IV (max 4 mg)
- Maintenance fluid; 20 mL/kg bolus often given to reduce PONV
- Analgesia, in combination:
  - LA with adrenaline infiltration (also aids haemostasis)
  - Great auricular nerve block: 2 mL 0.5% bupivacaine with or without adrenaline, injected superficially where a lateral line from the upper border of the cricoid crosses the posterior border of sternocleidomastoid
    - Nerve from the superficial cervical plexus (C3) becomes superficial here, then supplies the external ear and mastoid; useful for otoplasty and mastoid surgery
  - Opioid (weigh against PONV): morphine 100-150 mcg/kg, fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV
  - NSAID: diclofenac 1 mg/kg rectal
  - Paracetamol: 15 mg/kg IV or 40 mg/kg rectal

### Postoperative care
- Usually straightforward; maintenance fluid until drinking and free of PONV
- If PONV occurs, use a different antiemetic from the one given in theatre
- Oral paracetamol, NSAID and opioid (e.g. oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly)
- CXR after costal cartilage harvest to exclude pneumothorax
- Low rate of bleeding needing re-exploration
- Simple otoplasty for prominent ears often a day case

## Cleft lip and palate
- Commonest congenital orofacial abnormalities; diverse group
  - Cleft lip and palate: 1:600 live births
  - Isolated cleft palate: 1:1000 live births
  - More common in Far Eastern and Asian populations
  - Cleft lip alone 15%, cleft lip and palate 45%, isolated cleft palate 40%
  - Male to female 2:1 for cleft lip and palate; 1:2 for isolated cleft palate
- Embryology: failure (or breakdown) of fusion of the nasal and maxillary processes and palatine shelves at 6-8 weeks' gestation
  - Palate fuses front to back, so incomplete clefts tend to be posterior
- Cleft lip: unilateral (left more than right; either side commoner than bilateral) or bilateral; often with cleft palate or alveolus
- Cleft palate types:
  - Submucous (occult): mildest, no visible cleft, palatal muscles not united
  - Incomplete: soft palate only
  - Complete: soft and hard palate, may include the maxillary alveolus
  - More severe clefts carry higher risk of postoperative respiratory complications

### Aetiology
- Familial; families need genetic counselling
  - Cleft palate may be autosomal dominant with variable penetrance; an affected first-degree relative raises risk twenty-fold
- Environmental: maternal epilepsy, infection, drugs (steroids, benzodiazepines, anticonvulsants), possibly folate deficiency
- Usually isolated, but 10-20% have other anomalies (especially isolated cleft palate); isolated cleft lip rarely has
  - Syndromes: Pierre Robin sequence (cleft palate in 80%, retrognathia, posteriorly displaced tongue), Treacher Collins (cleft in 28%), Klippel-Feil (cleft in 15%), Stickler, Down's, hemifacial microsomia (Goldenhar), velocardiofacial syndrome, fetal alcohol syndrome
  - Skeletal anomalies of digits and limbs
  - Neural defects: encephalocoele, cervical vertebral synostosis
  - Congenital heart disease (5-10%)

### Complications of the cleft
- Feeding problems (cannot suckle)
- Nasal speech
- Eustachian tube dysfunction and hearing loss
- URTI and chronic otitis media
- Subglottic stenosis

### Surgical treatment
- Cleft lip repaired at about 3 months
  - Delay lowers anaesthetic risk and allows other anomalies to be diagnosed
  - Usually a mucosal advancement flap; complex bilateral lip and nasal floor repairs may need nasal stents, which can obstruct the airway
- Cleft palate repaired at 9-12 months (before speech develops)
  - Aims: appearance, normal speech, normal dental development
  - Lateral tissue mobilised for midline closure, including apposition of levator palati muscles (elevate the soft palate, close off the nasopharynx, prevent nasal speech)
  - E.g. Furlow's palatoplasty (double-reversing Z-plasty)
- Later complication: velopharyngeal insufficiency, may need pharyngoplasty or pharyngeal flap

### Preoperative assessment
- Look for associated conditions affecting anaesthesia or airway
- Recent or developing respiratory infection: usually defer (airway may be marginal after surgery)
- Chronic airway obstruction or OSA increases postoperative airway risk
  - Overnight oximetry before surgery; refer for formal sleep study if hypoxic episodes
- Possible physiological anaemia at 3-6 months; group and save (major bleeding rare)
- May have had multiple previous operations

### Anaesthetic technique
- No sedative premedication usually
- Inhalational induction; antisialogogue if wanted
- Intubation required, usually south-facing preformed RAE tube
  - Usually easy; occasionally difficult depending on syndrome: have difficult airway equipment ready
  - Keep the proximal part of the laryngoscope blade out of the cleft, or intubation becomes difficult
- IPPV common; spontaneous breathing possible
- Volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide; remifentanil cuts volatile needs and speeds emergence
- Maintenance fluid; replace volume if needed; group and crossmatch may be needed
- Temperature control rarely a problem
- Cleft lip repair: throat pack (usually placed by the anaesthetist)
- Cleft palate repair:
  - Throat pack (usually placed by the surgeon); check for ETT kinking or obstruction during placement
  - Shoulder roll to extend the neck for access
  - Gentle suction under direct vision without damaging the repair
  - Smooth emergence: crying and agitation can cause bleeding
  - Awake extubation
- Analgesia:
  - LA with adrenaline infiltration (mainly for haemostasis)
  - Opioid: morphine 100-150 mcg/kg, fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV
  - NSAID: diclofenac 1 mg/kg rectal
  - Paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV or 40 mg/kg rectal
  - Infraorbital nerve block for cleft lip:
    - Terminal branch of the trigeminal nerve; supplies upper lip skin and mucosa, lower eyelid, skin between them and side of the nose
    - Lies halfway between the midpoint of the palpebral fissure and the angle of the mouth, about 7.5 mm lateral to the side of the nose
    - Needle perpendicular to skin until bone is felt, withdraw slightly, aspirate, inject 1-2 mL 0.5% bupivacaine with 1:200000 adrenaline
    - Do not enter the infraorbital foramen

### Postoperative care
- After palate repair: bleeding (rarely needs re-exploration) and upper airway obstruction
- 10% show some airway obstruction, especially with a tight or lengthened palate
  - Causes: narrowed upper airway, clot, tongue swelling from the mouth gag
  - May need a nasopharyngeal airway or intubation and IPPV
  - A tongue suture may be left to pull the tongue forward
  - High-risk children (e.g. severe Pierre Robin) may go to ICU intubated for 24-48 hours until swelling settles
- Pulse oximetry on air to detect obstruction
- Maintenance fluid until feeding (usually early)
- Paracetamol, NSAID and oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly; some centres use a low-dose IV morphine infusion in HDU

## Craniofacial surgery
- Craniosynostosis: premature closure of cranial sutures, causing abnormal bone growth
  - Scaphocephaly: sagittal
  - Plagiocephaly: one coronal or lambdoid
  - Brachycephaly: coronal
  - Oxycephaly: coronal and sagittal
  - Trigonocephaly: metopic
- Incidence about 1:2000 live births
- Faciocraniosynostoses (much rarer): skull and facial abnormalities, especially midface hypoplasia
  - Multiple sutures; abnormal eye position, proptosis, breathing problems
  - Often syndromic with syndactyly: Apert, Crouzon, Pfeiffer
- Consequences:
  - Raised ICP if untreated, especially with multiple sutures
  - Papilloedema from raised ICP can impair vision
  - Breathing problems in faciocraniosynostoses: optimise first (treat choanal atresia, CPAP)
- Surgery relieves ICP, corrects deformity and improves appearance
  - Frontocranial remodelling restores forehead and vault and allows brain growth
  - Facial advancement for midface hypoplasia
- Timing:
  - Simple cases: first year of life
  - Complex: frontal and cranial surgery at 4-12 months; facial retrusion corrected around 5 years (severe) or around 12 years after final dentition (moderate)
  - Most need Le Fort I advancement of upper teeth in adolescence plus further cosmetic procedures
- Major blood loss possible; orbital dissection may trigger the oculocardiac reflex

### Preoperative assessment
- Multidisciplinary craniofacial team
- Thorough airway history: feeding difficulty, failure to thrive, sleep disturbance, daytime sleepiness, developmental problems
- Maxillary hypoplasia: ENT and respiratory optimisation with overnight sleep studies for upper airway obstruction or OSA
- Faciocraniosynostoses often need airway improvement first: choanal dilatation (with or without bony work), nasal stents, nasopharyngeal airways, nocturnal nasal CPAP
- Severe airway problems may need tracheostomy to allow growth before surgery
- Previous anaesthetic records (MRI, CT, choanal dilatation) show airway, intubation and access difficulties
- Crossmatch blood

### Anaesthetic technique
- Sedative premedication if no respiratory compromise or raised ICP
- Inhalational induction often preferred: many anaesthetics; limb anomalies make venous access hard
- Typical pattern: difficult mask airway but easy intubation; intubation usually straightforward
- Maxillary hypoplasia: obstruction after induction; marked proptosis makes a mask seal hard
- Intubation and IPPV required; armoured or preformed tube depending on surgery
  - Secure fixation essential; for maxillary osteotomy, mandibular intradental wiring is most secure
  - Throat pack may be needed
- Major cases: arterial line, femoral venous line, urinary catheter
- Positioning: avoid excess neck rotation (cerebral venous congestion); meticulous pressure care (foam or gel mattress)
- Moderate head-up tilt
- Balanced opioid and volatile technique; remifentanil infusion 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min useful
- Hypotensive anaesthesia used cautiously: cerebral perfusion concern with head-up and raised ICP
- Blood loss can be very large, especially infants and complex surgery
  - Mostly during skin incision and craniotomy, and postoperatively
  - Can be torrential from large abnormal veins; most perioperative morbidity comes from haemorrhage
  - At least two free-flowing, secured cannulae
  - Meticulous monitoring; maintain normovolaemia
  - Cell salvage reduces transfusion

### Postoperative care
- Most extubated and nursed in HDU; some need IPPV, especially after facial surgery
- Maintenance fluids
- Bleeding may continue: babies may lose 10% of blood volume into drains or head bandages
  - Regular cardiovascular review and serial FBC; transfuse as indicated
- Analgesia: paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly, ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly (if bleeding is not a concern), oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly
- Pain scores surprisingly low despite marked swelling and bilateral black eyes

## Free flap surgery
- Free tissue transfer (usually musculocutaneous, sometimes with bone) for large wounds that cannot be closed primarily; circulation restored by microvascular anastomosis
  - Muscle flap looks better than a skin graft and resists infection better
  - Uncommon in children, usually after trauma
  - Success rate better than adults (better vessels, higher blood flow)
  - Same principles apply to replantation of digits or limbs

### Phases
- Flap raised and vessels clamped
- Primary ischaemia: flow stops, metabolism becomes anaerobic
- Reperfusion when anastomoses are complete and clamps released; ideally reverses the derangement
- Secondary ischaemia after reperfusion: more damaging than primary
  - Intravascular thrombosis, interstitial oedema, raised local fibrinogen and platelets
  - Skin tolerates 10-12 hours of ischaemia; muscle changes become irreversible after 4 hours

### Causes of flap failure
- Arterial anastomosis inadequate, in spasm or thrombosed
- Venous anastomosis defective, in spasm, thrombosed or compressed
- Oedema: excess crystalloid, extreme haemodilution, handling trauma, long ischaemia time
  - Flap has no lymphatic drainage, so oedema accumulates easily

### Physiological goals
- Long surgery (6-12 hours), several incisions, significant blood, fluid and heat loss
- Hypovolaemia, vasoconstriction and hypothermia reduce flap flow and promote failure
- Aim: vasodilatation, good perfusion pressure, low viscosity
- Vasodilatation (vessel radius is the main determinant of flow):
  - Normothermia: cold causes vasoconstriction and raises haematocrit and plasma viscosity
  - Normovolaemia or mild hypervolaemia: losses at donor and recipient sites; modest hypervolaemia raises cardiac output, lowers sympathetic tone and dilates flap vessels
  - Avoid hypocapnia
  - Surgeon may apply topical vasodilators (e.g. papaverine) for vessel spasm
  - Sympathetic block: epidural with postoperative infusion is ideal (also less bleeding, faster recovery); alternatives brachial plexus or interpleural block depending on site
  - Good analgesia lowers catecholamines and pain-driven vasoconstriction
- Perfusion pressure: adequate anaesthetic depth and fluid
- Viscosity:
  - Haemodilution to haematocrit 30% improves flow; little further gain below this
  - Lower haematocrit may cut oxygen delivery more than it helps flow
- Usually combined regional and general anaesthesia; continue the block after surgery to prevent small-vessel vasoconstriction

### Preoperative assessment
- Surgical history, previous anaesthetic and analgesic experience (often several previous procedures)
- Coexisting conditions
- FBC, U&E, crossmatch
- Sedative premedication usually given

### Anaesthetic technique
- Induction as preferred
- Intubation and IPPV; avoid hypocapnia (vasoconstriction)
- Volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide; remifentanil 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min reduces volatile needs and gives mild hypotension
- Monitoring: standard plus arterial line, CVP, urine output, core and peripheral temperature
- Serial arterial blood gases and haematocrit
- Long case: careful positioning of limbs and joints; protect pressure areas and eyes
- Regional block for the recipient site (sympathetic block) if possible
- Moderate hypotension while the flap is raised improves the field and reduces bleeding; titrated volatile is ideal (direct vasodilator)
- Strict fluid management: maintenance, insensible losses, blood loss
  - Target haematocrit around 30% with maintained cardiac output
  - Excess fluid (especially crystalloid) collects in the flap, causing swelling and venous obstruction
- Core-peripheral temperature gap under 2°C suggests a warm, filled, vasodilated patient
  - Warm theatre, warming mattress and blanket, fluid warmer
- At reperfusion: normothermic, vasodilated, slightly hypervolaemic ("warm and wet"), cardiac output above normal
- Vasodilators or inotropes rarely needed
- Smooth emergence avoids sympathetic surge and vasoconstriction

### Postoperative care
- Postoperative regional techniques increasingly used
  - Epidural infusion ideal for lower limb flaps
  - Epidural LA for the recipient site plus IV morphine infusion or PCA for the donor site as needed
  - Femoral, fascia iliaca or 3-in-1 blocks for thigh donor sites (common skin graft site)
- Paracetamol, NSAID and antiemetic
- Maintenance fluids; colloid boluses as needed to maintain CVP and urine output
- Frequent flap checks: early detection of ischaemia or congestion may save the flap

## Flashcards
- Q: When are cleft lip and cleft palate usually repaired?
  - A: Lip at about 3 months; palate at 9-12 months
- Q: Infraorbital block dose for cleft lip repair?
  - A: 1-2 mL 0.5% bupivacaine with 1:200000 adrenaline, not entering the foramen
- Q: What proportion develop airway obstruction after cleft palate repair?
  - A: 10%
- Q: Target haematocrit for free flap surgery?
  - A: About 30%
- Q: Core-peripheral temperature gap target in free flap surgery?
  - A: Less than 2°C
