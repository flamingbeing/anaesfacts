# Intracranial Aneurysm Clipping and Coiling

Prevent rupture by controlling transmural pressure (MAP minus ICP), protect the brain, and be ready for sudden intraoperative rupture.

## Anaesthetic goals
- Prevent rupture or rebleeding: adequate depth and analgesia, BP control, fluid management
- Neuroprotection against secondary brain injury (physical, physiological, pharmacological)
  - Especially in high-grade SAH, where autoregulation may be lost
- Manage neurological and systemic complications

## Preoperative assessment and optimisation
- Usually managed in high dependency or ICU
- Assess the underlying problem
  - Detailed neurological examination: GCS, pupils, deficits, signs of raised ICP
  - Review imaging: CT brain (SAH grade), 4-vessel angiogram (aneurysm anatomy)
  - Treatment so far
  - Neurological and systemic complications of SAH (myocardial ischaemia, arrhythmias, pulmonary oedema)
  - Bloods: FBC (Hb), urea and electrolytes, creatinine, Ca/Mg/PO4 (correct abnormalities), PT/PTT (correct coagulopathy), group and crossmatch
- Optimise
  - BP control
  - Correct fluid status and electrolytes
  - Isotonic saline, not hypotonic fluids
  - Valid group and crossmatch; blood products available
  - Consent covering GA, surgical risks and postoperative care
  - Avoid sedative premedication if ICP is raised
  - Continue osmotherapy and ICP management, seizure prophylaxis or treatment, and steroids
  - Confirm a high dependency or ICU bed

## Specific preoperative issues after SAH
- Neurological state: from mild drowsiness to coma
  - May already be intubated for airway protection and ventilation
- Raised ICP: acute hydrocephalus and high ICP are common
- Cardiac: ischaemia, arrhythmias, LV dysfunction, pulmonary oedema
  - Review ECG, echo if done, and cardiac biomarkers
- Seizures: continue antiepileptics through surgery
- Hyponatraemia: common, often from SIADH, especially with vasospasm
  - Treat with hypertonic saline, not fluid restriction (restriction raises the risk of ischaemia from vasospasm)
- Vasospasm: symptomatic in 20 to 30% and can cause delayed cerebral ischaemia
  - Rare early; usually starts after day 3, peaks at days 7 to 8, lasts up to 21 days
  - Hypovolaemia worsens ischaemia risk; aim for euvolaemia
- Anaemia: common and linked to worse outcome; no defined transfusion threshold
  - Aim for Hb of at least 8 g/dl; outcomes seem best at 8 to 11.5 g/dl
- Nimodipine 60 mg every 4 hours orally or by nasogastric tube for all SAH patients; continue during surgery
- Premedication
  - Elective cases: anxiolysis such as midazolam 1 to 2 mg IV, titrated
  - After SAH: tailor to mental state

## Planning with the surgeon (clipping)
- Aneurysms can be clipped at craniotomy, treated endovascularly, or both
- GA is used for all intracranial vascular procedures
- Brain relaxation and ICP control, including CSF drainage if an EVD is present
  - Use with care: osmotic agents (mannitol, hypertonic saline) or brisk CSF drainage before the dura is open lower ICP suddenly
  - This raises transmural pressure (TMP = MAP minus ICP) across the aneurysm wall and the risk of rupture
  - Overzealous hyperventilation can cause cerebral ischaemia
- BP goals
  - Unruptured aneurysm, or ruptured with presumed normal ICP (normal neurological examination): systolic BP at or below the patient's normal, maximum 140 mmHg; MAP at least 60 mmHg
  - Avoid hypertension from noxious stimuli or from vasopressors
  - With an ICP monitor: keep CPP (MAP minus ICP) at least 50 to 60 mmHg
- Plan for temporary clipping
- Aneurysm site and patient position
- Expected surgical difficulty
- Plan for intraoperative rupture
- Neurophysiological monitoring (EEG, evoked potentials) affects choice and timing of anaesthetic drugs and NMBAs

## Clipping: overview
- GA with armoured ETT
- Arterial line and CVP
- TIVA or volatile
- Postoperative neuro-observation in high dependency or ICU
- Main issues
  - BP control to prevent rupture, brain relaxation, neuroprotection
  - Possible major blood loss (200-2000 ml): group and crossmatch 2 units packed cells
  - Long operation: careful positioning, judicious fluids, temperature control, thromboprophylaxis
  - Transfer of an intubated ICU patient

## Clipping: monitoring and equipment
- Arterial line before induction (under sedation or local); CVP after induction
  - Elective cases: arterial line can wait until after induction if the team judges rupture risk at induction to be low
- Large-bore IV access, resuscitation drugs, BP drugs (vasopressors for hypotension; beta-blockers or SNP for hypertension)
- Temperature probe, warmed fluids, forced-air warming
- If blood loss expected: rapid infusers (Hotline, Level 1) and blood in theatre
- Urinary catheter, BIS, thromboprophylaxis
- Continue any neurocritical care monitors already in place: EVD, jugular venous saturation (SjVO2) catheter, brain tissue oxygen probe

## Clipping: induction
- Airway and ventilation
  - Good preoxygenation
  - ETT because of reduced GCS, long surgery, limited airway access and possible instability
  - Controlled ventilation to optimise oxygenation and CO2 and avoid raised ICP
  - Videolaryngoscope to ease intubation
  - Armoured tube against kinking; no ETT ties; secure well
- Drugs: smooth induction; avoid hypertension (rupture risk) and hypotension (CPP)
  - Deep anaesthesia, full paralysis, generous opioid, topical anaesthesia to the glottis, brief laryngoscopy
  - Treat hypertension at once: more IV hypnotic, fast vasodilator (nitroprusside, nicardipine), stop the stimulus
- Positioning: Mayfield 3-pin head fixation

## Clipping: maintenance
- Volatile or propofol, chosen by:
  - Degree of pre-existing intracranial hypertension
  - Need for brain relaxation
  - Neuromonitoring in use
  - Comorbidities
- BP goals: systolic at or below normal, maximum 140 mmHg; MAP at least 60 mmHg; CPP at least 50 to 60 mmHg
  - Avoid hypertension from stimuli or vasopressors
- Vasoactive drugs ready
  - Adenosine: temporary circulatory arrest for clipping or for rupture
  - Esmolol
  - Labetalol
  - Nicardipine
  - Phenylephrine infusion, especially during the vasospasm window (days 3 to 8)
  - Venodilators such as nitroprusside are rarely used: they raise cerebral venous volume and ICP
- Immobility
  - Microscope dissection: no patient movement and no knocking equipment or monitors
  - Full neuromuscular block unless neuromonitoring prevents it

## Stages of surgery
- Skull pinning and skin incision
  - Pinning is brief but as stimulating as incision or laryngoscopy: sudden hypertension and tachycardia
  - Blunt it with IV lidocaine 1 mg/kg, fentanyl 100 mcg, propofol 20 to 50 mg or esmolol 0.5 to 1 mg/kg (adjust to patient), local infiltration at pin sites, or scalp block
- Brain relaxation once the dura is open
  - Head up 30 degrees, neck neutral, no venous kinking from ETT ties
  - Adequate depth, analgesia and paralysis
  - Normoxia and normocapnia
  - Normothermia (core 36-37°C)
    - IHAST: mild hypothermia (33°C) did not improve neurological outcome after clipping in good-grade SAH and raised bacteraemia risk
    - Hypothermia to 32°C or deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (maximum 45 min) only for complex vascular surgery such as EC-IC bypass or giant posterior fossa aneurysms
  - Mannitol or 3% hypertonic saline
  - Lumbar CSF drainage after the dura is opened (rupture risk from TMP change)
  - Barbiturates
- Induced hypotension
  - Surgeon may request lower BP during delicate dissection; avoid profound hypotension, especially after SAH
  - TMP = MAP minus ICP: higher MAP increases TMP and wall stress and so rupture risk
  - Indications: difficult dissection, rupture
  - Contraindications: vasospasm; stroke, acute MI or renal impairment
  - Target SBP 60-80 mmHg
  - Isoflurane (up to 1.5 MAC), labetalol 5-10 mg IV boluses, SNP infusion
  - Risks: cerebral ischaemia, more postoperative vasospasm
  - Only start ICP-lowering measures after the dura is open (TMP and rupture risk)
- Temporary clipping
  - Clip on a feeding artery proximal to the aneurysm to ease dissection
  - Better than induced hypotension: larger fall in TMP, softer aneurysm, easier clipping, less rupture risk, no need for hypotension
  - BP targets
    - Keep BP at or 10-20% above baseline for collateral flow (fluids, vasopressors)
    - SBP 120-160 mmHg before clipping
    - SBP 140-180 mmHg after clipping
  - Neuroprotection
    - The clip causes focal ischaemia; longer occlusion means worse outcome
    - Hypothermia and neuroprotective drugs can be used but have not been shown to help
    - Thiopentone 250 mg IV (3-5 mg/kg) before the temporary clip
      - Then 3-5 mg/kg/h titrated to burst suppression on BIS or EEG
      - Extra dose before re-applying a clip
      - Treat hypotension with vasopressors
    - Induced hypothermia (core 32°C): complex vascular cases only; surface cooling, rewarm once flow restored
  - Limit clip time: release after 10 min, never leave more than 20 min
  - Neuromonitoring (SSEP, EEG) can check flow; for ischaemic changes or long clip times, raising MAP by 10 to 20% may help
- Adenosine-induced flow arrest
  - Transient bradycardia or asystole to cut flow through the aneurysm
  - Alternative to temporary clipping in some patients, or for rupture
  - Avoid in severe reactive airways disease, severe coronary disease or conduction abnormalities
  - Brief arrhythmias and ST depression afterwards are rarely reported
  - Discuss in advance with the surgeon; consider transcutaneous pacing pads if adenosine is planned or likely
  - Dosing not well defined; response varies
    - Escalating doses: start at 6 to 12 mg IV, increase in 6 mg steps
    - No time to escalate (rupture): 0.4 to 0.6 mg/kg IV, expecting asystole for 20 to 40 seconds (may be 60 seconds or longer)
    - Repeat only after return to sinus rhythm and pre-adenosine BP
- After the permanent clip
  - Aim for normal BP (systolic below 140 mmHg)
  - Surgeon may ask for brief induced hypertension to confirm no flow beyond the clip
  - Indocyanine green 25 mg rapid IV to show brain perfusion after clipping
    - Causes a brief false fall in SpO2, often to the low 90s
    - Rarely causes anaphylaxis

## Emergence and extubation
- Keep ventilated in ICU if:
  - Preoperative: WFNS grade III-IV (poor GCS, neurological deficit), raised ICP, marked cerebral oedema or midline shift on imaging
  - Intraoperative: long, complicated surgery; complications (instability, cerebral oedema, venous air embolism, massive transfusion)
  - Postoperative: vertebrobasilar aneurysm (cranial nerve damage risk; protect the airway); vasospasm needing treatment
- Extubation
  - Smooth, without coughing, straining, hypertension or hypercapnia
  - Rewarm before extubation
  - Short-acting drugs for early waking and neurological assessment

## Postoperative care
- Confirm with the surgeon that the aneurysm is fully secured
- Rapid emergence allows neurological examination
- ICU for serial neurological checks, continuous BP monitoring and control, analgesia and PONV treatment

## Endovascular coiling: overview
- Ruptured and unruptured aneurysms may be coiled or treated with newer endovascular devices
- ISAT: coiling gave better survival at one and seven years than clipping for ruptured aneurysms, so many centres coil first
- Often done in interventional radiology rather than theatre
- Same principles as clipping: arterial line, BP control
- Needs heparin, with or without oral antiplatelets
- Specific crises: aneurysm rupture (can be fatal), distal thromboembolism, vasospasm
- Remote-site issues: away from main theatres, unfamiliar kit and staff, bulky equipment, ionising radiation, contrast allergy
- Less stimulating (hypotension common), less need for brain relaxation, less blood loss

## Endovascular coiling: preparation and technique
- Patients on dual antiplatelets: have platelets on standby for bleeding
- Theatre and neurosurgeon available for urgent transfer if rupture occurs
- GA with paralysis rather than sedation: any movement can rupture or dissect a vessel, rupture the aneurysm or push coils into brain
- Sedation may suit diagnostic angiography
- Same principles as for clipping: avoid hypertension, maintain cerebral perfusion
- Monitoring
  - Invasive arterial BP needed
  - The femoral sheath can be used for BP instead of a peripheral arterial line
    - Most patients will still need an arterial line in the end
    - The interventionist may place a femoral line after induction
- Drugs
  - Emergency drugs ready: phenylephrine infusion, nicardipine, noradrenaline, vasopressin
  - Heparin is usual
    - Baseline ACT, recheck 10 min after heparin
    - Agree ACT target and method with the interventionist
    - Example: heparin 5000 IU IV after femoral access, then infusion or boluses to keep ACT 2-3 times baseline
  - Protamine ready for emergency reversal if rupture occurs; some reverse routinely at the end
  - High thrombotic risk from coil in the parent vessel: aspirin 500 mg orally during the case and IV heparin continued afterwards
    - May need an orogastric tube to give aspirin or clopidogrel
- Maintenance
  - Intubation is often the most stimulating moment
    - Blunt with propofol 20 to 50 mg IV, lidocaine 1 mg/kg IV, fentanyl 100 mcg IV or esmolol 0.5 to 1 mg/kg IV before intubation
  - Little stimulation afterwards

## Endovascular coiling: complications
- Distal thromboembolism, seen on angiography
  - Abciximab (glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor): 0.25 mg/kg IV bolus, then 0.125 mcg/kg/min to a maximum of 10 mcg/min
  - Some advise induced hypertension once the aneurysm is secured, to treat embolic ischaemia
- Rupture: uncommon, most likely when the first coil is deployed
  - Signs: contrast leak, raised ICP (rising BP, falling HR)
- Vasospasm during the procedure
  - From vessel handling
  - Withdraw the catheter and wait; intra-arterial nimodipine

## Endovascular coiling: neuroradiology points
- Keep lines and tubes clear of the fluoroscopy arm; extensions may be needed
- Secure monitors and breathing circuit out of the image field; BIS may interfere
- Check lead aprons and screens are available

## Intraoperative aneurysm rupture
- Can happen any time from induction until the aneurysm is secured
  - Commonest during dissection and clipping, or during coil deployment
- Rare but catastrophic
  - Worse neurological outcome, especially if before dissection starts
  - Bleeding can be sudden and massive
- Risk factors
  - Aneurysm features, poorly controlled hypertension, coronary disease, COPD
  - Rises in TMP
    - Hypertension: intubation, pinning, positioning, incision, dural opening, extubation
    - Falls in ICP: fast mannitol, hyperventilation before dural opening, excess EVD drainage
- Under anaesthesia it can be hard to spot; usually signalled by a sudden unexplained rise in BP or ICP

## Rupture during coiling
- Signs: contrast leak, raised ICP (rising BP, falling HR)
- Emergency: declare a crisis and call for help
- Alert the interventionist: may control bleeding with glue or vessel repair
- Reverse heparin with protamine
- If under sedation: induce GA and intubate
- Treat BP and ICP
  - Control hypertension with IV GTN or esmolol
  - Treat ICP (there may be no ICP monitor)
    - Mannitol 0.25 to 1 g/kg and/or hypertonic saline
    - Head up 15 degrees for venous drainage
    - Ventricular CSF drainage can worsen bleeding by raising the transmural gradient; do not open an EVD abruptly
    - Consider hyperventilation
    - Consider an EVD
- Thiopentone or propofol 20 to 60 mg IV may be requested; titrate to burst suppression if EEG in use
- Controlled hypotension with esmolol or adenosine
- Consider rapid transfer to theatre for clipping

## Rupture during clipping
- Emergency: declare a crisis and call for help
- Alert the surgeon: may place temporary clips proximal and distal to the bleed
- Treat BP and ICP
  - Control hypertension with IV GTN or esmolol
  - Treat ICP (there may be no ICP monitor)
    - Mannitol 0.25 to 1 g/kg and/or hypertonic saline (100 ml of 3% or 30 ml of 23.4% sodium chloride), repeated as needed
    - Target serum sodium 155 mmol/l or less, or osmolality above 320 mmol/l (as written in the note)
    - Head up 15 degrees; beware abrupt EVD opening
    - Consider hyperventilation
- Create a bloodless field quickly
  - Induced hypotension: esmolol 10 to 20 mg IV as needed for MAP 50 to 60 mmHg
  - Adenosine 0.4 to 0.6 mg/kg with the surgeon for brief circulatory arrest (repeat for re-rupture)
  - Compress the carotid on the same side
- Neuroprotection: lower cerebral metabolic rate with thiopentone or propofol 20 to 60 mg IV; titrate to burst suppression if EEG in use
- After clipping: fluids and blood to euvolaemia and Hb of at least 8 g/dl

## Endovascular treatment of vasospasm
- Used with medical therapy for symptomatic vasospasm after SAH
- Angioplasty of larger spastic arteries
- Intra-arterial vasodilators (such as nicardipine)
- Patient factors
  - Critically ill with cerebral ischaemia and systemic effects of SAH
  - Already intubated with arterial and central lines, often ICP and other monitoring
  - Often on high-dose vasopressors; transfer from ICU needs continuous monitoring
  - May need repeat procedures over days or weeks
- Procedure factors
  - Agree BP target with the interventionist before and during
  - Ask to be told before vasodilator injection: BP can be very sensitive to small doses
  - Expect hypotension and treat at once; high vasopressor doses may be needed

## Flashcards
- Q: What BP targets apply before an unruptured aneurysm is secured?
  - A: Systolic at or below the patient's normal (maximum 140 mmHg), MAP at least 60 mmHg, CPP at least 50 to 60 mmHg
- Q: When does vasospasm occur after SAH?
  - A: Usually after day 3, peaking at days 7 to 8, lasting up to 21 days
- Q: What adenosine dose is used for flow arrest during aneurysm rupture?
  - A: 0.4 to 0.6 mg/kg IV, giving 20 to 40 seconds of asystole
- Q: How long may a temporary clip stay on?
  - A: Release after 10 min; never more than 20 min
- Q: What is the nimodipine dose after SAH?
  - A: 60 mg every 4 hours orally or via nasogastric tube
