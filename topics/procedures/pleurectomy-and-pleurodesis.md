# Pleurectomy and Pleurodesis

Thoracic procedures to obliterate the pleural space, usually under one-lung ventilation; very painful, and steroids and NSAIDs weaken the result.

## Summary
- Underlying pneumothorax or COPD
- OLV, DLT, VATS
- Avoid NSAIDs and dexamethasone
- Blood loss

## Procedures
- Pleurectomy: pleura stripped from the inside of the chest wall
- Pleurodesis: adhesions created by
  - Chemicals: talc, tetracycline
  - Physical abrasion

## Preoperative management
- Patients range from
  - Young and otherwise well: check for asthma
  - Frail elderly with severe COPD: check PFTs and functional status
- Review CXR for pneumothorax or effusion; pneumothorax needs a chest drain first
- Agree the postoperative analgesia plan
- Massive effusion: over two-thirds of the hemithorax (2000ml)
  - Drain at least >12hr before surgery
  - Rapid intraoperative reinflation of a collapsed lung can cause unilateral re-expansion pulmonary oedema

## Intraoperative management
- Lateral decubitus, broken table
- Open thoracotomy or VATS
- IPPV, DLT and OLV
- Keep airway pressures as low as possible
  - Watch for pneumothorax: may tension even on the good side
- Avoid N2O
- Collapse the lung while the surgeon instils the irritant
- Very painful
  - Surgeon to do intercostal blocks under direct vision
  - Morphine
  - Ask surgeons whether they want PCA
- Avoid NSAIDs and dexamethasone (make pleurodesis less effective)
- Aim for full lung expansion at the end to oppose the pleural layers
- Large effusion: cardiovascular collapse may occur when turned effusion side up
  - Mechanism: mediastinal shift and raised intrathoracic pressure reduce venous return

## Postoperative management
- Extubate and sit upright
- CXR to confirm full lung expansion
- Watch for re-expansion pulmonary oedema

## Flashcards
- Q: Which drugs should be avoided in pleurodesis, and why?
  - A: NSAIDs and dexamethasone: they make pleurodesis less effective
- Q: What defines a massive effusion and when should it be drained?
  - A: Over two-thirds of the hemithorax (2000ml); drain at least 12hr before surgery
- Q: Why collapse the lung during irritant instillation and fully expand it at the end?
  - A: To allow the irritant in, then oppose the pleural layers so adhesions form
- Q: Risk of turning a patient with a large effusion effusion side up?
  - A: Cardiovascular collapse from mediastinal shift and reduced venous return
