# Anaesthesia for MRI

Remote, noisy, magnetic environment with poor patient access: MR-compatible kit, strict zone safety, visual alarms, and resuscitation outside the scanner room.

## Overview
- Remote site principles (3P3Es); poor access, so GA is often chosen, with extended lines and remote monitoring
- MRI safety covers
  - Equipment: MR-safe, interference
  - Patient: noise, RF heating
  - Staff
- Sedation or GA is often needed to keep still through long scans in a loud, claustrophobic tube
- All equipment must suit the MR environment and allow safe anaesthesia and monitoring

## MRI physics
- Hydrogen nuclei (protons) spin and so create a magnetic field
  - Hydrogen is imaged because it is so abundant in tissue
- In a strong field the spins align with the static field (B0)
- Pulsed RF energy (B1) tips them out of alignment
- As they relax they release energy, picked up by a receiver coil; signal depends on tissue and pulse sequence
- Relaxation is measured two ways (T1 and T2)
  - T1 (longitudinal): time for protons to realign with the external magnetic field; fat bright, CSF dark
    - Fat realigns quickly, so is bright; water realigns slowly
    - Good for anatomy
  - T2 (transverse): time for protons to dephase; water and CSF bright ("WW2": water white on T2)
    - High-fat tissue (e.g. white matter) appears dark
    - Good for pathology, as most lesions carry oedema and extra water
- Gadolinium mainly shortens T1
- Clinical magnets: 1.5 to 3.0 Tesla, generated by superconductors
  - 1 Tesla = 10 000 Gauss; the Earth's field is 0.5 to 1.0 Gauss
- Superconducting coils sit in liquid helium below 4.2 Kelvin
- Field strength falls off exponentially with distance from the magnet
  - Safety line at 0.5mTesla (5 Gauss): the "5 gauss line"
  - Second line at 50 Gauss: strong pull on ferromagnetic objects (projectiles); pumps and other electronic or mechanical kit may fail inside it
- On most modern scanners the field is always on, even between scans

## Hazards
- Ferromagnetic attraction and projectiles
  - Inside the 50 G contour objects are pulled towards the magnet centre and twisted (torque) to align with the field
  - Small objects become lethal projectiles; large ones can trap or crush
  - Ferromagnetic material inside the body can move or malfunction, possibly fatally
  - Nobody crosses the 5 G contour without an MR safety check for implants and other contraindications
- Radiofrequency heating
  - RF transmission at the scanner's resonant frequency deposits power (possibly unevenly) in the patient, raising temperature
  - Conductive material on the skin can cause rapid severe burns: avoid contact with metal in clothing, RF coils, ECG leads and other kit
  - Scanner limits RF power continuously; room temperature, airflow, humidity and clothing also matter
- Induced currents from time-varying fields
  - Gradient switching exposes the body and conductors to changing fields; body and blood movement induce small currents
  - Peripheral nerve and muscle stimulation (discomfort, limb movement)
  - Possible ventricular fibrillation
  - Nausea and vertigo from semicircular canal stimulation
  - Flashing lights from retinal stimulation
  - Scanners limit gradient switching to avoid limb movement or VF
- Acoustic noise
  - Gradient switching is loud, usually above the 85 dB safe level
  - Risk of noise-induced hearing loss on long scans: ear protection for staff and patients, awake or anaesthetised
- Helium escape (quench)
  - Cryostat holds about 1000 litres of liquid helium a few degrees above absolute zero
  - In a quench (spontaneous or emergency field shutdown) helium boils off and must vent fast through a quench pipe to the outside
  - If the pipe fails or blocks, gas may fill the suite: evacuate quickly
  - Suites have oxygen sensors, ventilation controls and pressure equalisation so the door can always open
- Contrast
  - Gadolinium 0.2 ml/kg; minor side effects: nausea, vomiting, pain on injection
  - Rare: nephrogenic systemic fibrosis with renal impairment

## Contraindications
- Absolute
  - Pacemakers and implanted defibrillators
  - Cochlear implants
  - Metallic foreign bodies or shrapnel in the eye
  - Ferromagnetic arterial or aneurysm clips, especially neurovascular
- Pregnancy: strong fields, noise and unscavenged gases may harm the fetus; evidence is limited, but UK advice is ideally not to scan in the first trimester

## Common ferromagnetic items
- Implants: pacemakers, cochlear implants, metallic foreign bodies (e.g. eye), ferromagnetic clips
  - Most modern implants, including joint prostheses, are non-ferromagnetic
- Patient equipment: wheelchairs, trolleys, oxygen cylinders (must be aluminium), drip stands
- Personal items: phones, belts, ID badges, stethoscopes; test glasses for compatibility
- Others: floor polishers, vacuum cleaners

## Conduct of the scan
- Keep the patient comfortable and completely still
  - A study has several sequences, each up to 10 min; any movement badly distorts images
- Complex studies can last up to 2 h
- Safety
  - MR safety check: a standard screening questionnaire usually suffices; X-ray for metal if history is unclear
  - Staff remove jewellery, watches, phones, pagers, keys, fobs, wallets, coins, metal pens, stethoscopes and metal badges
  - Remove all transdermal patches before entry
  - Ear protection for the patient

## Equipment and monitoring
- Labels
  - MR Safe (green): anywhere, including next to the table
  - MR Conditional (yellow triangle): stays behind the 50 Gauss line (first grey floor line, nearest the table)
  - MR Unsafe (red circle): outside the 5 Gauss line (second grey line, about 10 feet from the table out to the walls)
- Use MRI-compatible kit
  - Compatible: safe in the room and works normally without degrading images
  - Safe: no danger in the room, but may not work properly or may spoil images
- MRI-compatible anaesthetic machines, ventilators and vaporisers are widely available and should be used
  - Piped gases with aluminium (non-ferrous) backup cylinders
  - MR-compatible scavenging
  - Previously the machine stayed in the control room with a long coaxial Mapleson D (Bain) through the waveguide
- Monitoring to the same standard as theatre
  - Repeater screens in the control room so the anaesthetist can leave the scan room; cables run through waveguide ports
  - Alarms must be visual because of noise; keep patient, monitor and machine in clear view
  - ECG: MR-compatible electrodes in a narrow triangle on the chest
    - Induced currents cause spikes, T-wave and ST changes
    - Moving blood in the aorta also induces currents and ECG changes
  - Pulse oximetry: MR-compatible only; finger burns reported with standard probes
  - NIBP: possible with plastic connectors
  - Invasive pressure: transducer cable through the waveguide; arterial line extensions needed
  - Capnography and gas sampling: long sample line gives about a 20 second delay
  - Fibreoptic cables carry ECG as light and resist electromagnetic interference
- Airway
  - LMA without ferromagnetic parts (ProSeal has a metal ring in the tube)
  - Standard ETT only, never reinforced; tape the pilot balloon (small ferromagnetic spring) away from the scan area
- Infusion pumps (e.g. for TIVA)
  - MR-conditional pumps allow TIVA but may fail above 30 Gauss near the magnet
  - Shielded aluminium enclosures are available; MRI-safe pumps are newer
  - Extend IV lines enough to reach the 5 Gauss line
- Lines
  - PA catheters with conductive wires and epicardial pacing wires carry a theoretical microshock risk: remove before scanning
  - Central venous catheters are safe
  - Disconnect all indwelling catheters from electrical connections and accessories before entry
- Count all needles and sharps into the sharps bin

## Other issues
- Restricted access: patient lies inside a narrow bore; head coils reduce access further
- Only trained staff should work in the suite, often far from theatres
- Noise can mask alarms and the sound of partial airway obstruction: rely on visual cues

## Sedation
- Deep sedation is needed throughout because of noise, claustrophobia and the need for stillness
- Propofol may be used

## General anaesthesia
- Indications
  - Infants and children
  - Learning disability
  - Some seizure or movement disorders
  - Claustrophobia
  - Critically ill (ventilated, ICU)
- Many children can be day cases and go home once they meet standard discharge criteria
- Management
  - Induce in a dedicated anaesthetic room next to the scanner, outside the 5G contour, holding standard and resuscitation kit
  - Room placed so the patient can be moved in easily and retrieved fast
  - LMA widely used
  - Transfer on an MRI-safe non-ferrous trolley; restart maintenance and monitoring at once in the scan room
  - Ear protection on before the scan, off before waking
  - Confirm the airway is secure before scanning starts
  - Anaesthetist can then monitor from the control room
  - Afterwards remove the patient, wake and recover in a suitable area
  - Analgesia rarely needed; paracetamol for a sore throat in children
- ICU patients
  - More infusions (e.g. inotropes)
  - More monitoring (e.g. arterial lines)
  - Other kit: many tracheostomy tubes are not MR compatible and must be changed first

## Emergencies
- Resuscitation cannot be done inside the scan room or the 5 Gauss line (projectiles, poor access)
- Remove the patient from the field quickly to the induction room, which holds resuscitation drugs and kit
- Resuscitation team must not cross the 5 Gauss line; radiology staff direct them to the induction room

## Intraoperative MRI (iMRI)
- Near real-time imaging during neurosurgery
- Inner controlled area: projectile risk (>50 G)
- Larger MR-controlled area: implant risk (>5 G); access by self-locking doors or cards
- Table inside the 5 G area: all instruments must be MR safe (non-ferrous), at great cost
  - Alternative: table outside the 5 G line with full instruments; patient moved into the magnet for imaging when needed
- Anaesthetic concerns as for standard MRI, plus potentially complex neuroanaesthesia

## Flashcards
- Q: What are the 5 Gauss and 50 Gauss lines?
  - A: 5 Gauss (0.5mTesla): no entry without safety check, MR Unsafe kit stays outside; 50 Gauss: strong projectile pull, MR Conditional kit stays behind it.
- Q: Above what field strength may MR-conditional infusion pumps fail?
  - A: 30 Gauss.
- Q: Delay on capnography in MRI and why?
  - A: About 20 second, from long sample tubing.
- Q: Gadolinium dose and rare serious complication?
  - A: 0.2 ml/kg; nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in renal impairment.
- Q: How are emergencies managed in MRI?
  - A: Remove patient from the field to the induction room; no resuscitation inside the 5 Gauss line.
