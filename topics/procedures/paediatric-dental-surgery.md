# Paediatric Dental Surgery

Shared-airway surgery in often anxious children; usually nasal intubation, throat pack, local anaesthetic by the dentist and dual antiemetics.

## Quick points
- Often anxious: check the Frankl score
  - Autism or intellectual disability: premedication often needed; allow time for it to work
- Neurological or neuromuscular disease, e.g. cerebral palsy
- Note where loose teeth are
- Nasal intubation
  - Check which nostril is less blocked
  - Patient on a head strap; spray the nose after induction
  - Induce, intubate, secure the tube, insert throat pack
- Antibiotic (co-amoxiclav) may not be needed
- Two antiemetics
- No need to keep the patient paralysed
- Remember to remove the throat pack
- Suction blood before extubation
- Endocarditis prophylaxis if prosthetic heart valve or certain congenital heart disease

## Elective dental surgery
- Includes
  - Extraction of decayed baby or adult teeth
  - Restorations
  - Surgical exposure of impacted or unerupted teeth
  - Extraction of healthy adult teeth for orthodontics
- 56% of 5-year-olds have dental disease; in 5-year-olds only 8% of decay is restored, 20% extracted and 70% untreated
- Simple extraction of baby teeth: a few minutes; complex extractions, restorations or oral surgery: 1-2 hours

## Indications for general anaesthesia
- Most simple extractions are done under local anaesthesia
- GA is used when the child is:
  - Too young to cooperate
  - Too anxious
  - Developmentally delayed or has behavioural problems
  - Having complex or long procedures
  - Has failed treatment under local anaesthesia before
  - Allergic to local anaesthetic

## Preoperative assessment
- Most children having extractions under GA are 3-8 years old
- Child and parents often anxious, sometimes after a bad dental experience
- Sedative premedication commonly needed, e.g. midazolam 0.5 mg/kg oral or 100-150 mcg/kg nasal; maximum 20 mg
- Frankl Behaviour Rating Scale (used by dentists): four points, two positive and two negative behaviour grades
- Neurological or neuromuscular disease is common in this group; the condition shapes the anaesthetic
  - e.g. reflux in severe cerebral palsy, cardiomyopathy in muscular dystrophy
- Note loose teeth

## Airway options
- Routine induction; airway choice depends on procedure, patient, anaesthetist and dentist
- Nasal ETT (north-facing RAE or armoured)
  - For multiple extractions or restorations in a small child, reflux risk or need for IPPV
  - Secure, protected airway; more room in the mouth
  - Preferred for oral surgery
  - Ask parents which nostril is clearer; spray it after induction
- Oral ETT (south-facing RAE or armoured)
  - Poorer surgical access; discuss with the surgeon
- LMA (armoured or flexible)
  - Hands-free airway and protection from debris
  - Bulk limits access; easily displaced in small children when bite blocks are moved
  - Suitable for extractions, not oral surgery
- Nasal mask (McKesson or Goldman)
  - Held with jaw thrust; no protection from blood or debris
  - Largely replaced by the LMA

## Intraoperative management
- Throat pack with LMA or ETT to stop aspiration of blood and debris; remember to remove it
- Dentist usually places a bite block, taking care not to dislodge the airway
  - A rubber dam is an alternative and can be very stimulating
- Dentist usually gives local infiltration or a nerve block, so pain is seldom a problem and long-acting opioids are not needed
  - Lidocaine 2% with 1:80000 adrenaline (most common): maximum 7 mg/kg; one cartridge is 2.2 mL = 44 mg, so at most one cartridge per 7 kg
  - Prilocaine 3% with felypressin 0.03 IU/mL: useful when adrenaline is contraindicated; felypressin is a vasopressin-like synthetic hormone that does not cause local or distal ischaemia; maximum 6 mg/kg
  - Articaine 4% with 1:100000 adrenaline: infiltration only; maximum 7 mg/kg
- Antiemetic, especially for longer cases or after opioids: ondansetron 100 mcg/kg (max 4 mg) IV or dexamethasone 100-200 mcg/kg
- Suction thoroughly before extubation

## Postoperative care
- Encourage oral intake, though the numb mouth may put the child off
- Oral paracetamol and NSAIDs
  - Pain should be minimal after local anaesthetic, but children may dislike the numbness
- Length of stay
  - Healthy children after a few baby-tooth extractions: day surgery
  - Medically complex children or oral surgery: several hours or overnight stay

- Surgeons may ask for nasal intubation

## Dental abscess
- Infection at the centre of a tooth spreading to the supporting bone and nearby tissue
- Common in children, usually from caries; the periapical abscess (starting in the pulp) is the commonest type
- Features
  - Toothache, can be severe and throbbing
  - Tender, possibly loose tooth
  - Gum and facial swelling
  - Red, inflamed skin over the abscess
  - Fever and malaise
  - Severe cases: jaw muscle spasm and difficulty swallowing
- Treatment: extraction of one or more teeth, drainage of pus, IV then oral antibiotics
- GA needed in all but the most cooperative older children
- Shared airway: the anaesthetist cannot reach the airway quickly if it partly obstructs; good communication with the dentist is essential

## Dental abscess: preoperative assessment
- Anxious and in pain: give adequate analgesia beforehand
- Febrile, toxic children may be dehydrated (especially with poor intake from pain): IV fluids while fasting
- Look for swelling around the jaw and neck, especially crossing the midline
- Note mouth opening; pain may stop the child opening, making airway assessment harder

## Dental abscess: anaesthetic technique
- Airway concern: inhalational induction, keep spontaneous breathing until intubated
- Trismus from pain or spasm usually resolves once anaesthetised and airway manoeuvres are easy
  - Rarely it persists and advanced airway techniques are needed
- Intubation preferred over LMA because pus could soil the trachea; south-facing preformed RAE ETT
- Throat pack to protect against blood and pus
- After extraction and drainage the dentist may give a nerve block; often omitted with infection, so give systemic analgesia, e.g. fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg
- IV antibiotic during the case
- Extubate awake after removing the throat pack and suctioning the oropharynx

## Dental abscess: postoperative care
- Swelling and pain settle quickly; oral intake usually resumes
- Paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly usually suffice
- Oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly if needed
- May stay 24 hours for further IV antibiotics before switching to oral

## Flashcards
- Q: Maximum dose of dental lidocaine 2% with adrenaline, and mg per cartridge?
  - A: 7 mg/kg; one 2.2 mL cartridge = 44 mg (one cartridge per 7 kg).
- Q: Maximum dose of prilocaine 3% with felypressin?
  - A: 6 mg/kg.
- Q: Oral and nasal midazolam premedication doses here?
  - A: 0.5 mg/kg oral or 100-150 mcg/kg nasal; maximum 20 mg.
- Q: Preferred airway for paediatric oral surgery?
  - A: Nasal ETT (north-facing RAE or armoured) with a throat pack.
- Q: Why intubate rather than use an LMA for a dental abscess?
  - A: Pus may contaminate the trachea with an LMA.
