# Abdominal Surgery and Laparotomy

Fluid shifts, aspiration risk, heat loss and postoperative lung impairment drive anaesthesia for major abdominal surgery.

## Preoperative assessment
- **Third spacing**
  - Bowel obstruction or peritonitis can sequester large volumes in gut or interstitium, causing severe dehydration
  - Signs: thirst, dry mucosa, tachycardia, hypotension, low urine output
  - Start fluid resuscitation before induction to reduce haemodynamic collapse at induction
  - Electrolyte loss depends on the fluid lost: vomiting or gastric suction loses H+ and Cl-, giving hypokalaemic hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis
  - Bowel prep before elective colon surgery can also upset fluids and electrolytes
  - Consider checking electrolytes and haematocrit before major abdominal surgery
- GI bleeding
  - Can cause significant hypovolaemia
  - Raises aspiration risk
- Cancer
- Underlying disease shapes each case, e.g. splenectomy for sickle cell disease differs from splenectomy for ITP (immune thrombocytopenic purpura)

## Intraoperative management
- **GA** most common; **neuraxial** possible
- Usually **intubate**
- **Aspiration risk: RSI**
  - Treat any emergency case as a full stomach: intra-abdominal emergencies cause ileus and aspiration risk even after hours of fasting
  - Ileus: nausea, vomiting, distension
  - Some elective cases are high risk by nature, e.g. antireflux surgery, achalasia
  - H2 blockers and sodium citrate often given before induction in high-risk patients
  - Metoclopramide is contraindicated in bowel obstruction
- **Fluid loss can be large** even without much bleeding
- **Adequate IV access**
- **Heat loss** by radiation, conduction and evaporation is a particular problem
- **Invasive monitoring** (arterial, central lines) depends on comorbidity and expected blood loss

## Postoperative care
- **Lung function falls more** than after non-thoracic, non-abdominal surgery; **upper abdominal** surgery has the most pulmonary complications
- Pain (especially **upper abdominal** incisions) limits breathing and leads to **atelectasis**, so **good analgesia** matters
  - **PCA** with morphine, oxycodone or fentanyl: on demand, no procedure needed; may limit deep breathing, delay mobilisation, cause respiratory depression and drowsiness
  - Epidural: level matches the incision
    - Upper abdominal: low thoracic or upper lumbar (T6–L1)
    - Pelvic and lower abdominal: mid to low lumbar (L2–L5)
    - Risk of hypotension; needs high dependency care after surgery
  - Truncal blocks: **rectus sheath block, TAP block**
  - **Skin local anaesthetic; surgically placed wound catheters**
  - Laparoscopy: shoulder tip pain from phrenic irritation by pneumoperitoneum; reduce by fully evacuating the gas at the end
  - Low-dose ketamine before incision and during surgery is used in some centres to improve pain control
- **PONV** is common: give prophylaxis; use several antiemetics of different classes (multimodal) in high-risk patients
- Haemodynamic change after surgery may be **occult intra-abdominal bleeding**: **check drains**
- After upper abdominal surgery, consider **pneumothorax or haemothorax** if breathing worsens

## Minor abdominal surgery
- The above may not apply

## Flashcards
- Q: What acid-base disturbance follows loss of gastric secretions?
  - A: Hypokalaemic hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis
- Q: Which antiemetic is contraindicated in bowel obstruction?
  - A: Metoclopramide
- Q: What epidural level suits upper abdominal surgery, and lower abdominal or pelvic surgery?
  - A: T6–L1 for upper; L2–L5 for lower and pelvic
- Q: What causes shoulder pain after laparoscopy and how is it reduced?
  - A: Phrenic irritation by pneumoperitoneum; fully evacuate the gas at the end
