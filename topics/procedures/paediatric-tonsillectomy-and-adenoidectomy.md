# Paediatric tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy

Shared airway; indication is infection or obstruction/OSA; OSA children need caution with sedatives and opioids and overnight stay; a bleeding tonsil is a full-stomach, hypovolaemic, possibly difficult airway.

## Key points
- Shared airway
- Indication: OSA versus recurrent tonsillitis
- OSA: not for day surgery
- Bleeding tonsil: resuscitate; aspiration and difficult airway risk; empty the stomach; extubate lateral

## Indications
- Recurrent throat infection
  - Ensure no active infection or URTI
  - Usually more painful
- Adenotonsillar hypertrophy with upper airway obstruction, with or without OSA
  - These children are usually younger than the infection group
- Asymmetrical tonsils (to rule out malignancy): avoid dexamethasone

## Contraindications
- Bleeding disorder, anaemia (ideally Hb >10)
- Active tonsillitis or URTI (relative)

## Adenoidectomy
- Often combined with tonsillectomy; may be alone or with grommets; similar anaesthetic considerations
- Indication: nasal obstruction (e.g. mouth breathing)
- Contraindication: velopharyngeal insufficiency

## Conditions predisposing to upper airway obstruction
- OSA
- Craniofacial abnormalities (e.g. Pierre Robin, Treacher Collins, Down's syndrome)
- Neuromuscular disease (e.g. cerebral palsy)

## Preoperative assessment
- Upper respiratory symptoms are common
  - Consider proceeding if apyrexial with a clear chest; often due to adenoidal hypertrophy
- Upper airway obstruction is common
  - Avoid premedication if possible; if given, monitor closely
- OSA history: restless sleep, snoring, apnoeic pauses
  - Sleep studies quantify apnoea frequency, duration and desaturation
  - May fail to thrive and develop facial abnormalities
  - Severe chronic OSA: chronic hypoxia leads to pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale and right heart strain (seen on ECG)

## Anaesthetic technique
- OSA: avoid sedative premedication; use opioids cautiously; others premedicate as needed
- Induction (gas or IV): airway obstruction possible; usually relieved by CPAP or an oral airway once deep enough
- Airway:
  - Preformed oral south-facing RAE tube in the midline, with a short-acting relaxant, is commonest
  - Must be secure and protected from blood and debris
  - Reinforced LMA is an alternative
    - Harder gag placement; surgeon must take care
    - Risks: laryngospasm, obstruction when the gag goes in
    - Usually not suitable under 3 years
  - No throat pack needed
- Position: supine, neck extended
  - A small ETT leak can become large with extension
- Antibiotic: co-amoxiclav (Augmentin)
- Mouth gag can obstruct, kink or displace the ETT: watch during placement
- Multimodal analgesia: paracetamol plus opioid
  - Morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV, or fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg IV
  - NSAIDs widely used in the UK despite concern about haemostasis
- High PONV risk:
  - Ondansetron 100 mcg/kg IV (max 4 mg) and/or dexamethasone 150 mcg/kg IV
  - IV fluid bolus often given to reduce PONV
- Extubation deep or awake by preference; awake is safest in OSA
  - Suction carefully to clear debris without causing bleeding
- Recover head-down, lateral

## Postoperative care
- Encourage oral intake (may be poor from throat pain)
- Common regimen: paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly, ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly, oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly
- Most UK patients stay overnight; day case is growing but needs careful selection, meticulous surgery and close early observation for bleeding
- Overnight stay recommended for:
  - Age <2-3 years
  - Significant chronic illness, including OSA
    - Obstruction does not resolve immediately after surgery; risk of postoperative apnoea and hypoxia
    - Severe cases may need oxygen, nasopharyngeal airway, nasal CPAP, or intubation and ICU
  - Craniofacial abnormalities
  - Failure to thrive or significant obesity

## Infection control
- In the UK all airway equipment for adenotonsillectomy is single-use because of concern about prion transmission

## Bleeding tonsil or adenoids
- Primary: within the first 6 hours; secondary: about a week after surgery
- Potentially life-threatening
- Signs: fresh blood in the mouth, frequent swallowing, pallor, sweating

### Preoperative assessment
- Assess ability to protect the airway; emergency surgery, little time to optimise
- Assess hypovolaemia and resuscitate before induction if possible
  - Much blood may have been swallowed unnoticed; loss hard to estimate
  - Check pulse, BP, capillary refill; healthy children compensate well and hide hypovolaemia
  - Hypotension is a late sign of gross hypovolaemia with decompensation
  - IV fluid: 20 mL/kg 0.9% saline, Hartmann's or colloid, repeat as needed
  - If time allows send FBC and group and crossmatch; otherwise do it intraoperatively
- Review previous anaesthetic record
  - Airway difficulty (especially if the indication was OSA) and ease of intubation
  - Drugs given, especially opioids

### Anaesthetic technique
- Usually arrives with an IV cannula (especially primary bleed); otherwise site one before induction
- RSI standard: full stomach, raised aspiration risk
  - 2 Yankauer suckers; difficult airway trolley
- Empty the stomach with a large-bore orogastric tube after induction (much swallowed blood)
- Ongoing fluid resuscitation may be needed; sometimes an arterial line or vasopressors
- Titrate analgesia if analgesics were already given in theatre and on the ward
  - Avoid NSAIDs
- PONV prophylaxis
- Suction the airway dry; extubate awake in the head-down left lateral (tonsil) position
  - If the airway was previously easy and not swollen

### Postoperative care
- Usually rapid recovery; maintenance fluids until drinking
- Analgesia: oral opioid and paracetamol, no NSAID
- Adenoidal bleed with post-nasal pack: admit to high dependency in case the pack dislodges and obstructs the airway

## Flashcards
- Q: Ondansetron and dexamethasone doses for tonsillectomy PONV prophylaxis?
  - A: Ondansetron 100 mcg/kg IV (max 4 mg); dexamethasone 150 mcg/kg IV
- Q: Timing of primary and secondary post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage?
  - A: Primary within 6 hours; secondary about a week later
- Q: Fluid bolus for a hypovolaemic bleeding tonsil?
  - A: 20 mL/kg crystalloid or colloid, repeated if needed
- Q: Why avoid dexamethasone with asymmetrical tonsils?
  - A: Malignancy has not yet been ruled out
- Q: How is a child extubated after a bleeding tonsil?
  - A: Awake, after suctioning dry, in the head-down left lateral position
