# Craniotomy

Neuroanaesthesia for craniotomy: control BP and ICP, protect the brain, give a slack brain and allow early neurological assessment.

## Types
- Elective: brain abscess drainage, palliative or debulking tumour surgery, brain biopsy
- Emergency: evacuation of subdural or intracerebral haematoma, aneurysm clipping, brain abscess drainage

## Main considerations
- Patient:
  - Primary pathology: complications, signs of raised ICP
  - Baseline neurological status
- Surgical:
  - General neuroanaesthesia goals:
    - Blood pressure control
    - Neuroprotection: balance cerebral blood flow and metabolic rate (stable haemodynamics and CPP, ICP control, no hypoxia, normocapnia); normoglycaemia and normal electrolytes
    - ICP control and brain relaxation, so the surgeon needs minimal retraction
    - Early neurological assessment
  - Head pinning
  - Long surgery: positioning, careful fluids, temperature, thromboprophylaxis
  - Potential for blood loss and major fluid shifts
  - Prone, sitting or park bench positions
- Anaesthetic:
  - TIVA versus volatile
  - Postoperative destination and monitoring

## Preoperative assessment
- Primary pathology (always think of it):
  - Meningioma: benign, curative surgery
  - Glioma: debulking
  - Vascular lesions and haemangioma: bleeding risk
  - Pituitary tumour: associated endocrine disorders
  - Metastasis: disease elsewhere
- Review CT or MRI:
  - Site: near the sagittal sinus means bleeding and venous air embolism (VAE) risk
  - Size: large means bleeding and long surgery
  - Vascularity: bleeding risk
  - Cerebral oedema or midline shift: need for ICP measures; steroids for brain tumours
- Current treatment:
  - ICP control measures
  - Seizure prophylaxis or treatment (levetiracetam, Keppra)
  - Corticosteroids: chronic use may cause adrenal insufficiency; consider it in refractory hypotension
- Complications of disease or treatment:
  - SAH: neurological and systemic effects
  - Osmotherapy: electrolyte disturbance, dehydration
  - Dexamethasone: hyperglycaemia
- Detailed neurological assessment: GCS, deficits (cranial nerve palsies, pupils, limbs, bulbar function and aspiration risk), signs of raised ICP
- Routine assessment: comorbidities, BP control, airway, heart and lungs (neurocardiogenic effects of SAH, breathing pattern)
- Investigations: FBC, renal function and electrolytes, Ca/Mg/PO4, glucose, PT/PTT/INR, group and crossmatch

## Preoperative orders
- Fasting
- BP control
- Fluids:
  - Isotonic, not hypotonic
  - Restrict to 30ml/kg/day if cerebral oedema present
- Electrolytes: high risk of abnormalities (altered mental state, steroids, diuretics, contrast); correct before surgery
- Neuroprotection:
  - Continue osmotherapy and ICP management
  - Continue seizure prophylaxis: phenytoin 15mg/kg loading dose, then 3-4mg/kg/day
  - Continue corticosteroids
- Thromboprophylaxis: TED stockings, pneumatic calf compression
- Valid group and crossmatch; blood products available
- High dependency or ICU bed available

## Premedication
- Avoid sedatives and opioids if raised ICP is a concern
  - They depress breathing, raising CO2 and ICP, and cloud the neurological exam
  - CBF changes by 1-2 mL/100g/min for every 1 mm Hg change in pCO2
- Consent covers GA, surgical risks and postoperative care

## Technique
- GA with an ETT for most intracranial surgery
- Exception: awake craniotomy for epilepsy surgery or lesions in motor or speech cortex

## Monitoring and equipment
- Standard monitoring: NIBP, 3-lead ECG, SpO2
- Invasive monitoring for all but the smallest procedures (e.g. stereotactic biopsy, burr hole drainage):
  - Arterial line before induction: fast BP changes (induction, intubation, positioning, hyperventilation, surgery, blood loss, emergence) and frequent sampling (CO2, osmolality, Hb, oxygenation)
  - CVP for major cases: big expected blood loss (invasive tumour, AVM), vasoactive drugs, aspirating air (sitting position)
    - Often femoral, as the neck is hard to reach
    - In emergencies (e.g. decompressive craniectomy) may go in during or after surgery
- Second large-bore IV at the foot (e.g. for TIVA)
- Temperature probe, warmed fluids, forced-air warming
- Urinary catheter
- BIS if possible (agree position with surgeon)
- TED stockings and calf compressors
- Infusion pumps for TIVA if used
- Rapid infusers or fluid warmers for transfusion; blood in the theatre fridge if needed
- Resuscitation and BP drugs: vasopressors for hypotension; beta-blockers or SNP for hypertension

## Airway
- Intubate to control ventilation, optimise oxygen and CO2 and avoid ICP rises
  - Also for low GCS, long surgery, no airway access, possible haemodynamic instability
- Laryngoscopy:
  - Good preoxygenation
  - Low threshold for video laryngoscopy, for first-pass success
  - Consider hyperventilating straight after induction, as a margin if laryngoscopy is stimulating
- Secure the tube very well: airway will be inaccessible
  - Consider an armoured ETT to prevent kinking
  - Avoid ETT ties

## Induction
- Aims: smooth, haemodynamically stable induction; full paralysis and deep anaesthesia before laryngoscopy to prevent hypertension and coughing
- Propofol IV 2-3mg/kg or thiopentone IV 3-5mg/kg
  - Etomidate (0.2-0.3 mg/kg) or midazolam (0.2-0.4 mg/kg) may give a more stable induction
- Fentanyl IV 2-5mcg/kg, remifentanil IV 0.3-0.5mcg/kg/min, alfentanil 25-50mcg/kg/h
  - Opioids blunt the response to laryngoscopy and craniotomy
- Lidocaine IV 1.5mg/kg or sprayed on the cords; low-dose volatile also helps blunt the response
- Atracurium 0.6mg/kg

## Positioning and pinning
- Head up 15-20 degrees, neck neutral
- Mayfield 3-point fixator: blunt the haemodynamic response
  - LA infiltration of pin sites
  - Surgeon warns before pinning: ensure depth (propofol) and analgesia (remifentanil or fentanyl)
  - Esmolol boluses during pinning, watching the arterial line
- Protect the eyes well (skin prep can seep in)

## Maintenance
- TIVA (propofol, remifentanil) or volatile (isoflurane or sevoflurane) at 0.6-0.8 MAC, kept < 1 MAC to avoid raising ICP
- Analgesia: fentanyl, remifentanil; avoid morphine
- Muscle relaxant by bolus or infusion
- Avoid nitrous oxide, ketamine, morphine, midazolam
- Serial ABGs: correct Hb, acidosis and electrolytes
- Fluids: watch urine output; avoid hypotonic and glucose-containing fluids

## Blood pressure control
- Blunt hypertensive responses at laryngoscopy, pinning, skin incision, bone flap, extubation:
  - Propofol IV 0.5-1mg/kg, remifentanil IV 0.5-1mcg/kg
  - Esmolol IV 0.5-1mg/kg, labetalol IV 5-10mg
- Expect falls in BP; keep stable, CPP 60-70mmHg
  - BP falls when the dura opens; surgeon warns first; may need vasopressor boluses
  - After this, anaesthetic needs fall a lot, as brain tissue has no sensation
  - Euvolaemia with normal saline
  - Vasopressor infusion (e.g. phenylephrine) if needed

## Neuroprotection and brain relaxation
- Normotension, normoxia, normocapnia, normothermia, normoglycaemia
- Seizure prophylaxis if surgeons request
- Reduce ICP:
  - Adequate depth, analgesia and paralysis
  - Keep PaCO2 30-35 mm Hg, hyperventilate if needed; normoxia
  - Avoid high PEEP
  - On surgical request: mannitol 20% 100ml, hypertonic saline 3% 100ml or furosemide 10-20mg; dexamethasone 8mg
- Check glucose; keep normoglycaemic
- Keep core temperature 36-37°C

## Emergence
- Indications for postoperative ICU ventilation:
  - Preoperative: large or deep tumour, poor GCS (< 6), raised ICP, marked oedema or midline shift
  - Intraoperative: long complex surgery, complications (instability, brain swelling, VAE, massive transfusion), poor haemostasis with likely return to theatre, surgery near critical posterior fossa structures
- Smooth emergence without coughing or hypertension; esmolol boluses if needed
- Analgesia: fentanyl boluses

## Postoperative management
- Neurosurgical high dependency or ICU for neuromonitoring
- Recovery: early neurological assessment (short-acting drugs, little hangover); BP control and haemodynamic monitoring
- ICU care:
  - Analgesia: paracetamol, codeine phosphate 60-90mg, NSAIDs (bleeding risk in ICH; renal risk if hypovolaemic on diuretics)
  - Wean and extubate according to neurological state
  - BP control
  - Euvolaemia with isotonic fluids
  - Early enteral feeding within 24-48h once planned surgery is complete
  - Stress ulcer prophylaxis: esomeprazole IV 40mg each morning; stop when enteral feeding is established
  - TED stockings and calf compressors
  - Seizure prophylaxis

## Specific indications
- Intracranial aneurysm
- Vascular malformation (AVM)
- Haemorrhagic stroke
- Cerebral embolectomy
- Trauma
- Tumour
- Extracranial-intracranial (EC-IC) bypass

## Flashcards
- Q: Target CPP and PaCO2 during craniotomy in this note?
  - A: CPP 60-70mmHg; PaCO2 30-35 mm Hg.
- Q: Maximum volatile dose for craniotomy?
  - A: Keep 0.6-0.8 MAC, under 1 MAC to avoid raising ICP.
- Q: What happens to BP when the dura is opened?
  - A: It falls; vasopressor boluses may be needed and anaesthetic requirement drops.
- Q: How is pinning managed?
  - A: LA to pin sites, deepen with propofol and remifentanil or fentanyl, esmolol boluses guided by arterial line.
- Q: How much does CBF change per mm Hg of pCO2?
  - A: 1-2 mL/100g/min.
