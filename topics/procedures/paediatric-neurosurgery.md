# Paediatric Neurosurgery

Anaesthesia for children having shunts, ventriculostomy, baclofen pumps, craniotomy and AVM embolisation.

## General considerations
- Raised ICP in children
  - Vomiting, irritability, seizures, lethargy
  - Bulging anterior fontanelle, growing head circumference, engorged scalp veins, "sun setting" eyes
- Investigations: cranial ultrasound, CT or MRI
- As in adults: record preoperative neurological status; control ICP and BP; neuroprotection
- Conscious level
  - Glasgow Coma Scale if older than 4 years; Children's Coma Scale if younger
  - AVPU is a quick alternative in young children: Alert, responds to Voice, responds to Pain, Unresponsive
- No sedative or opioid premedication with suspected raised ICP: respiratory depression causes hypercapnia and further raises ICP
- Airway: a shoulder roll may be needed for a very large head
- Induction: IV ideal to limit ICP rise; without easy IV access, sevoflurane by facemask is preferred, as crying and struggling raise ICP further
- TIVA use limited by pharmacokinetic models and monitoring issues
- Avoid glucose-containing fluids where possible, but watch for hypoglycaemia in neonates and ex-premature infants and monitor glucose closely
- Venous air embolism can become paradoxical; in infants and small children the head is above the heart even supine

## Hydrocephalus: background
- Abnormal CSF accumulation in the cranium
- Congenital (aqueduct stenosis, myelomeningocele, Arnold-Chiari) or acquired (infection, intraventricular haemorrhage, tumour)
  - Occurs in over 80% of patients with myelomeningocele
- Communicating: CSF leaves the ventricles but is blocked beyond them (e.g. after meningitis)
- Non-communicating: blockage inside the ventricular system, before the subarachnoid space
- Progressive hydrocephalus is diagnosed by rising head circumference
- The classic big head, bulging fontanelle, engorged veins and sun setting eyes is rare and late

## Hydrocephalus: treatment
- CSF diversion via a low-pressure valved shunt from ventricle to peritoneum (VP shunt)
- Non-communicating: surgical or endoscopic third ventriculostomy (third ventricle to subarachnoid space)
  - Bypasses obstructions such as aqueduct stenosis
  - Avoids shunt infection and blockage
- Premature babies with post-haemorrhagic hydrocephalus: high CSF protein blocks shunts
  - Repeated ventricular taps, directly or via an Ommaya or Rickham reservoir, until protein falls
- VP shunt has 3 parts:
  - Proximal catheter in the ventricle
  - One-way valve with set opening pressure; usually a reservoir to check pressure (reflects ICP) and sample CSF
  - Distal catheter tunnelled under the skin to the peritoneum
- Ventricular access device: self-sealing dome reservoir under the scalp with a ventricular catheter
  - Volume usually 1.5-2.5 mL
  - Used for ICP measurement, CSF sampling and sometimes antibiotics or chemotherapy

## Hydrocephalus: preoperative assessment
- Record baseline neurology; reassess regularly on the ward, as deterioration can be rapid
- Raised ICP brings vomiting, dehydration, reduced consciousness and aspiration risk
- Many are ex-premature: bronchopulmonary dysplasia, oxygen need, difficult venous access
- Apnoeic episodes are an important sign of raised ICP in neonates
- Repeat revisions common; often cerebral palsy and/or epilepsy
- Chronic shunt infection causes anaemia (poor intake, marrow suppression)
- On dexamethasone: consider stress-dose steroids

## Hydrocephalus: anaesthetic technique
- IV induction preferred in theory to limit ICP rise, if a cannula is in or easily placed
- In practice either works; many feel gas induction harms ICP less than a difficult cannulation with crying; often the realistic choice in infants
- Intubate and ventilate
- Fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg; reduce or omit (if local infiltration is adequate) in ex-premature infants under 5 kg
- Blood loss usually small; invasive monitoring rarely needed
- VP shunt
  - Tape the ETT firmly to one side (head turned)
  - Blunt haemodynamic and ICP responses to tunnelling with deeper anaesthesia, opioid or esmolol
- Third ventriculostomy
  - Handling near the hypothalamus and brainstem can cause bradyarrhythmias, hypotension or hypertension
- Local anaesthetic to the surgical site reduces opioid need

## Hydrocephalus: postoperative care
- Preterm babies and those with neural tube defects need apnoea monitoring
- Respiratory support may need stepping up (oxygen to nasal CPAP; nasal CPAP to intubation and IPPV)

## Baclofen pump: background
- Spasticity results from loss of descending inhibition in the cord; a major problem in cerebral palsy
- Baclofen: GABA-B agonist acting in the cord to reduce excitatory transmitter release; eases spasm pain and may delay contractures
- Oral baclofen limited by somnolence, confusion, ataxia and urinary frequency
- Intrathecal baclofen works at lower doses with fewer side effects
- Trial first: subarachnoid catheter placed under GA, intrathecal baclofen for 48-72 hours to judge response
- Pump and reservoir go in a subcutaneous pocket in the lateral abdominal wall; catheter placed via Tuohy needle and tunnelled to the pump
- Refilled through the skin; programmed externally
- Complications: infection (5%), catheter disconnection or fracture
- Numbers needing GA for pump insertion have risen markedly over the last decade

## Baclofen pump: preoperative assessment
- Cerebral palsy issues: respiratory function, reflux, impaired airway reflexes, epilepsy, medications
- Give usual medications; premedication as indicated (antisialogogue, sedative, antacid)
- Venous access may be difficult; often many previous anaesthetics

## Baclofen pump: anaesthetic technique
- Induction by preference, venous access and aspiration risk
- Intubation and IPPV usual
- Lateral position
- Pad pressure points, care with contractures, keep warm, guard against aspiration
- Analgesia: morphine 100-150 mcg/kg or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg plus wound infiltration
- At the end the pump is purged over 40 min to prime the dead space of pump and catheter

## Baclofen pump: postoperative care
- Maintenance fluids until drinking; usual drugs continued
- Paracetamol, NSAID and oral opioid
- Spasticity usually eases within 24-48 hours of starting the infusion
- Overdose (sudden or gradual): drowsiness, respiratory depression, ascending hypotonia, loss of consciousness
- Abrupt withdrawal of oral or intrathecal baclofen: seizures, hallucinations, disorientation, dyskinesia, itch, lasting up to 72 hours

## Craniotomy: background
- Commonest reason is tumour resection
- CNS tumours are the second commonest childhood malignancy
- 60% in the posterior fossa
- Peak age 1-8 years
- Astrocytoma 30%, medulloblastoma 30%, brainstem glioma 30%, ependymoma 7%
- Other indications: epilepsy, head injury, vascular lesions, craniosynostosis

## Craniotomy: preoperative assessment
- Neurology: raised ICP, conscious level, focal signs (e.g. cranial nerve palsies)
- Bulbar palsy may have caused aspiration and lung damage
- Dehydration from vomiting
- Multisystem disease is rare
- FBC, U&E and crossmatch
- Avoid sedative premedication
- Consider an antisialogogue in infants and prone cases (dries secretions, cardiovascular stability)

## Craniotomy: anaesthetic technique
- IV induction usually preferred with raised ICP; a smooth gas induction beats a traumatic IV one (depends on age, cooperation, access)
- Blunt the intubation response with fentanyl or remifentanil bolus and adequate depth; neuromuscular blocker for intubation
- Reinforced (armoured) ETT secured with waterproof tape
  - Thicker wall: internal diameter may need to be 0.5 mm smaller than the age-based size
  - Check bilateral air entry in neutral and in the surgical position; neck flexion can cause endobronchial intubation
- Aim: good operating conditions and rapid wake-up for neurological assessment
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen and air or nitrous oxide
  - Nitrous oxide concerns: increases CBF, ICP and the size or pressure of intracranial air
  - Remifentanil 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/minute lowers volatile need and gives moderate hypotension with stability
  - Propofol and remifentanil TIVA is good in older children
  - Avoid hypertension from light anaesthesia
- Opioid infusion (or boluses) blunts pressor responses: head pins, incision (also local infiltration), dural opening, closure
- IPPV to normocapnia; hyperventilation only for acute ICP rises
- Two secure large cannulae for rapid fluids
- Mannitol, furosemide, steroids and antibiotics as per protocol or surgeon
- Profound induced hypotension rarely used
- Brainstem stimulation can cause hypertension and arrhythmias; tell the surgeon

## Craniotomy: monitoring
- Capnography: ventilation and venous air embolism (any neurosurgical case, more so if hypovolaemic)
- Arterial line for major cases
- Femoral route may be best if CVP needed
- Urinary catheter: long cases, after mannitol or diuretic, expected high urine output (e.g. craniopharyngioma), or major bleeding

## Craniotomy: positioning
- Most cases supine with head turned
- Moderate head-up and free jugular veins keep cerebral venous pressure low
- Short necks and large heads make venous kinking more likely in small children
- Prone (e.g. posterior fossa): free abdomen (venous pressure and ventilation), protect eyes and pressure points
- Sitting position rare (venous air embolism)
  - Air embolism less common than in adults (less negative intracranial venous pressure)
  - Benefits: better anatomy, less bleeding, lower ICP, fewer cranial nerve injuries
  - Postural hypotension rare, but sit up gradually over a few minutes

## Craniotomy: fluid balance
- Some fluid restriction to limit cerebral oedema, but circulating volume for cerebral perfusion comes first
- 0.9% saline usual maintenance
- Glucose can be avoided for the first 24 hours in older children (hyperglycaemia worsens brain injury outcome); small children are prone to hypoglycaemia, so monitor glucose and give dextrose if needed
- Avoid hyperosmolar or hypo-osmolar fluids (worsen cerebral oedema)
- Blood loss hard to measure: guide replacement by clinical state, urine output and serial haematocrit

## Craniotomy: postoperative care
- Most extubated at the end; early neurological assessment, then regular checks in HDU
- Maintenance fluids
- Analgesia: wound infiltration, paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly, oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly
- NSAIDs may be held early (platelet effect)
- Major cases: IV morphine infusion, NCA or PCA

## AVM embolisation: background
- Presentation depends on size and flow
  - Large: high output cardiac failure
  - Infants: enlarging head from hydrocephalus
  - Older children: neurological deficit from vascular steal
- Clinical state predicts procedural risk
- Some lesions carry high mortality and morbidity whatever the treatment (e.g. vein of Galen aneurysm)
- Interventional embolisation (coils, glue into feeding arteries) is first choice where feasible; several sessions may be needed
- Risks: intracerebral bleeding, embolus migration, pulmonary emboli, ischaemia of vital areas

## AVM embolisation: preoperative assessment
- Baseline neurological function
- Look for high output cardiac failure

## AVM embolisation: anaesthetic technique
- Neuroangiography suite is poor for infant induction and line insertion; consider anaesthetising in a normal anaesthetic room then transferring
- Intubate and ventilate to normocapnia
- Limited access: long IV and ventilator extensions
- Invasive monitoring usually indicated
  - Hypertension, bradycardia and arrhythmias from stimulation or sudden flow changes
- Femoral angiography catheters are relatively large in small children
- Repeated catheter flushes can cause hypervolaemia, especially in infants
- Long, demanding procedures; cramped, dim and cool suite
- Monitor and maintain core temperature
- Iodinated contrast
  - Small anaphylaxis risk
  - Maximum recommended dose 4 mL/kg
  - Hyperosmolar: initial volume load then dehydration from osmotic diuresis; give IV fluids
  - May affect clotting
- Blood loss hard to estimate; have blood available

## AVM embolisation: postoperative care
- Brief ICU ventilation sometimes, especially if hypothermic or transferred between hospitals
- Look for new neurological deficit
- Maintenance fluids; monitor fluid balance; FBC and electrolytes (flush volume)
- Watch femoral puncture sites for bleeding; if arterial, check leg perfusion regularly

## Flashcards
- Q: Which coma scale is used by age in children?
  - A: GCS if older than 4 years; Children's Coma Scale if younger (AVPU as a quick alternative).
- Q: Fentanyl dose for VP shunt insertion, and when to reduce it?
  - A: 1-2 mcg/kg; reduce or omit in ex-premature infants under 5 kg.
- Q: Remifentanil infusion rate for paediatric craniotomy?
  - A: 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/minute.
- Q: Maximum recommended dose of iodinated contrast in children?
  - A: 4 mL/kg.
- Q: Signs of intrathecal baclofen overdose?
  - A: Drowsiness, respiratory depression, ascending hypotonia, loss of consciousness.
