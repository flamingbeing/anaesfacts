# Trauma Surgery

Use ATLS, assume a full stomach and difficult airway, keep resuscitating and do not delay lifesaving surgery.

## General considerations
- Manage by ATLS principles:
  - Primary survey and initial resuscitation
  - Secondary survey and emergency treatment
  - Definitive care
- Ongoing resuscitation
  - Hypovolaemia and haemorrhage
  - Lethal triad: hypothermia, coagulopathy, acidosis
- Difficult airway
  - Emergency, full stomach
  - C-spine injury: neck movement restricted by manual in-line stabilisation
  - Airway trauma
  - Facial trauma: difficult mask ventilation; securing the ETT may be hard
- Multiple obvious and occult injuries
  - Pulmonary contusions: ventilation may be difficult
  - Pneumothorax: may worsen with positive pressure ventilation
  - Cardiac contusions
  - Traumatic brain injury: risk of rebleed
- May need angioembolisation in interventional radiology

## Immediately life-threatening injuries
- Airway obstruction
- Tension pneumothorax
- Open pneumothorax
- Cardiac tamponade
- Massive haemothorax
- Flail chest

## Delayed or hidden injuries
- Thoracic aortic disruption
- Tracheobronchial disruption
- Myocardial contusion
- Traumatic diaphragmatic tear
- Oesophageal disruption
- Pulmonary contusion

## Preoperative assessment
- Several procedures are often needed; lifesaving surgery first, but combine procedures where possible
- Review the patient fully yourself: major injuries are easily missed in the emergency department
  - Check the CXR for missed pathology
  - At least an ATLS primary (1°) survey just before anaesthesia
  - Suspect injuries missed at first assessment that can cause cardiorespiratory compromise: pneumothorax, spinal cord injury, tamponade, fat embolism, occult haemorrhage
- Do not delay lifesaving surgery to fully correct hypovolaemia or for unnecessary tests; keep resuscitating during transfer
- Permissive hypotension (hypotensive resuscitation) to a systolic BP of 80 mmHg may be better with ongoing uncontrolled haemorrhage

## Intraoperative management
- Airway
  - Tracheal intubation is usual
  - Intubate with C-spine control; expect difficulty and have all equipment ready before induction
  - Assume a full stomach: RSI
  - Place a gastric tube for decompression; use the oral route with nasal, mid-face or base of skull fractures
- Suxamethonium is contraindicated more than 48 hours after burns or spinal injury (hyperkalaemia)
- Ventilation
  - Normal settings may need changing for trauma-specific problems
  - Raised or possibly raised ICP: keep PaCO2 4.5–5 kPa (35–38 mmHg) for stable ICP
  - Chest trauma may need special settings, possibly an ICU ventilator
- Regional anaesthesia: possible adjunct, but often impractical (urgency, instability, coagulopathy, risk of masking compartment syndrome)
- Resuscitate by the ATLS approach
- Venous access
  - Ideally two 14–16G cannulae
  - If difficult: cut-down, femoral line, external jugular line or intraosseous access
- Temperature: hypothermia causes significant morbidity; prevent and treat it
  - Run one IV line through a high-performance warmer
- Arterial line when practical; not usually needed at once and should not delay surgery
- Central venous access
  - Not usually a priority; may be hard with vascular injury and hypovolaemia
  - Best after fluid resuscitation
  - Femoral line may be quickest if vasopressors or inotropes are needed, but avoid in abdominal trauma

## Differential diagnosis
- Unexplained hypotension and tachycardia: hypovolaemia, pneumothorax, tamponade, fat or air embolism
- Unexplained hypoxia with rising inflation pressure: tension pneumothorax, fat embolism
- Unexplained hypertension: pain, raised ICP (look for neurological signs, CT brain), rarely thoracic aortic disruption

## Trauma in pregnancy
- Even higher risk of aspiration and difficult airway
- Different injury pattern: preterm labour, abruption, uterine rupture, amniotic fluid embolism, alloimmunisation
- Shock may decompensate late because of physiological hypervolaemic haemodilution
- Modified ACLS:
  - IV access above the diaphragm
  - Chest compressions higher on the sternum
  - Left uterine displacement
  - Immediate caesarean section at 4 minutes after cardiac arrest
- 2 patients:
  - Fetal monitoring
  - Obstetric and paediatric input
  - Steroids for fetal lung maturity if under 34 weeks gestation
  - Magnesium sulphate for fetal brain protection
- Consider imaging issues (CT, X-ray)

## Flashcards
- Q: What systolic target is used for hypotensive resuscitation in uncontrolled haemorrhage?
  - A: 80 mmHg
- Q: When is suxamethonium contraindicated after burns or spinal injury?
  - A: More than 48 hours after the injury (hyperkalaemia)
- Q: What PaCO2 target is used with raised ICP in trauma?
  - A: 4.5–5 kPa (35–38 mmHg)
- Q: When should perimortem caesarean section be done in maternal cardiac arrest?
  - A: At 4 minutes after arrest
- Q: What venous access is ideal in major trauma?
  - A: Two 14–16G cannulae
