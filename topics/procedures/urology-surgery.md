# Urological Surgery

Overview of urology anaesthesia: elderly, often renally impaired patients, position effects, procedure-specific complications, and a prostate biopsy recipe.

## Overview
- Patients often elderly with many comorbidities, including renal impairment
- Ranges from outpatient cystoscopy to major cancer surgery, so anaesthesia varies widely

## Preoperative assessment
- Signs of renal failure: haematuria or proteinuria, raised urea and creatinine, reduced creatinine clearance
- If in renal failure, decide acute or chronic and the cause: prerenal, renal or postrenal (obstructive)

## Intraoperative management
### Renal protection
- Avoid nephrotoxic drugs
- Correct hypovolaemia
- Dose drugs for renal function
- Watch for urinary outflow obstruction
- Anaesthesia can lower renal blood flow and GFR even with normal BP, and lowers BP through myocardial and sympathetic depression

### Positioning
- Lithotomy: cystoscopy, TURP, TURBT, ureteroscopy
  - Beyond two hours: risk of sensory neuropathy, and rhabdomyolysis from compartment syndrome
  - Abdominal contents push up: compliance, FRC and vital capacity fall; more atelectasis
  - Raising the legs increases venous return, cardiac output and BP, usually not clinically important
- Kidney rest (lateral flexed) position for renal surgery
  - Axillary roll under the upper chest protects the brachial plexus
  - Lateral position causes marked V/Q mismatch and dependent atelectasis
  - BP, cardiac output and renal perfusion pressure fall

### Approaches to the kidney
- Open
- Transperitoneal laparoscopic
- Retroperitoneoscopic
- Robot-assisted

## Complications
- Bladder perforation at cystoscopy
  - From obturator nerve stimulation (violent thigh adduction) or high irrigation pressure
  - Awake: lower abdominal pain and nausea; under GA: haemodynamic instability
  - Pain may be suprapubic, inguinal, periumbilical, upper abdominal, or referred to the shoulder
- Autonomic hyperreflexia at cystoscopy (rare, serious)
  - Hypertensive emergency in spinal cord injury at T6 or above
  - Also headache, chest tightness, flushing, sweating
  - Treat with short-acting beta-blockers or other IV agents for rapid BP control
- Irrigation fluid absorption
  - Bladder is distended with irrigant for vision in cystoscopy and TURP
  - Ideal fluid (isotonic, non-haemolytic, clear, non-electrolytic, cheap, non-toxic) does not exist; know which fluid is used and its risks (see TURP syndrome)
- Bacteraemia after TURP
  - High-pressure irrigation and indwelling catheters
  - Prophylactic antibiotics before the start, continued for 2–3 days after catheter removal
- Hypothermia: elderly patients given large volumes of cool irrigant

## Laser surgery
- Uses: condylomata, interstitial cystitis, BPH, ureteric or bladder stricture, contracture or stones, superficial urinary tract or genital cancers
- Little blood loss and pain
- Types: carbon dioxide, argon, pulsed-dye
- Safety
  - Eye protection for all staff and the patient
  - Thermal injury: one operator only, standby between uses to cool
  - Smoke and viral particles: laser masks and smoke evacuation

## Prostate biopsy and spacer insertion
- E.g. robotic (Biobot) biopsy, rectal spacer insertion
- Very quick (about 30 min), low stimulation, but no movement while the ultrasound probe is in, so do not run too light
- Usually GA; rarely prilocaine spinal
- Conduct
  - Position: lithotomy, left arm out
  - Airway: LMA
  - Induction: fentanyl
  - Maintenance: sevoflurane
  - Antibiotics: ceftriaxone
  - Analgesia: coxib, fentanyl
  - Antiemetics: dual
  - Postoperative: home (day case)

## Flashcards
- Q: Spinal injury level at risk of autonomic hyperreflexia during cystoscopy?
  - A: T6 or above
- Q: How long in lithotomy raises the risk of neuropathy and compartment syndrome?
  - A: More than two hours
- Q: What causes bladder perforation at cystoscopy?
  - A: Obturator nerve stimulation causing thigh contraction, or high irrigation pressure
- Q: How long are antibiotics continued after TURP?
  - A: 2–3 days after the catheter is removed
