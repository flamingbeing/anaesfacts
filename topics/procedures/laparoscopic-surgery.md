# Laparoscopic Surgery

CO2 pneumoperitoneum plus steep positioning changes cardiovascular, respiratory and renal physiology; watch ETCO2, airway pressure and occult complications.

## Common procedures
- Fundoplication, Heller's myotomy, cholecystectomy, hernia repair, bariatric surgery, some bowel surgery

## Insufflation gases
- Gas in the peritoneal cavity lifts the abdominal wall off the viscera
- Gases used: CO2 (most common), N2O, O2, air
- CO2
  - For: colourless, non-flammable (diathermy and laser safe), non-toxic, highly blood soluble (less discomfort from leftover gas, fewer problems if it ends up outside the peritoneum)
  - Against: peritoneal irritation; hypercarbia from absorption
- N2O
  - For: less peritoneal irritation
  - Against: supports combustion (explosion risk with diathermy or laser); slightly lower blood solubility
- Insufflation at 4-6 l/min to a pressure of 10-15 mmHg

## Effects of gas insufflation
- Systemic CO2 absorption
  - Readily absorbed from peritoneum: hypercapnia and acidosis
  - Absorption depends on gas solubility, intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) and duration
  - Long cases with rising ETCO2 may need a "CO2 break"
  - Rising ETCO2 comes from peritoneal absorption, ventilatory effects of pneumoperitoneum and positioning
  - Effects of hypercarbia (direct and via sympathoadrenal stimulation)
    - Heart rate and CO increase, SVR falls, arrhythmias
    - Myocardial oxygen use rises, risking ischaemia
- Extraperitoneal gas
  - Causes
    - Misplaced trocar or Veress needle
    - Anatomical defect (e.g. between pleura and peritoneum)
    - Pressurised gas tracking along tissue planes
    - Surgery near the diaphragm (e.g. hiatus hernia repair)
  - Results: subcutaneous emphysema, pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, pneumothorax
  - Sign: rapidly rising ETCO2 from absorption
  - Pneumomediastinum and pneumothorax: rising airway pressure and falling saturation; with major cardiorespiratory compromise, needle decompress as for tension pneumothorax
  - Severe subcutaneous emphysema can reach the neck and rarely compromise the airway: consider laryngoscopy and a cuff leak test before extubation
  - Risk factors: surgery over 200 min, 6 or more ports, age over 65, Nissen fundoplication
  - Letting the CO2 out often fixes it quickly; surgery can usually finish at lower insufflation pressure
- Venous gas embolism
  - Rare: gas insufflated straight into a vessel
  - CO2 causes 6.5 times less harm than air because it is more soluble
  - Signs: ETCO2 falls, desaturation, arrhythmias, myocardial ischaemia, hypotension, raised CVP
- Hypothermia from dry gas: fluid warmer, forced-air warming, warm theatre

## Effects of pneumoperitoneum
- Cardiopulmonary effects scale with IAP; significant above 12 mmHg
- Cardiovascular
  - Variable and dynamic; usually well tolerated if healthy
  - Significant cardiac dysfunction can occur in elderly and comorbid patients (COPD, heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, valve disease)
  - More sympathetic output (pneumoperitoneum and CO2): SVR and heart rate increase
  - Venous return
    - Increases when IAP is below 10 mmHg (splanchnic compression)
    - Falls when IAP is above 20 mmHg (IVC compression; higher intrathoracic pressure from diaphragm splinting)
  - Cardiac output falls from
    - Less venous return
    - Less contractility (cardiac compression or distortion, acidosis)
    - Less ventricular compliance (splinting, compression; at IAP above 30 mmHg)
    - Higher afterload: SVR and PVR rise (vessel compression; lung compression from a raised diaphragm)
  - BP is usually maintained or rises
  - CVP and PCWP rise as IAP is transmitted to the vena cava and pulmonary vessels; high values do not mean adequate filling: use echo (2D) instead
  - More arrhythmias: peritoneal stretch causes vagal bradycardia, nodal rhythm, sometimes asystole; anticipate and treat with atropine or glycopyrronium
  - More venous thrombosis: venous stasis, less femoral venous flow
- Respiratory
  - Diaphragm splinting: compliance falls, airway and peak inspiratory pressures rise
  - Diaphragm pushed up: FRC falls, atelectasis, V/Q mismatch, hypoxaemia (more shunt)
- Neurological
  - ICP rises: higher intrathoracic pressure impedes cerebral venous outflow; hypercarbia dilates cerebral vessels
  - CPP unchanged or rises
- Abdominal and renal
  - Regurgitation risk rises with IAP
  - Lower abdominal perfusion pressure and venous obstruction reduce splanchnic and renal flow
  - Mesenteric flow falls at IAP above 20 mmHg; gut mucosal ischaemia at above 40 mmHg
    - Mucosal barrier fails: bacterial translocation and sepsis
    - Lactic acidosis, more oxygen free radicals
    - Poor healing of anastomoses and wounds
  - Hepatic flow falls at IAP above 20 mmHg
    - Less hepatic metabolism and mitochondrial function; lactate clears poorly, so it is a weaker resuscitation marker
    - Liver failure: poor clotting factor and protein synthesis, infection risk, encephalopathy
  - Renal impairment: less renal blood flow and GFR (higher renal vascular resistance, lower filtration gradient)
    - Oliguria at IAP 15-20 mmHg; anuria above 30 mmHg
    - Renin-angiotensin activation: more renin, aldosterone and ADH; less ANP
    - Filtration gradient = (MAP - IAP) - proximal tubular pressure (PTP); IAP above normal PTP (15-18 mmHg) impairs filtration and urine output

## Effects of positioning
- Reverse Trendelenburg (head up), for upper abdominal surgery
  - Cardiovascular: venous pooling, less venous return and CO, hypotension (worse if hypovolaemic, elderly or with heart disease); baroreflex raises sympathetic tone, heart rate and SVR; DVT; venous air embolism
  - Respiratory: diaphragm moves down, FRC rises, work of breathing falls; lungs and carina move down, risking accidental extubation
  - CNS: ICP falls; cerebral ischaemia if CPP falls
- Trendelenburg (head down), for lower abdominal surgery
  - Cardiovascular: more venous return and filling pressures; can worsen fluid overload in those at risk of heart failure
  - Respiratory: diaphragm moves up, FRC falls, atelectasis, V/Q mismatch, hypoxaemia; compliance falls and airway pressure rises; carina moves up, risking endobronchial intubation; bronchospasm
  - CNS: ICP rises from poor cerebral venous drainage
  - Other: intraocular pressure rises; aspiration risk rises
- Lateral tilt, e.g. left tilt for cholecystectomy or appendicectomy

## Trocar and surgical hazards
- Organ injury (liver, spleen, stomach, bowel, uterus, bladder), possibly unnoticed at the time
- Vessel injury (aorta, iliac vessels, epigastric and splenic arteries): massive bleeding needing conversion to open
- Small field: hidden bleeding may be missed

## Preoperative assessment
- Contraindications are relative; risk rises with cardiac disease, hypovolaemia and raised ICP
- Every case may convert to open: plan analgesia for that (e.g. PCA)
- H2 antagonist or PPI premedication if at risk of regurgitation (obesity, hiatus hernia)

## Intraoperative management
- Position
  - Upper abdominal: head up; lower abdominal: head down
  - Some left tilt usual for cholecystectomy and appendicectomy
  - Arms may be tucked: extend the drip, keep the drip outside the gel wrap, tuck with a perforated sheet
- Technique
  - GA with ETT, relaxation and controlled ventilation is safest: protects against aspiration, controls PaCO2, helps surgical view
  - N2O is debated: possible bowel distension and more PONV
  - LMA: personal choice
    - Avoid with reflux, obesity, expected difficult or long surgery (especially head down) or an inexperienced surgeon
    - Can suit short cases (e.g. laparoscopic sterilisation) with an experienced anaesthetist and careful selection
    - Third-generation supraglottic airways with drain tubes (LMA ProSeal, LMA Supreme, i-gel) are preferable
  - Regional alone is rarely used: the block would need to be very high
- Trocar insertion
  - Mask ventilate carefully after induction to avoid gastric inflation, which raises injury risk
  - Place a nasogastric or orogastric tube after intubation; remove at the end once the stomach is deflated if needed
  - Urinary catheter may be used to empty the bladder
- Ventilation
  - CO2 absorption and raised IAP need higher minute volume and raise intrathoracic pressure: aim for normocapnia without excessive pressures
  - ETCO2 may be hard to control in long cases, obesity and head down: occasional release of gas or accepting some hypercapnia
  - High peak pressures: check ETT position; try pressure control, I:E 1:1 and PEEP 5 cmH2O; recheck tidal volume or settings when the patient is levelled
- Analgesia
  - Depends on procedure
  - Pain may be intense but brief: short-acting opioids (fentanyl, alfentanil) for short cases
  - Remifentanil infusion for longer cases; also blunts pneumoperitoneum haemodynamic changes
  - Longer-acting opioids for extensive surgery
  - Extensive surgery: epidural or spinal (with or without intrathecal opioid) may help
- Fluids: avoid hypovolaemia; it worsens the cardiovascular effects
- Monitoring
  - Watch ETCO2 and airway pressure closely
  - Arterial line and CVP for extensive surgery or cardiorespiratory compromise
- Causes of hypoxia
  - Hypoventilation: pneumoperitoneum, head-down tilt
  - Low cardiac output: IVC compression, arrhythmia, bleeding, myocardial depression, gas embolism, extraperitoneal gas
  - V/Q mismatch: low FRC, atelectasis, endobronchial tube, gas embolism, aspiration, rarely pneumothorax
  - Subcutaneous emphysema during surgery: stop insufflation at once and find the source

## Postoperative care
- Ask the surgeon to expel as much gas as possible: less pain
- Local anaesthetic to port sites and intraperitoneally reduces analgesic needs
  - Laparoscopic cholecystectomy: 20 to 30 ml of 0.25% bupivacaine on the gallbladder bed
- Pain is usually worst in the 1st few hours; significant pain beyond day 1 suggests an intra-abdominal complication
- Shoulder tip pain from diaphragmatic irritation is common but brief
- Regular paracetamol and NSAIDs; opioids as needed for bigger cases
- PONV is common: give prophylaxis and prescribe antiemetics

## Flashcards
- Q: Usual insufflation rate and pressure for laparoscopy?
  - A: 4-6 l/min to 10-15 mmHg.
- Q: Above what IAP does venous return fall?
  - A: Above 20 mmHg (it increases below 10 mmHg).
- Q: Risk factors for subcutaneous emphysema in laparoscopy?
  - A: Surgery over 200 min, 6 or more ports, age over 65, Nissen fundoplication.
- Q: At what IAP do oliguria and anuria occur?
  - A: Oliguria at 15-20 mmHg; anuria above 30 mmHg.
- Q: Ventilator changes for high peak pressures during laparoscopy?
  - A: Check ETT position; pressure control, I:E 1:1, PEEP 5 cmH2O.
