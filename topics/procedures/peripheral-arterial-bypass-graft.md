# Peripheral Arterial Bypass Graft

Common elective vascular operation in arteriopaths: plan for heparin, long and unpredictable duration, slow blood loss and reperfusion.

## Procedure
- Named by where the bypass starts and ends: aorto-femoral, ilio-femoral, femoro-popliteal, femoro-tibial, popliteal-pedal
  - Femoropopliteal: femoral to above-knee popliteal artery
  - Femorodistal: femoral to anterior or posterior tibial artery
  - Femorofemoral crossover: one femoral artery to the other
- Conduit: long saphenous vein or synthetic graft
  - Dacron or PTFE for larger arteries
  - Below the groin, the patient's own vein (usually long saphenous) is often best for femoral and popliteal vessels
- Heparin 3000–5000U usually given before clamping
  - Reverse with protamine 0.5–1mg per 100U heparin, slowly, after unclamping; sometimes not reversed
- Reperfusing an ischaemic limb may cause brief myocardial depression and hypotension

## Key considerations
- Vascular patients with systemic arterial disease
- Heparinisation
- Reperfusion injury

## Preoperative assessment
- Cardiovascular review; usually better tolerated than aortic surgery
- Dynamic cardiac testing rarely needed unless something new, e.g. unstable angina
- Group and crossmatch

## Intraoperative management
- Supine
- Duration 2–6hr and unpredictable; overruns are common
  - Operations >3hr may make regional alone impractical
- Technique
  - GA often preferred; LMA often adequate; cardiostable induction
  - Neuraxial possible: spinal alone for short simple cases, or epidural with sedation
  - Time heparin around neuraxial needle or catheter placement and removal (ASRA)
- Monitoring and access
  - 5-lead ECG
  - Arterial line if instability expected or patient is sick
  - CVP rarely needed
  - At least one large IV cannula (14 or 16G)
  - Temperature monitoring and active warming
- Blood loss: variable, usually slow and insidious; valid group and crossmatch

## Postoperative care
- Stable patients to the general ward
- Those with comorbidities such as heart failure may need HD

## Axillo-bifemoral bypass
- Extraperitoneal trouser graft from axillary artery to both femoral arteries
- Less invasive and better tolerated than open AAA repair, but very long
- For severe aorto-iliac occlusive disease in patients too frail for open aortic surgery; now less common as stenting improves
- Intraoperative
  - Supine; long, about 6hrs
  - GA with intubation
  - Arterial line in the non-donor arm; CVP useful in these frail patients
  - Tunnelling to the groins is very stimulating; remifentanil works well
  - Heparin at clamping
  - Protamine usually given: the long subcutaneous tunnel is prone to haematoma
- Postoperative
  - Graft is superficial: avoid lying on the graft side or other pressure
  - Watch for axillary artery steal (donor arm ischaemia) and brachial plexus injury (check donor arm motor and sensory function)

## Flashcards
- Q: Usual heparin dose before clamping in peripheral bypass?
  - A: 3000–5000 U
- Q: Protamine dose for heparin reversal?
  - A: 0.5–1 mg per 100 U heparin, given slowly after unclamping
- Q: Why is protamine usually given after axillo-bifemoral bypass?
  - A: The long subcutaneous tunnel is at high risk of haematoma
- Q: Which arm takes the arterial line in axillo-bifemoral bypass?
  - A: The non-donor arm
