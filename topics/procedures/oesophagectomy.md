# Oesophagectomy

Long, high-risk operation: RSI, one-lung ventilation, lung protection and careful fluids to protect the anastomosis.

## Procedure
- Removal of part or all of the oesophagus, with the stomach brought up into the chest
- Usually for oesophageal cancer; sometimes for benign stricture or achalasia
- Among the highest perioperative mortality of any elective operation: about 5%
  - 66% of deaths are from sepsis secondary to anastomotic breakdown

## Surgical approaches (determine ventilation strategy)
- Transhiatal: laparotomy and cervical anastomosis
- Ivor Lewis: laparotomy and right thoracotomy (tumour in upper two-thirds)
- Thoracoabdominal: left thoracotomy across the costal margin and diaphragm (tumour in lower two-thirds)
- Minimally invasive: thoracoscopic oesophageal mobilisation, laparoscopic gastric mobilisation and cervical anastomosis

## Key considerations
- Major, prolonged surgery
- RSI
- One-lung ventilation for the thoracic part; bronchial blocker ideal
- Lung-protective strategy: pulmonary morbidity is the commonest cause of postoperative death
- Protect the anastomosis
- AF risk

## Preoperative assessment
- Confirm the surgical approach
- Cardiorespiratory assessment: TTE, consider CPET
- Nutrition: often cachectic; consider preoperative nutritional support and prehabilitation
- Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is common: look for side effects

## Intraoperative management
- Airway and ventilation
  - RSI: high aspiration risk from functional or anatomical obstruction
  - One-lung ventilation needed for the thoracic phase (not for transhiatal)
  - Surgeons may prefer a bronchial blocker, as the trachea is easier to handle
  - With a DLT, change to a single-lumen tube after the thoracic phase if a cervical anastomosis is planned
  - Volatile (immunomodulating) versus propofol
- Lines
  - Large-bore IV access
  - Arterial line
  - Central line, not on the side of a cervical anastomosis (check with surgeon)
- Monitoring: FloTrac for goal-directed fluid therapy
- Fluids: judicious
  - Overload leads to pulmonary oedema and anastomotic dehiscence
  - Goal-directed fluid therapy ideal
- Arrhythmias and hypotension can occur while the thoracic oesophagus is mobilised
- Analgesia: hard, as there are separate thoracotomy and laparotomy wounds
  - Good analgesia improves breathing
  - Opioid-sparing to limit respiratory depression
  - PCA, as patients may be nil by mouth
  - Thoracic epidural is best, but hypotension may increase IV fluid needs
  - Alternatives: paravertebral or erector spinae plane catheter
- Minimally invasive or laparoscopic approach
  - May be prone or semi-prone
  - Bronchial blocker
  - Watch for tension capnothorax: stop CO2 insufflation
  - Extraperitoneal CO2 absorption: EtCO2 rises quickly
  - With significant CO2 surgical emphysema, delay extubation until CO2 is normal

## Postoperative management
- Jejunostomy or nasoduodenal tube for early enteral feeding; never pass an NG tube blind
- Postoperative NIV is contraindicated
- Complications
  - Cardiac: high incidence of AF
  - Pulmonary (pneumonia, ARDS): leading cause of death
  - Anastomotic leak: the most feared surgical complication
    - Presents with sepsis or SIRS
    - Investigations: CT thorax and abdomen with oral and IV contrast acutely; gastrografin swallow at 2-3wks after repair; endoscopy
    - Small or contained leak: conservative (nil by mouth, enteral or parenteral nutrition, IV antibiotics, percutaneous drainage)
    - Large or uncontained leak: endoscopic covered stent or endoscopic vacuum therapy
    - Conduit necrosis: emergency re-operation, conduit resection, cervical oesophagostomy (spit fistula) and delayed reconstruction

## Patients after previous oesophagectomy
- Aspiration risk from loss of the lower oesophageal sphincter: sleep head-up
- Recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy
- Insert orogastric tubes with care: risk of conduit perforation
- Nutritional deficiencies

## Flashcards
- Q: Perioperative mortality of elective oesophagectomy, and main cause?
  - A: About 5%; 66% of deaths are sepsis from anastomotic breakdown
- Q: Commonest cause of postoperative death after oesophagectomy?
  - A: Pulmonary complications (pneumonia, ARDS)
- Q: Why limit IV fluids during oesophagectomy?
  - A: Overload causes pulmonary oedema and anastomotic dehiscence
- Q: Where should the central line not go?
  - A: On the side of a planned cervical anastomosis
