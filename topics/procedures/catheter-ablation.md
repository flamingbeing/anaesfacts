# Catheter Ablation Procedures (AF and VT Ablation)

Long, non-stimulating electrophysiology procedures in a remote site, with heparin, TEE, and risks of bradyarrhythmia and tamponade.

## AF ablation

- Broadly the same concerns as implantable defibrillator insertion
- Long-term success about 70%

### Key issues

- Remote location; poor access to the patient
- Heparinisation and radiation
- TEE, so GA is needed
- Bradyarrhythmias; tamponade risk

### Procedure

- Pulmonary vein ostial segmental disconnection (OSD)
- Left atrial circumferential ablation (LACA): wires passed through the foramen ovale
- Long and non-stimulating (more than 3 hrs)

### Intraoperative management

- Positioning
  - Difficult on the table
  - Watch for brachial plexus injury if arms are above the head
- Monitoring and equipment
  - Arterial line
  - Forced-air warmer above the waist
- Technique
  - Light GA with ETT (because of TEE)
  - TCI propofol and remifentanil
  - Volatiles may suppress AVNRT
  - No patient movement: remifentanil or non-depolarising relaxant
  - Mapping systems correct for breathing, but low tidal volumes may be requested
  - Phenylephrine infusion likely needed
- TEE
  - Atrial thrombus must be excluded with TOE first, hence the ETT
  - Pull the probe back into the upper oesophagus during ablation
- Pacing and ECG
  - Cardiology attach their ECG electrodes and pacing wires before the patient lies down
  - These interfere with the anaesthetic ECG
- Transseptal puncture needs heparin to an ACT of 250-300
- Restrict fluids: large volumes are given as catheter irrigation, and a distended left atrium makes ablation harder
  - Blood loss can be hidden under the drapes
  - Aim for euvolaemia at the end
- Ablation near the pulmonary veins can trigger vagal reflexes (bradycardia, asystole): have atropine or glycopyrrolate ready
- Hypotension: always consider pericardial effusion or tamponade, or aortic puncture

### Cryoballoon ablation

- Phrenic nerve lies close to the right upper pulmonary vein
- Phrenic nerve is paced and diaphragm movement watched, so the patient must not be paralysed at this stage

## VT and VF ablation

- Done less often and much less stable than AF ablation
- GA with ETT, arterial line, CVP line
- Avoid antiarrhythmics (e.g. lignocaine)
- Defibrillator pads mandatory
- TEE
- Heparinisation
- Expect repeated deliberate VT induction
  - Can be very unstable during induced VT
  - May degenerate into VF: tell the operator to cardiovert
- Risk of heart failure afterwards from myocardial stunning and large fluid volumes

## Flashcards

- Q: Why is GA with ETT used for AF ablation?
  - A: TEE is needed to exclude left atrial thrombus first
- Q: ACT target for transseptal puncture in AF ablation?
  - A: 250-300
- Q: Hypotension during AF ablation: what must be excluded?
  - A: Pericardial effusion or tamponade (or aortic puncture)
- Q: Why avoid paralysis during cryoballoon ablation?
  - A: The phrenic nerve is paced and diaphragm movement is monitored
- Q: Long-term success of AF ablation?
  - A: About 70%
