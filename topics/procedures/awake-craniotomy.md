# Awake Craniotomy and Deep Brain Stimulation

Awake surgery allows brain mapping and electrode testing; the anaesthetist balances comfort, a safe unprotected airway and minimal interference with neuromonitoring.

## Advantages
- Testing and preserving function
  - Neurological status can be checked during surgery with continuous feedback to the surgeon; eloquent cortex can be mapped
  - Maximal tumour resection with minimal deficit; aggressive resection gives more accurate diagnosis, lower ICP, less tumour before adjuvant therapy, later recurrence, better survival
- Accurate localisation
  - Stimulating electrodes in movement disorders
  - Resection margins in epilepsy surgery (no interference with electrocorticography)
- Avoids GA risks: difficult airway, haemodynamic instability
- Less need for high dependency care
- Shorter hospital stay, lower cost

## Disadvantages
- Needs cooperation: hard with confused or anxious patients, or Parkinson's disease
- No secured airway: aspiration, obstruction
- Cannot control ventilation if ICP is raised
- Seizure risk

## Indications
- Anatomical
  - Resection or biopsy of tumours near eloquent cortex (motor, sensory, speech)
  - Resection or obliteration of vascular lesions supplying functionally important areas
- Physiological: deep brain stimulation (e.g. subthalamic nucleus) for Parkinson's disease and dystonia; electrodes localise deep nuclei
- Pharmacological: epilepsy surgery (resection of seizure foci); GA interferes with electrocorticography used to set margins
- No functional goal: faster recovery and earlier discharge (e.g. ventriculostomy, brain endoscopy)

## Contraindications
- Patient
  - Reduced consciousness
  - Cannot lie still for many hours (~4h): confusion, anxiety, communication difficulty (severe dysphasia, language barrier)
  - OSA or morbid obesity (relative)
  - Reflux disease (relative)
- Surgical
  - Low occipital tumour needing prone position
  - Large vascular tumour
  - Significant dural involvement: painful (relative)

## Main aims
- Comfort: anxiolysis, sedation, analgesia, antiemesis, seizure prophylaxis
- Homeostasis: airway, breathing, circulation, brain relaxation
- Minimal anaesthetic interference with electrophysiological monitoring

## Preoperative assessment
- Pathology
  - Diagnosis, indication, consent
  - Detailed neurology: cranial nerve palsies, pupils, upper and lower limbs; signs of raised ICP
  - Seizures: type, pattern, past and current anticonvulsants, drug levels
  - CT/MRI: site, size, vascularity (bleeding risk), lesion type
  - Seizure prophylaxis or treatment; complications of disease or treatment
- Patient selection
  - Informed, motivated, able to consent
  - Can cooperate and lie still throughout
  - Low PONV risk
- Routine: comorbidities, airway (difficulty, apnoea risk), heart and lungs

## Preoperative preparation
- Psychological preparation: explain the procedure, sequence, duration, discomfort, risks and brain testing; reassure
- Premedication
  - Antihypertensives: BP tends to be higher when awake
  - Anxiolytics: avoid with raised ICP, obesity, OSA or respiratory depression risk
  - Continue anticonvulsants on the day
  - Omit Parkinson's drugs to induce the "off" state
  - Dexamethasone for tumours: less cerebral oedema and nausea
- Book an HD/ICU bed

## Local anaesthesia
- Infiltration at Mayfield pin sites
- Field block around the craniotomy
- Scalp blocks
- LA-soaked pledgets on the dura

## Monitored conscious sedation
- Apnoea and airway obstruction risk
  - Lateral position least likely to obstruct
  - Emergency airway equipment ready throughout
- Short-acting, rapidly titratable drugs so the patient can be woken for testing
- Propofol 50-100mg/h with remifentanil 0.02-0.1 mcg/kg/min (TIVA/TCI with BIS)
  - Sedation and analgesia, easy, rapid on/off, little respiratory depression if titrated and monitored
- Dexmedetomidine 0.2-0.7 mcg/kg/h
  - For: sedation and analgesia, rousable to voice, no respiratory depression
  - Against: slower offset and harder to titrate, hypotension, bradycardia, oversedation, impaired neurocognitive testing after stopping

## Asleep-awake-asleep technique
- Indications: children, intolerance of sedation, intolerance of long surgery
- Airway options: awake LMA under topical anaesthesia, asleep LMA, fibreoptic-assisted LMA or ETT, cuffed oropharyngeal airway
- Short-acting drugs
- Asleep phase (craniotomy and dural opening)
  - Propofol and remifentanil induction with an LMA
    - Controls the airway: less obstruction and hypoventilation; deep anaesthesia without oversedation risk
    - Controls oxygenation and ventilation
    - Little coughing or straining: good conditions, no uncooperative patient
    - No neuromuscular blockade needed
  - Consider doing LA blocks awake first to confirm they work
- Awake phase (cortical stimulation)
  - Reduce remifentanil to 0.005-0.01 mcg/kg/min once the dura is open and tumour exposed, to restore spontaneous breathing
  - Reduce or stop propofol once breathing returns
  - Remove LMA as the patient wakes
- Asleep phase (resection and closure)
  - Re-induce with propofol and remifentanil; reinsert LMA
  - Morphine for transition analgesia at the end

## Brain mapping
- After the pial surface is exposed and the lesion confirmed with frameless stereotaxy
- Cortical stimulation with an Ojemann stimulator
- Psychologist tests
  - Motor: limb movement and strength; positive = cannot make a commanded movement, or involuntary movement
  - Sensory: numbness, paraesthesia
  - Speech: serial counting, days of the week, naming objects; positive = hesitation, speech arrest, dysnomia

## Intraoperative management
- Comfort
  - Well-padded table, care with head and limbs; let the patient settle themselves
  - Adjust sedation and analgesia to surgical events; dural traction near the middle meningeal artery or sagittal sinus can hurt
  - Tight depth control; keep the patient still
  - Antiemetic prophylaxis
  - Clear drapes for monitoring and less claustrophobia; unobstructed airway access
  - Avoid surgical delays; minimise noise and staff traffic
- Homeostasis
  - Recheck head position after pinning for comfort and easy breathing
  - Keep the airway open: lateral or semi-lateral, nasopharyngeal airway, LMA
  - Adequate oxygenation, ventilation and haemodynamic stability
  - Causes of hypertension: being awake, pain, cold, full bladder, omitted Parkinson's drugs (autonomic dysfunction)
  - Prevent brain swelling; keep normothermic; watch for complications
- Neuromonitoring: choose drugs that interfere least; patient alert and cooperative for testing

## Complications
- Haemodynamic instability (more common than under GA)
  - Hypertension or tachycardia (e.g. pinning) raises ICP and bleeding
  - Hypotension (e.g. blood loss, uncommon) risks cerebral infarction
  - Bradycardia (e.g. trigeminocardiac reflex)
  - Treat: adequate depth; beta-blockers or vasopressors
- Airway obstruction or respiratory depression
  - Hypoxaemia and hypercarbia raise ICP
  - Risk factors: BMI > 30, OSA, supine more than lateral
  - Treat: less sedation, jaw thrust, adjuncts (bag-mask, nasopharyngeal airway, LMA); emergency airway kit ready
  - Rescue airway: blind nasal, direct or fibreoptic intubation, LMA
- Pulmonary aspiration
- Venous air embolism
  - Chest pain, dyspnoea, cough, hypotension, tachycardia, arrhythmia, hypoxaemia, falling ETCO2
  - Risk: high negative intrathoracic pressure when upright
  - Treat: Trendelenburg; aspirate air from the right atrium via a CVC
- Headache
- Focal deficit
  - Transient: oedema and inflammation around the resection
  - Persistent
  - Mapping is less reliable with pre-existing deficits
- Seizures (3-18%)
  - Mostly during mapping or resection from cortical (especially motor) stimulation
  - Higher in epilepsy surgery than tumour resection
  - Treat: stop stimulation, cold saline on the cortex, support ABC, propofol, thiopentone or midazolam, prevent injury
- Nausea and vomiting (6-8%)
  - Less than under GA
  - From handling dura, temporal lobe or meningeal vessels, or deep resection
  - Treat: less brain traction; anticholinergics
- Conversion to GA (2-6%)

## Postoperative management
- HD/ICU: haemodynamic, neurological, fluid and electrolyte monitoring; seizure prophylaxis
- Analgesia: paracetamol, codeine phosphate
- BP control
- Isotonic fluids to euvolaemia
- Early enteral nutrition within 24-48h once all planned surgery is done
- Stress ulcer prophylaxis: esomeprazole IV 40mg once daily in the morning until enteral feeding is established
- Thromboprophylaxis: TED stockings, intermittent pneumatic compression

## Deep brain stimulation: overview
- Uses
  - Parkinsonism: shortens "off" time
  - Severe disabling tremor not controlled by drugs
  - Psychiatric disorders, e.g. OCD
  - Chronic pain
- Mechanism: high suppressive frequency 130-180Hz damps overactive nuclei
  - Subthalamic nucleus: improves many Parkinson's symptoms; preferred target unless contraindicated
  - Thalamus: severe tremor
  - Pallidum: severe dyskinesia
  - Benefit: L-dopa and dopamine agonist doses fall to 50% of preoperative levels
- Risks
  - Bleeding and intracranial haemorrhage: the main concern, though overall <1% (hypertension may cause capillary bleeding as the probe crosses brain)
  - Seizures, neurological deficit
  - Device infection; lead migration or fracture (care with activities such as surfing or diving)
- Stimulation side effects (usually adjustable): altered gait or speech, mood change, weight gain, MRI restrictions
- Components
  - Multicontact quadripolar electrodes placed in the deep brain, one or both sides
  - Plastic ring and cap in a burr hole to fix electrodes to the skull
  - Single- or dual-channel internal pulse generator (IPG); battery lasts between 2 and 5 yr and is replaced with the generator
  - Extension cable tunnelled under the skin from the head to the chest or abdomen
- Two stages
  - Stage 1: electrode implantation, usually monitored anaesthesia care ("awake and sedate")
    - Stereotactic frame placed under LA and sedation, CT scan, return to theatre for implantation
  - Stage 2: IPG placed under the skin under GA
    - Leads tunnelled under the scalp, down the neck to the IPG site (chest wall or abdomen)
    - Programmed externally with a handheld device

## Sedation vs GA for electrode implantation
- Monitored anaesthesia care is ideal in theory
  - GA may affect microelectrode recording (MER), depending on the nucleus, and prevents macrostimulation testing of benefit and side effects
- Consider GA for dystonia (involuntary neck movements when awake may injure the cervical spine) or patient choice

## Three ways to find the target nucleus
- 1. Frame-based imaging with CT (or MRI): frame coordinates plus fixed offsets to the target
- 2. Microelectrode recording (MER)
  - Sedation should barely affect subcortical activity
  - Dexmedetomidine is best; propofol and opioids have small effects; avoid benzodiazepines
  - Propofol can be used, titrated up to Cet 1.5 (dose-dependent effect)
- 3. Clinical testing (macrostimulation) at different points and frequencies by a neurologist or neuro nurse

## DBS: preoperative preparation
- Usually first and only case of the list
- Patient must be in the "off" state for testing
  - Usually admitted 1 day before
  - Last Parkinson's dose the night before; discuss with surgeons if there are concerns (e.g. severe "off" state)
- Contraindications to awake technique: severe dystonia, claustrophobia, cannot lie flat
- Explain the stages: sedated portion can take up to 4h or more, then GA
  - Patient must perform tasks; will get stiffer and more uncomfortable with time, so patience matters
- Valid group and crossmatch

## DBS stage 1: electrode implantation under sedation
- Preparation
  - IV cannula: avoid the back of the hand if possible (movement testing); reliable line for infusions; small gauge is fine (22/20G)
  - Arterial line optional: useful for BP in a long case (frequent cuff inflation is uncomfortable); mainly for comorbidities
    - ICP is not an issue in Parkinson's, so sedation and ETCO2 are less critical than in tumour surgery
  - Nasal cannula with ETCO2
  - Start dexmedetomidine (Precedex) in the induction room: load at 0.5 mcg/kg/h, then 0.2mcg/kg/h; gauges response to sedation before theatre
  - Urinary catheter in theatre on the ward bed (propofol bolus 10-20mg can help)
  - Consider an NG tube for antiparkinsonian drugs
- Frame application (on the ward bed in theatre)
  - First held by two probes in both external auditory canals (painful); warn that the frame will feel tight over the ears
  - Sedate with dexmedetomidine with or without propofol boluses (fewer pumps on the bed)
  - Oxygen by nasal cannula with ETCO2
  - Surgeons infiltrate LA before pinning; fentanyl 25-50mcg helps tolerance
  - Warn of a ring-like tightness lasting up to 5 minutes as screws enter the outer table of the skull
  - Warm with a forced-air blanket
- CT scan to define targets relative to the frame
  - Quick; surgeons check frame placement
  - Light dexmedetomidine; take oxygen, nasal cannula and emergency drugs (phenylephrine, ephedrine)
- Back in theatre on the operating table
  - BIS may be hard to place or get wiped off; usually clear of the posterior incision
  - IV cefazolin 2g before incision
  - Ondansetron to reduce retching, vomiting and aspiration
  - Warmed fluids, forced-air warming, comfortable positioning
- Electrode insertion: 1 x each side
  - Burr hole: warn about drill noise; deepest sedation here (may become apnoeic)
  - Stop propofol and dexmedetomidine completely after the burr hole
- Microelectrode recording
  - Reduce electrical interference: turn off warming blanket and calf pumps, unplug mains devices
  - Near the subthalamic nucleus the sound becomes "whiny" like raindrops on a roof
  - Beta activity in the STN shows as dark blue on the recording display
- Macrostimulation testing: patient must follow commands, so avoid oversedation (e.g. dexmedetomidine at 0.1)
  - Weakness or numbness not expected (sensory and motor cortex)
  - Rigidity
  - Speech (e.g. "today is a sunny day")
  - Eye movements: ophthalmoplegia (possible CN3 involvement)
  - Bradykinesia: drawing small and big stars, turning a door knob
- Dexmedetomidine can stop once both sides are tested and closure begins
- Blood pressure: aim SBP < 140 (some surgeons ask < 120) to limit bleeding, using sedation and analgesia
  - Surgeons asking about BP usually means tunnelling is near
  - IV labetalol, hydralazine, sometimes nicardipine
- Frame removal
  - Remove pins with an Allen key, supporting head and neck throughout
  - Know how to remove the frame urgently at any time if airway access is needed
  - Have a second-generation LMA (e.g. AuraGain) ready for rescue and as an intubation conduit
- Restart Parkinson's drugs in PACU if there will be a wait; make sure they are prescribed
- Neurosurgical high dependency afterwards
- Assisted daily activities may be needed; no driving for 6 weeks

## DBS stage 2: IPG insertion and tunnelling under GA
- Stimulation usually starts two to four weeks after lead placement; oedema around new electrodes ("microlesion effect") can confuse early assessment
- Consider redosing Madopar (levodopa) if needed, e.g. crushed in water after stage 1
- GA (consider RSI with bad reflux)
  - LMA (AuraGain or ProSeal with straight connector) or ETT; circuit to the left to expose the right side
- Insert an orogastric tube
  - Give crushed Madopar via the tube with water flushes
  - Do not aspirate the tube, even before removal, so the drug is absorbed
- Horseshoe headrest after induction
- IPG below the clavicle or in the abdomen (as for VP shunt); keep ECG leads clear of the field (right-sided lead on the back of the shoulder)
- Paralyse and give fentanyl before tunnelling, as for a VP shunt

## Battery replacement
- No lead handling in the head or neck: LA with sedation is often enough
- GA for uncooperative patients or children

## Anaesthesia for patients with a DBS in place
- Similar to cardiac pacemakers, but patients can switch some devices off themselves
  - Diathermy can burn tissue around the electrodes or reprogram the device
  - Devices can cause monitoring artefact
- Identify the device and how severe symptoms are when it is off
  - Oral medication may be needed if it must be switched off and symptoms are severe
- Ideally switch it back on before waking
  - Rigidity can follow switch-off; rarely, ventilation is needed if rigidity impairs breathing

## DBS and electromagnetic interference
- MRI
  - Risks: heating, electrode movement, increased stimulation, device damage
  - Usually possible if manufacturer guidance is followed and scan time is kept short
- Diathermy (as for pacemakers)
  - Bipolar where possible
  - Monopolar: low-voltage mode, lowest power, return pad as far as possible from the device and its leads (subclavicular space, neck, head)
  - Never use short-wave diathermy: radiofrequency currents heat the electrodes
- Defibrillation: no set guidelines, not contraindicated
  - Pads as far from the device as possible; check the device afterwards
- ECT, radiofrequency ablation and peripheral nerve stimulation are safe if the stimulator is off and probes are far from the generator

## Flashcards
- Q: Typical sedation doses for awake craniotomy?
  - A: Propofol 50-100mg/h with remifentanil 0.02-0.1 mcg/kg/min, or dexmedetomidine 0.2-0.7 mcg/kg/h.
- Q: Incidence of seizures in awake craniotomy and first-line treatment?
  - A: 3-18%; stop stimulation and irrigate the cortex with cold saline.
- Q: Rate of conversion to GA during awake craniotomy?
  - A: 2-6%.
- Q: Preferred sedative during DBS microelectrode recording?
  - A: Dexmedetomidine; avoid benzodiazepines; propofol up to Cet 1.5.
- Q: Blood pressure target during DBS electrode insertion?
  - A: SBP < 140 (some surgeons ask < 120) to limit bleeding.
