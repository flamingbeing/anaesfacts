# Endovascular Thrombectomy for Ischaemic Stroke

Time is brain: move fast, hold blood pressure in target, choose GA or sedation, keep the patient still in a remote site.

## Key points
- Time is brain
- Blood pressure control
- GA versus monitored anaesthesia care (MAC) decision
- Remote location
- Immobility
- Neuroprotection
- Typical GA: sevoflurane, arterial line, phenylephrine infusion around induction; MAC is an option
- Carotid stenting: pacing pads on
- High-risk patient: ask the radiologist to insert a central line

## Indications
- Decide on thrombolysis and thrombectomy referral within 4.5 h of onset; ideally arterial puncture within 6 h
- Untreated ischaemic brain ages 3.6 yr for every hour
- Most benefit: large vessel anterior circulation stroke within 6hrs
- Extended window: DAWN and DIFFUSE 3 trials support EVT up to 24hrs
- Wider selection now: posterior circulation, larger infarct cores
- 2026 AHA/ASA guideline: EVT for selected patients up to 24hrs from onset, including large ischaemic core and basilar artery occlusion
- Not currently for medium vessel occlusion (e.g. M1/2): DISTAL and ESCALE-MePO trials showed no benefit as of 2025

## Procedure
- Access usually femoral artery
  - Radial or brachial if aortofemoral disease
  - Very rarely direct internal carotid puncture
- Guide catheter into the internal carotid, then microcatheter and guidewire to the occlusion
- Wire removed, devices advanced to the clot:
  - Stent retrievers first line: opened in the clot to restore flow, or deployed distally and pulled back to trap it
  - Aspiration catheters placed proximal to the clot
- Some give a single heparin dose to reduce catheter-related embolism

## Surgical complications
- Clot propagation
- Vessel rupture and haemorrhagic transformation

## Main considerations
- Speed: minimise delay; door to reperfusion <90min is good
- Haemodynamic control:
  - Hypotension reduces penumbral flow; hypertension risks cerebral oedema and haemorrhagic transformation
  - Outcome versus blood pressure is a U-shaped curve
  - Targets differ by phase
  - More than 60% of patients arrive hypertensive: baseline hypertension, plus a Cushing response to oedema mass effect and a stress response
- Others:
  - Neuroprotection
  - Delicate procedure: patient must be still
  - Poor airway access
  - Remote site
  - Radiation

## Preoperative assessment
- Be as quick as possible
- Often elderly with many comorbidities
- Unfasted
- Often cannot give a history
- Document baseline neurology
- Use admission investigations; rarely time for more
- Transfers from other hospitals need urgent reassessment on arrival
- May have had rtPA

## Choice of technique
- GA versus MAC is controversial
- GA benefits:
  - Immobility; possibly better recanalisation rates
  - Airway control: less hypoxia and aspiration
  - No emergency conversion to GA (4-10% risk with sedation)
- MAC / conscious sedation benefits:
  - Faster set-up, less delay
  - Less hypotension and haemodynamic swing
- Local anaesthesia without an anaesthetist is rarely used
- Evidence:
  - Older non-randomised studies showed worse outcomes with GA, but with selection bias
  - Single-centre RCTs: SIESTA, AnStroke, GOLIATH
  - Recent RCTs: GASS trial 2022 (protocolised anaesthesia, no difference in mRS) and AMETIS trial 2023 (pragmatic, no difference in functional outcome)
- Practical factors:
  - Cooperative patient favours MAC
  - Sick, unstable patient favours MAC
  - Complex procedure (distal, tandem, high clot burden) favours GA
  - Unfasted or vomiting favours GA
  - Institutional practice

## General anaesthesia
- Intubate; RSI if fasting status is in doubt
  - LMA possible but hard to troubleshoot remotely
- TIVA versus volatile:
  - Evidence lacking; observational data hint at benefit from TIVA
  - Nearly all RCTs used TIVA
  - BIS usually possible
  - Volatile need not reach 1 MAC; about 0.5-0.6 may be enough
- Sedation, when used, is kept very light

## Monitoring
- Arterial line placed during induction to save time
  - Will delay the start but helps blood pressure control
  - Not routine; can omit if not needed
- Central line may be needed for noradrenaline if ejection fraction is very low (e.g. 20%)

## Intraoperative management
- IV phenylephrine infusion: blood pressure usually falls after induction
- Consider atracurium infusion to prevent movement
- NG tube and heparin not needed from the anaesthetist

## Blood pressure targets
- Before recanalisation: SNACC 2014 consensus, SBP 140-180mmHg to perfuse the penumbra
- After successful recanalisation: SBP 120-160 to balance ischaemia against hyperperfusion and haemorrhagic conversion (expert lecture 2025)
- 2026 ASA/AHA guidelines: keep BP <180; no need to target <140

## Postoperative management
- Decide whether to extubate

## Other
- ZODIAC trial: better neurological outcome with supine positioning
- Cerebral embolectomy (open): thrombectomy and intra-arterial lysis are standard, but surgery may suit large atherosclerotic plaque or foreign body; manage as a craniotomy
- Modified Rankin Scale (mRS): 6-level ordinal scale of disability after stroke

## Flashcards
- Q: What is the SBP target before recanalisation (SNACC 2014)?
  - A: 140-180mmHg.
- Q: What is the SBP target after successful recanalisation?
  - A: 120-160.
- Q: How long is the extended EVT window and which trials support it?
  - A: Up to 24hrs; DAWN and DIFFUSE 3.
- Q: What is the risk of conversion from sedation to GA?
  - A: 4-10%.
- Q: What door to reperfusion time is good?
  - A: <90min.
