# Microlaryngeal Surgery, Microlaryngoscopy and Panendoscopy

Shared, often difficult airway surgery where the choice of ventilation technique is central, and stimulation is high with movement to be avoided.

## Key issues
- Airway surgery
  - Shared airway
  - Difficult airway
  - Choice of ventilation technique
- Highly stimulating; avoid movement

## Ventilation techniques

### Tracheal intubation (microlaryngeal tube)
- Advantages
  - Adequate surgical field and laryngeal access in most cases
  - Still surgical field
  - Paralysis keeps the patient and vocal cords still
  - Stable, controlled ventilation
  - Reliable continuous monitoring of FiO2, EtCO2, airway pressure and anaesthetic gases
  - Good airway protection
  - Suits long procedures
- Disadvantages
  - Mostly unsuitable for posterior glottic pathology (posterior glottic or subglottic stenosis, transglottic tumours)
- Laser surgery needs a laser tube

### Spontaneous ventilation (insufflation and TIVA)
- Advantages
  - Unobstructed surgical field and free access to the larynx
  - Dynamic airway function and obstruction can be assessed
- Disadvantages
  - Unprotected airway; aspiration risk
  - Moving surgical field makes precise or laser surgery hard
  - FiO2, EtCO2 and anaesthetic gases cannot be measured accurately
  - Theatre pollution with anaesthetic gases
  - Depth hard to titrate; patient may move
  - Hypercarbia and hypoxia; may not suit significant lung or cardiovascular disease
  - Higher FiO2 needed, so airway fire risk
- Best for short, uncomplicated cases

### Apnoeic intermittent ventilation
- Advantages
  - Unobstructed field and free laryngeal access
  - Still surgical field
  - Paralysis keeps the patient and vocal cords still
  - Gas exchange can be controlled intermittently
- Disadvantages
  - Unprotected airway; aspiration risk
  - FiO2, EtCO2 and anaesthetic gases cannot be measured accurately
  - Slows surgery
  - Repeated tube passage risks airway trauma and disrupts the field
- Best for short, uncomplicated cases

### Jet ventilation
- Frequency: low or high
- Site: supraglottic, infraglottic or transtracheal
- Advantages
  - Unobstructed or minimally obstructed field and access
  - Fairly still surgical field
  - Paralysis keeps the patient and vocal cords still
  - Automated ventilators can monitor and control FiO2, EtCO2 (low frequency only), driving pressure, airway pressure and PEEP
  - Suits long procedures
- Disadvantages
  - Barotrauma risk; needs an open airway for free exhalation
  - High frequency jet ventilation (HFJV) needs special equipment and training
  - Manual and transtracheal techniques have limitations
  - May not suit significant cardiopulmonary disease

## Jet ventilation sites

### 1) Supraglottic
- Jet needle fixed to the proximal end of the suspension laryngoscope, aimed down at the glottis
- Usual method for microlaryngeal surgery
- Advantages
  - Unobstructed surgical access
  - Most effective way to raise airway pressure, especially with poor compliance (counterintuitive; due to Venturi effect and expiratory impedance)
- Disadvantages
  - Vocal cords vibrate; surgeons often ask for pauses during fine work
  - Highest barotrauma risk (reflected jet plus entrained air create adverse pressure gradients)
  - Ventilation easily lost with scope position, causing poor gas exchange or gastric inflation
  - EtCO2 and airway pressure monitoring unreliable
  - FiO2 varies because of entrainment
  - Can blow blood, laser smoke or tumour debris (papillomas) down into the trachea

### 2) Infraglottic (subglottic)
- Thin semi-rigid catheter (e.g. Hunsaker, Benjet) passed through the glottis into the mid trachea
  - Side holes help the catheter centre itself in the trachea
- Usual method for rigid bronchoscopy
- Advantages
  - Little vocal cord movement
  - Lowest barotrauma risk: the airway above stays open for exhalation throughout the cycle
  - Ventilation does not depend on scope position
  - Reliable airway pressure and EtCO2 monitoring through extra lumens
  - Steady FiO2, as less room air is entrained
  - Upward expiratory flow clears blood and debris out of the airway rather than into the lungs
- Disadvantages
  - Catheter may block access to the posterior larynx or subglottic lesions

### 3) Transtracheal
- Cannula through the cricothyroid membrane, aimed towards the carina
- A form of infraglottic ventilation with similar pros and cons, except:
- Advantages
  - Unobstructed surgical access with little vocal cord movement
- Disadvantages
  - Most invasive route; misplacement or dislodgement risks subcutaneous emphysema or mediastinitis
  - Cannula kinks or blocks more easily
- Watch for chest rise

## Microlaryngeal surgery

### Preoperative assessment
- Careful airway assessment: the site and nature of the lesion (glottic or subglottic) decides the ventilation plan
  - Stridor
  - CT, nasendoscopy
  - Backup plans to secure the airway, with the ENT surgeon scrubbed and ready
- Laryngeal cancer
  - Difficult airway from local inflammation, post-radiation oedema or fibrosis, and previous surgery

### Intraoperative management
- GA
- Ventilation strategy
  - Microlaryngeal tube (MLT, 5.5 to 6.0mm): standard for non-laser, anterior glottic surgery
  - Spontaneous ventilation: common in children (e.g. microlaryngobronchoscopy) to assess dynamic airway function
  - Apnoeic intermittent ventilation: short, uncomplicated procedures
  - Jet ventilation (HFJV): posterior glottic lesions, subglottic stenosis
- TIVA for tubeless techniques
- Paralysis preferred to stop vocal cord and patient movement
- Topical local anaesthetic reduces stimulation
- Dexamethasone to limit airway swelling afterwards
- Glycopyrrolate as an antisialagogue to improve the surgical view

### Postoperative care
- Smooth emergence; avoid bucking
- Post-extubation stridor
  - Laryngospasm: extubate awake; CPAP often helps
  - Glottic oedema: dexamethasone
  - Haematoma, bleeding
- Expect a difficult airway in patients returning for re-exploration

## Microlaryngoscopy
- Examination of the larynx with an operating microscope, e.g. via a suspension laryngoscope

### Indications
- Diagnostic: assess laryngeal lesions, biopsy suspected pathology, investigate hoarseness
  - Staging: often part of a panendoscopy (including oesophagus and bronchi)
- Therapeutic: endoscopic resection of laryngeal cancer (laser, cold steel, microdebrider or cautery)

### Contraindications
- Unstable cervical spine
- Coagulopathy
- Larynx cannot be exposed (e.g. retrognathia): consider alternatives to direct laryngoscopy

### Preoperative assessment
- Usually elderly smokers: assess cardiovascular and respiratory systems thoroughly
- Careful airway assessment

### Intraoperative management
- Position: supine, shoulder roll, head extended, head down
- Airway: microlaryngoscopy tube, or tubeless (e.g. jet ventilation)
- Topical local anaesthetic to the cords
- Short-acting paralysis
- Consider dexamethasone to reduce oedema
- Consider glycopyrrolate to reduce secretions

## Panendoscopy
- Endoscopic examination of the upper aerodigestive tract, combining:
  - Rhinoscopy, nasopharyngoscopy
  - Inspection of the oral cavity and oropharynx
  - Direct laryngoscopy and hypopharyngoscopy
  - Oesophagoscopy
  - Bronchoscopy

### Indications
- Biopsy of a tumour not reachable under local anaesthesia in clinic
  - Most upper aerodigestive squamous cell carcinomas are assessed endoscopically under GA
- Exclude a synchronous second cancer
- Most patients do not need every component
  - Rhinoscopy is usually done in clinic with topical anaesthesia
  - Bronchoscopy has low yield if the chest X-ray is normal and the patient has no symptoms

### Intraoperative management
- Usually GA, especially if bronchoscopy is needed
- Airway: an MLT tube is usually enough
  - Tubeless techniques may be needed (see ventilation techniques above)
- As for microlaryngoscopy
  - Local anaesthetic to the cords
  - Short-acting paralysis
  - Consider dexamethasone to reduce oedema
  - Consider glycopyrrolate to reduce secretions
- Positioning
  - Table head turned 90° away from the anaesthetist
  - Left arm tucked to allow the suspension laryngoscope support
  - Neck extended on a shoulder roll
  - Head of bed raised 15 to 30°
- Usual order (depends on the problem)
  - Laryngoscopy
  - Bronchoscopy
  - Oesophagoscopy
  - Nasopharyngoscopy
- May use imaging with topical stain, and biopsy

## Flashcards
- Q: What size microlaryngeal tube is standard for non-laser anterior glottic surgery?
  - A: 5.5 to 6.0mm.
- Q: Which jet ventilation site carries the highest barotrauma risk, and which the lowest?
  - A: Supraglottic has the highest; infraglottic has the lowest because the upper airway stays open for exhalation.
- Q: Which ventilation technique suits posterior glottic lesions and subglottic stenosis?
  - A: Jet ventilation (HFJV), as a tube blocks posterior glottic access.
- Q: Why give glycopyrrolate and dexamethasone for microlaryngoscopy?
  - A: Glycopyrrolate dries secretions for a better view; dexamethasone limits airway oedema.
- Q: What is the main risk of transtracheal jet ventilation if the cannula is misplaced?
  - A: Subcutaneous emphysema or mediastinitis.
