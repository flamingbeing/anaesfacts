# Non-Intubated VATS

Video-assisted thoracic surgery under deep sedation or LMA anaesthesia with spontaneous breathing and no lung isolation.

## How it differs from standard VATS
- Deep sedation, spontaneous ventilation, no ETT or lung isolation
- Good analgesia needed
- Cough suppressed with a vagal nerve block
- May need conversion to GA with intubation
- Main drawback: the patient must breathe spontaneously
  - Tolerated for short cases
  - For long cases, GA with controlled one-lung ventilation (OLV) is better

## Ventilation goals
- Keep spontaneous breathing adequate
  - Aim for a respiratory rate of 12 to 20 per minute so the lung collapses well for the surgeon
  - In sedated patients, opioids control respiratory rate well
  - If hypoventilation is significant, gentle mask-assisted ventilation may be needed after telling the surgeons
- Supplemental oxygen by nasal cannula at 3-4 litres/minute or Venturi mask
- Avoid excessive hypercapnia: a good end-tidal CO2 trace plus arterial gases before and after the surgical pneumothorax are usually enough

## Technique
- Sedation: consider securing the airway if upper airway obstruction is a concern
- GA with LMA and spontaneous breathing:
  - BIS helps judge depth
  - Target TV > 4 ml/kg, RR 12–20/min
  - Permissive hypercapnia is accepted, with continuous ETCO2 monitoring

## Analgesia
- Order of painful stimuli:
  - Port insertion first: skin to parietal pleura
  - Then lung manipulation and traction on chest structures: visceral pleura
- Chest wall and parietal pleura: regional techniques work well
  - Paravertebral block
  - Intercostal block, percutaneous or thoracoscopic
  - Intrapleural analgesia
  - Thoracic epidural
- Visceral pleura:
  - IV opioids
  - Surgeon infiltrates the vagus nerve (2–3 ml 1% lidocaine) to suppress cough
  - Surgeon infiltrates the phrenic nerve if diaphragm movement is excessive
- Postoperative analgesia: best approach still unsettled
  - Epidurals were favoured for open thoracotomy
  - For VATS, epidural has been reported to offer no advantage over IV PCA
  - Continuous intercostal-intrapleural or paravertebral catheters look promising

## Cough suppression
- Coughing is a problem near central intrathoracic lesions; suppression must be effective but short, because a returning cough reflex reduces postoperative chest complications
- Options:
  - IV opioids, for short procedures so suppression wears off quickly
  - Nebulised lidocaine
  - Ipsilateral stellate ganglion block
  - Thoracoscopic vagal block during surgery

## Conversion to intubated GA
- Occasionally unavoidable: significant bleeding, pleural adhesions, inadequate anaesthesia
- Plan A: lung isolation through the existing SGA
- Plan B: intubation through the existing SGA with fibreoptic guidance
- Plan C: laryngoscopy and intubation in the lateral position
- Plan D: emergency supine intubation
  - Call for theatre help at once
  - Surgeons cover the wound with a sterile adhesive drape, clear drapes and trolleys, consider a rapid chest drain

## Other considerations
- As for VATS; consider invasive arterial pressure monitoring

## Emergence
- Gentle recruitment to TV 6 ml/kg
- Remove the SGA in theatre, or transfer to PACU on a face mask once ETCO2 is back to normal

## Flashcards
- Q: What respiratory rate is targeted during non-intubated VATS?
  - A: 12 to 20 per minute
- Q: What tidal volume is targeted with an LMA during NIVATS?
  - A: Above 4 ml/kg
- Q: How is cough suppressed during NIVATS?
  - A: Vagus nerve infiltration with 2–3 ml 1% lidocaine, IV opioids, nebulised lidocaine or stellate ganglion block
- Q: What is Plan A if NIVATS must convert to lung isolation?
  - A: Lung isolation through the existing SGA
- Q: What tidal volume is used for recruitment at emergence?
  - A: 6 ml/kg
