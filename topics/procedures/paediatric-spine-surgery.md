# Paediatric Spine Surgery and Scoliosis

Mostly scoliosis correction and neural tube defect surgery: prone positioning, neuromonitoring-friendly anaesthesia, major blood loss and cardiorespiratory comorbidity.

## Range of surgery
- Commonest: bony spine surgery for idiopathic or secondary scoliosis
- Spinal cord surgery mostly for neural tube defects and associations: tethered cord, diastematomyelia, syringomyelia
- Tumours less common; vascular lesions (e.g. AVMs) rare; disc disease rare, but seen in adolescents
- Spinal cord injury surgery
- Intrathecal baclofen pump insertion for spasm in cerebral palsy

## Scoliosis
### Definition
- Fixed structural lateral curve of the spine with vertebral rotation
- Severity measured by Cobb angle on standing X-ray
- Congenital or acquired; acquired is idiopathic or non-idiopathic (especially neuromuscular)

### Congenital scoliosis
- Any age at presentation; from failed vertebral segmentation (bar) or failed formation (hemivertebra)
- Often with other vertebral anomalies, e.g. spina bifida, tethered cord
- Often part of a wider syndrome, e.g. Goldenhar, VACTERL
- May involve renal, cardiac, respiratory or neurological anomalies

### Idiopathic scoliosis
- 70% of cases (majority)
- Early onset (infantile, <5 years) or late onset (adolescent)
  - Infantile onset (before 8 yr) has the worst prognosis: untreated, likely cardiopulmonary failure in middle age
- Mostly adolescents; severe curves more common in girls
- Severe curves narrow the chest: restrictive defect
  - Rarely significant if <65°; severe respiratory compromise probably only >100°

### Neuromuscular scoliosis
- Myopathies (e.g. Duchenne) and upper or lower motor neurone disease (e.g. cerebral palsy, spinal muscular atrophy)
- Breathing further limited by weak cough, bulbar palsy with recurrent aspiration, low ventilatory capacity
- Curve usually spans most of the thoracolumbar spine
- More complications than idiopathic, including bleeding and respiratory failure

### Other causes
- Neurofibromatosis
- Mesenchymal disease: dwarfism, Marfan syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, osteogenesis imperfecta
- Trauma (vertebral fracture) or irradiation

## Neural tube defects (myelodysplasia)
- Failed fusion of the neural groove in the first month of gestation; incidence 1 per 1000 live births
- Meningocele: meninges herniate; myelomeningocele: neural tissue herniates
- Myelomeningocele usually lumbosacral but can be at any level; high thoracic lesions give severe deficit

### Spina bifida
- Failed fusion of the vertebral arches
- Spina bifida occulta in 5–10%: skin covers the defect, usually at L5; often a dimple or hairy patch
- Spina bifida aperta: arch defect communicates with a meningocele or myelomeningocele
- Exposed neural tissue risks infection and death; early closure reduces infection and limits neural damage
- Myelomeningocele repair is a surgical emergency: operate in the first 24 h of life
- First of many operations (urological, orthopaedic); real risk of latex allergy

### Tethered cord
- Thick filum terminale anchors the conus at or below L2
- Traction on the cord (especially with flexion and extension) causes neurological signs
- Cutting the filum usually halts progression
- Uncorrected meningoceles can tether the cord via sacral roots, causing orthopaedic and urological problems

### Diastematomyelia
- Cord split in two by a fibrocartilaginous or bony septum from the back of the vertebral body
- Neural tube fusion disorder with persisting mesoderm from the primitive neurenteric canal
- Mostly lumbar; linked with vertebral anomalies (fusion defects, hemivertebra, hypoplasia, kyphoscoliosis, spina bifida, myelomeningocele)
- Signs from cord movement in flexion and extension; may coexist with tethered cord
- Usually found by preschool age
- Symptomatic: excise the spur and free adhesions

### Syringomyelia
- Fluid cavity in the cord; communicating with CSF pathways or isolated
- Central canal normally closes after birth; ventricles end at the obex in the fourth ventricle floor
- In spina bifida with Chiari malformation the canal often stays open and enlarged: symptomatic hydrosyringomyelia

### Chiari malformation
- Cerebellar vermis displaced through the foramen magnum, elongated brainstem and fourth ventricle, non-communicating hydrocephalus
- Most children with myelomeningocele have Chiari II and develop hydrocephalus, usually in the first month
- Needs a VP shunt or ventriculostomy

## Other spinal conditions
### Primary spinal cord tumours
- A fifth of childhood CNS tumours
- Intramedullary or extramedullary (intradural or epidural)
  - Intramedullary: mostly low-grade astrocytoma, ependymoma
  - Extramedullary intradural: usually benign, neural crest origin (neurofibroma, ganglioneuroma, meningioma)
  - Extramedullary epidural: usually metastases (neuroblastoma, sarcoma, lymphoma)
- Many fully resectable; benign extramedullary tumours have a good prognosis

### Acute spinal cord injury
- Indirect trauma (hyperflexion, hyperextension, vertical compression); fracture dislocation or epidural bleeding can compress the cord
- Causes: traumatic breech delivery, abuse (shaken baby), car and diving accidents, playground falls, underlying vertebral anomalies (e.g. Down syndrome)
- Surgery only after full secondary survey and imaging
- About half have a normal spine X-ray
- Fracture dislocation: traction and immobilisation; fusion for unstable injury
- Laminectomy and cord exploration only for worsening signs with CT or MRI evidence of epidural or intraspinal bleeding

### Spinal vascular disease
- Rare; can cause compression, vascular steal and ischaemic cord injury
- Maintain spinal cord perfusion pressure (SCPP) and avoid compression

## Spinal cord monitoring
- Spinal cord injury risk 0.3-0.6%; mostly ischaemic, with anterior spinal artery motor pathways most at risk
- Monitoring aims to reduce neurological injury

### Wake-up test
- Lighten anaesthesia enough to test leg movement
- Main advantage: tests anterior cord function
- Only a single time point, not continuous; deficit may be irreversible; a positive test means removing all implants
- Movement on emergence risks accidental extubation, lost IV access, dislodged fixation, air embolism on deep breaths

### Somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs)
- Stimulate a peripheral nerve (usually posterior tibial); record from epidural electrodes (spinal) or scalp (cortical)
- Monitor dorsal sensory pathways only, not the vulnerable anterior motor tracts
  - Injury sparing sensory tracts is missed; SSEPs plus MEPs give global cord monitoring
- Amplitude fall of more than 50% usually significant
- Neuromuscular block reduces muscle artefact
- False negatives occur (cord injury despite preserved SSEPs)

### Motor evoked potentials (MEPs)
- Stimulate motor cortex or cord through skin, directly on exposed tissue, or on roots (e.g. tethered cord release)
- Record as compound muscle action potential (CMAP) or efferent compound nerve action potential (CNAP)
- Test motor cortex and descending motor tracts (anterior cord)
- CNAP recording allows neuromuscular blockers
- Root irritation or cord concussion by surgery can also generate CMAPs

### Dermatomal responses
- Pudendal nerve responses are especially useful, particularly in spina bifida

### Anaesthesia for reliable monitoring
- Volatiles and propofol depress SSEPs and MEPs dose-dependently
- Ketamine and etomidate may enhance signals
- Nitrous oxide strongly depresses SSEPs and MEPs unless epidural recording used
- Relaxants reduce noise and improve SSEPs; profound block abolishes CMAPs but not epidural MEPs
- Bite blocks with CMAP monitoring (prevents tube biting and tongue laceration)
- Typical MEP anaesthetic: sevoflurane (0.4-0.7 MAC) plus high-dose remifentanil, no nitrous oxide or relaxant; propofol infusion can replace volatile

## Anaesthesia for paediatric spine surgery
### Preoperative assessment
- Document neurological deficit fully
- Focus on respiratory and cardiovascular systems (affected by or associated with the spinal condition)
- Order appropriate investigations for this major surgery
- Expect blood loss: arrange blood and products

### Intraoperative management
- GA with intubation and ventilation is usual
  - Consider fibreoptic intubation for expected difficult airway or unstable spinal injury
  - Meningocele and myelomeningocele: intubate lateral, or supine on a padded ring to protect the neural plaque
- Facilitate spinal cord monitoring
- Usually prone:
  - Secure tube and lines before and after turning
  - Armoured tube reduces kinking
  - Protect eyes and pressure points
  - Complications: accidental extubation; tangled cables and dislodged lines; abdominal compression (worse ventilation, more epidural plexus bleeding, lower cardiac output); eye injury (corneal abrasion, conjunctival and periorbital oedema of the dependent eye, postoperative visual loss from ischaemic optic neuropathy); poor head and neck position causing venous and lymphatic obstruction
- Maintain spinal cord perfusion pressure:
  - SCPP = MAP minus extrinsic pressure on the cord (tumour, haematoma, venous congestion, raised intraspinal fluid pressure)
  - Control blood pressure; remifentanil increasingly used
  - Avoid venous congestion: keep the abdomen free
- Major blood loss possible:
  - Invasive BP where appropriate
  - Good IV access; more than one large cannula if major loss expected; +/- CVP
  - Heavy loss with spinal cord tumours, AVMs and scoliosis surgery
  - Blood conservation where suitable
  - Urinary catheter
  - Frequent acid-base, Hb, haematocrit and coagulation checks in some
- Air embolism monitoring in some cases, especially scoliosis
- Antibiotic prophylaxis agreed with surgeons

### Postoperative care
- HDU or ICU where appropriate
- Postoperative analgesia

## Scoliosis surgery
### Surgical approaches
- Posterior (commonest):
  - Prone; spinous processes and interspinous ligaments removed, facet joints destroyed
  - Pedicle screws or laminar hooks with two contoured rods correct the curve in all planes
  - Small children or weak bone (most neuromuscular curves): Luque rods with sublaminar wires
  - Bone graft over the whole fusion
  - Instrumentation allows early mobilisation before fusion
- Combined anterior and posterior:
  - More severe or rigid curves, or to stop anterior growth
  - Anterior part: large thoracoabdominal incision, disc removal; one-lung ventilation sometimes needed, especially high thoracic curves
  - Staged over 1-2 weeks or done together
    - Single operation: faster recovery, shorter stay
    - Staged: less morbidity and mortality in high-risk patients
    - High-risk non-idiopathic patients often get posterior surgery only (anterior approach adds morbidity)
- Anterior alone: some congenital curves

### Complications
- Major neurological injury, blood loss, coagulopathy, venous air embolism, postoperative visual loss

### Anaesthetic goals
- Manage respiratory and neuromuscular disease
- Anticipate and minimise blood loss
- Allow neuromonitoring
- Good analgesia for early mobilisation

### Preoperative assessment
- Often severe restrictive lung disease and other anomalies; focus on cardiorespiratory function (exercise tolerance, cough, frequency and severity of chest infections)
- Idiopathic scoliosis:
  - Valid group and crossmatch
  - FBC and coagulation screen
  - CXR
  - Spirometry routinely; usually restrictive
    - Surgery can be tolerated even with severe restriction (FVC < 32%)
  - Good exercise tolerance and no respiratory symptoms suggest adequate cardiorespiratory reserve
  - Echo if curve >100°, RVH on ECG or signs of right heart failure
  - About 25% have mitral valve prolapse; rarely significant; antibiotic cover given
- Non-idiopathic scoliosis:
  - Harder to assess: often cannot report exercise tolerance or do spirometry (e.g. wheelchair-bound severe cerebral palsy)
  - Cardiac disease may accompany the cause, e.g. cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias in Duchenne, aortic and mitral valve disease in Marfan
    - May be subclinical
    - More than 50% with Duchenne have some dilated cardiomyopathy and ejection fraction <45% by 15 yr; any reduction may limit tolerance of rapid fluid shifts
    - Preoperative ECG and cardiology review needed
    - Echo for LV function, but a normal study does not rule out significant disease
    - Pharmacological stress echo may better predict function under anaesthesia but is less available
  - Duchenne patients on steroids need perioperative cover
- Explain invasive lines, catheters and the analgesia plan to the child and family

### Intraoperative management
- Consider armoured ETT for posterior fusion
- TIVA with propofol and remifentanil for evoked potential monitoring
- Lines:
  - Large-bore IV access
  - Central line for combined procedures or if inotropes may be needed
  - Arterial line for monitoring and sampling
  - Urinary catheter
- Temperature: warming mattress, forced air, warmed fluids, core temperature monitoring
- Prone positioning for posterior fusion (teamwork, meticulous care):
  - Arms no more than 90° abduction or forward flexion
  - Avoid pressure on axilla, ulnar nerves at the elbow, lateral cutaneous nerves of the thigh
  - Abdomen free to move (ventilation; avoids raised venous pressure and bleeding)
  - Eyes protected and free of pressure
- Anterior approach:
  - Lateral position for thoracotomy
  - Double-lumen tube or bronchial blocker may be needed in older children
- Blood loss can exceed circulating volume (large raw decorticated bone surface, rich venous plexus); risk factors:
  - Many levels or extensive surgery
  - Long surgery
  - Neuromuscular disease (longer, complex surgery; osteopenic bone bleeds more)
  - Hypothermia
- Intraoperative blood gases and point-of-care Hb; consider point-of-care lactate
- Reducing allogeneic transfusion:
  - Screen and treat anaemia preoperatively
    - Ferritin testing (common in Australian practice): ferritin <100 mcg/L, even without anaemia, gets preoperative iron, mainly to prevent postoperative anaemia
  - Antifibrinolytics: tranexamic acid or aprotinin
  - Controlled hypotension
  - Avoid coagulopathy and hypothermia; keep the abdomen free
  - Cell salvage
  - Acute normovolaemic haemodilution (ANH)
    - Possible alternative: hypervolaemic haemodilution, giving colloid before surgery without removing blood; small RCTs and modelling show similar transfusion savings to ANH, but may work less well with losses >40% of blood volume
  - Surgical: meticulous haemostasis, topical haemostatic agents
- Analgesia:
  - Long-acting opioid to take over from remifentanil
  - Paracetamol, NSAIDs
  - Dexmedetomidine infusion as a supplement

### Postoperative care
- Good analgesia allows frequent physiotherapy and early mobilisation
  - Typical: IV morphine infusion 20-60 mcg/kg/hour with regular paracetamol, NSAID and antiemetic
  - Or PCA with 20 mcg/kg morphine bolus if the child can use it
  - Ketamine for constant pain or excess morphine sedation
  - +/- intrathecal morphine 5 mcg/kg (max 300 mcg), given by the anaesthetist after induction or by the surgeon
    - If not possible: caudal morphine 50 mcg/kg (max 3 mg)
  - +/- posterior fusion: surgeon-placed epidural catheter under direct vision, tunnelled from the wound, for local anaesthetic infusion
  - Anterior correction: paravertebral catheter under the closed parietal pleura
- Careful fluids: hourly urine output, volume assessment, replace ongoing losses
- After anterior surgery:
  - Respiratory complications (e.g. atelectasis) common
  - Chest drain needed for the resulting pneumothorax and haemothorax
  - ICU for IPPV in some, especially poor preoperative lung function or both stages on the same day

## Flashcards
- Q: Risk of spinal cord injury in spine surgery, and the most vulnerable pathway?
  - A: 0.3-0.6%; anterior spinal artery motor pathways
- Q: What fall in SSEP amplitude is significant?
  - A: More than 50%
- Q: Typical anaesthetic for MEP monitoring?
  - A: Sevoflurane 0.4-0.7 MAC (or propofol) plus high-dose remifentanil, no nitrous oxide or relaxant
- Q: Intrathecal morphine dose for scoliosis surgery in children?
  - A: 5 mcg/kg, max 300 mcg (caudal alternative 50 mcg/kg, max 3 mg)
- Q: Curve size above which severe respiratory compromise and echo are indicated?
  - A: >100°
