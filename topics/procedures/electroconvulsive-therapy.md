# Electroconvulsive Therapy

Brief anaesthesia at a remote site for an induced seizure: fast on and off, minimal anticonvulsant effect, control the autonomic surge.

## Key points
- Remote site
- Seizure threshold
- Physiological effects of ECT

## Workflow
- Agree the propofol and suxamethonium doses and timing with psychiatry
- Preoxygenate well
- Give propofol, wait a little, then suxamethonium; no need to wait for the eyes to close
- Bag gently while suxamethonium takes effect
- After fasciculation, insert the bite block
- Hand over to psychiatry for the stimulus
- Do not touch the patient during ECT
- After the seizure, remove the bite block and bag until breathing returns

## Indications and background
- Mainly severe or drug-resistant depression; also mania and catatonia
- Produces a generalised tonic-clonic seizure
- First described in 1938; mechanism still unknown

## Delivery of ECT
- Current passed through the scalp via two electrodes, bilateral or unilateral
  - Bilateral: more common; chosen when fast recovery matters most
  - Unilateral: over the non-dominant hemisphere; fewer cognitive effects
- Aim: generalised seizure with typical EEG changes
- Ideal seizure length uncertain
  - Under 10 s or over 120 s may reduce efficacy
  - Some evidence suggests delivered charge matters more than seizure length
- Usually 2 - 3 times a week, course of six to twelve treatments
  - Daily ECT possible but causes more confusion
- Maintenance ECT not usually recommended; occasionally used for drug-resistant patients after careful review

## Cerebral effects
- Cerebral oxygen consumption, blood flow and ICP increase
  - Transient ischaemic deficits and intracranial haemorrhage reported
  - Blood-brain barrier permeability increases
- Prolonged seizures or status epilepticus
- Cognitive effects are the most common
  - Headache, confusion and short-lived memory loss straight after treatment
  - Short-term memory problems for several weeks in more than 50%
  - Retrograde and anterograde amnesia both possible
  - Usually resolves within 6 months; permanent loss possible
  - Worse with existing dementia
  - Intelligence and judgement are not affected
  - Reduced by changing stimulus intensity or waveform, unilateral placement, longer gaps between treatments
  - Evidence on neuronal death and its relevance is conflicting
- Epilepsy: ECT can be given, but anticonvulsants raise the seizure threshold, so more energy is needed
- ECT is itself anticonvulsant (has treated status epilepticus); threshold rises over a course

## Cardiovascular effects
- Driven by autonomic activation
- First a parasympathetic discharge for 10–15 s: bradycardia, hypotension, even asystole
- Then a larger, longer sympathetic surge, peaking at 3-5 minutes
  - Systolic BP may rise 30-40%; heart rate may rise 20%
  - Arrhythmias occasionally
- Myocardial oxygen demand rises (rate-pressure product)
  - Greater with bilateral ECT, older patients and hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia
  - Seizure raises whole-body oxygen use, which can cut myocardial supply
  - Cardiac rupture reported
- Risk of arrhythmia, ischaemia and infarction, especially with existing heart disease
- Left ventricular systolic and diastolic function can be impaired for up to 6 h

## Other effects
- Intraocular pressure increases
- Intragastric pressure increases; nausea and anorexia
- Dental damage and mouth lacerations
- Myalgia from the seizure or suxamethonium
- Fractures and dislocations were common with unmodified ECT, now rare; WHO is moving to ban ECT without anaesthesia and paralysis
- Some patients develop intense fear of treatment; support and information help

## Morbidity and mortality
- Low risk, despite frequent use in elderly patients with comorbidity
- Mortality about 1 per 10 000 patients (1 per 80 000 treatments), similar to anaesthesia for minor surgery
- Rare serious complications: cardiovascular compromise, postictal agitation, prolonged seizures or status epilepticus
- Cardiac problems are the main medical complication; arrhythmias by far the most common

## Contraindications (none absolute)
- Raised ICP, stroke within 3 months, untreated cerebral aneurysm
- Myocardial infarction within 3 months, myocardial ischaemia, uncontrolled heart failure, severe valve disease
- Phaeochromocytoma
- Retinal detachment or glaucoma
- Unstable major fracture or cervical spine injury
- DVT until anticoagulated
- Cochlear implants (though unilateral ECT has been used successfully)

## Special cases
- Cochlear or other brain implants: technically difficult; discuss with neurology; unilateral ECT has worked with cochlear implants
- Cardiac implantable devices: turn off ICD defibrillation and anti-tachycardia functions before ECT, and back on straight after
- Pregnancy
  - Maternal risk: aspiration
  - Fetal risks: decelerations, preterm labour, abruption
  - Multidisciplinary plan with obstetrics and neonatology
  - RSI with ETT for aspiration risk
  - Thorough preoxygenation; left lateral tilt
  - Tocolysis if preterm labour is a risk, such as terbutaline or magnesium
  - Consider sevoflurane
- Cerebral aneurysm (contraindication)
  - Higher wall stress can cause enlargement or rupture
  - Cerebral blood flow velocity rises less with propofol than thiopental
  - Nitroprusside and beta-blockers partly blunt the rise

## Main anaesthetic considerations
- Often at remote sites: resuscitation equipment and drugs, trained help and recovery must be at hand
- Patients often young, but may be elderly with comorbidity
- Cardiovascular and CNS responses are the most dangerous effects
- Aim: rapid onset and offset of unconsciousness and muscle relaxation for the stimulus and seizure
- Anaesthetic choice affects efficacy: many agents are anticonvulsant, so balance adequate depth against seizure quality

## Preoperative assessment
- Look for heart failure, severe valve disease, arrhythmia, uncontrolled hypertension, poor teeth, dehydration needing fluids
- Many are elderly with comorbidity; severe depression can make history unreliable
- ASA III or higher: discuss before agreeing to ECT at a remote site (transfer risks, critical care back-up)
- Weigh risks of untreated depression against ECT and anaesthesia
  - ECT works faster than other treatments and can be life-saving in catatonia, for example with no oral intake
  - Emergency ECT usually starts within days, not hours
- Psychiatric drug issues
  - SSRIs: SIADH
  - Lithium: nephrogenic diabetes insipidus
  - Tricyclics or MAOIs with indirect sympathomimetics: hypertensive crisis
  - SSRIs with pethidine (meperidine) or tramadol: serotonin syndrome
  - Few interactions in practice, as these anaesthetic drugs are rarely needed for ECT
- Avoid sedative premedication: slows emergence and hinders the seizure
- Escort patients with suicide risk

## Consent and mental health law
- Illness may impair capacity, so assess capacity first
  - Capacity: understand, retain, believe and weigh the information to reach a choice
  - A patient with capacity cannot be given ECT without consent
- Informal patients lacking capacity and not resisting: incapacity legislation may apply
- Detained patients: some still have capacity; if resisting or unable to consent, a second opinion confirms ECT is only to save life or prevent serious deterioration or suffering
- Emergency treatment may proceed without consent or formal second opinion; an informal second opinion is good practice
- An advance directive refusing ECT is normally overridden only in an emergency

## Induction agents
- Dose first by weight, then adjusted to previous response and changing seizure threshold
- Use the same agent throughout a course, as the threshold usually rises over the course
- Systematic review: all current agents are suitable; small differences in recovery should not decide the choice
- Methohexital 0.5–1.5 mg/kg (alone or with an opioid)
  - Seizure quality: gold standard; minimal anticonvulsant effect
  - Long track record; now less available and less familiar
- Propofol 0.75–2.5 mg/kg
  - Shortens seizures more than thiopental; shortest duration, but mean difference only 9 s
  - Better cardiovascular stability, less PONV, faster emergence
  - Pain on injection
- Thiopental 2–5 mg/kg
  - Shortens seizures, less than propofol
  - Haemodynamic response less blunted; slower recovery
- Etomidate 0.15–0.3 mg/kg
  - Lengthens seizures; may lower threshold
  - Can produce seizures in resistant cases where other agents fail
  - Bigger haemodynamic response than propofol; more PONV; slower emergence
- Ketamine 0.7–2.8 mg/kg
  - Seizure effect unclear (shorter in some studies, longer in others); traditionally avoided
  - Useful in resistant cases; has its own antidepressant effect
  - Emergence phenomena, less cardiovascular stability, raised ICP
- Sevoflurane 6–8% inspired for induction, then 1–2 MAC
  - Seizures similar to thiopental; shorter than methohexital
  - Useful with difficult venous access; relaxes the uterus in pregnancy
  - Needs extra equipment; slower
- Opioids (not with etomidate) lengthen seizures by sparing induction agent and blunt the haemodynamic response
  - Remifentanil 1 mcg/kg over 30–60 s
  - Alfentanil 10–25 mcg/kg

## Neuromuscular blockers
- Reduce convulsive movement and the risk of serious injury
- Complete paralysis not needed; seizure tracked by visible movement and, better, EEG
- Suxamethonium 0.5 mg/kg most common (fast on and off)
  - Up to 1.5 mg/kg in severe cachexia, osteoporosis or existing skeletal injury
  - If contraindicated, use a non-depolarising agent
- Mivacurium: 0.08 mg/kg gives poor modification; use at least 0.15 mg/kg
- Rocuronium and vecuronium were used mainly for precurarisation; full use is possible now that sugammadex exists

## Adjuncts
- Used to spare induction agent or, more often, to control cardiovascular responses in high-risk patients
- Parasympathetic effects: atropine or glycopyrrolate
  - Glycopyrrolate: better antisialagogue, no CNS effects, less post-ECT tachycardia
  - Routine atropine premedication not advised: raises myocardial work and oxygen demand
- Sympathetic effects: beta-blockers before (atenolol) or during (labetalol, esmolol)
  - Disputed whether labetalol and esmolol shorten seizures
  - Calcium channel blockers control BP: sublingual nifedipine, IV nicardipine (reflex tachycardia), diltiazem (may shorten seizures)
  - Alpha-2 agonists such as dexmedetomidine beforehand, or GTN, blunt the surge; consider GTN with high ischaemic risk

## Airway management
- Preoxygenate
- Intubation not routine unless reflux risk, such as pregnancy
- Gentle face mask assistance after induction
  - Hyperventilation lowers the seizure threshold and can lengthen the seizure
- Bite block protects teeth, lips and tongue
- Gentle hand ventilation during and after the seizure until breathing returns
- Early sessions: stimulus may be titrated up, needing extra induction boluses

## Postoperative management
- Standard monitoring; oxygen until saturation is adequate on air
- Most recover fast and go home by standard criteria
- Common: confusion, agitation, violent behaviour, amnesia, headache, myalgia, nausea and vomiting
- Emergence agitation can be hardest to manage: calm quiet recovery first, then small doses of midazolam
- Cardiovascular complications can still occur

## Flashcards
- Q: What seizure durations may reduce ECT efficacy?
  - A: Under 10 s or over 120 s
- Q: Describe the autonomic response to ECT.
  - A: Parasympathetic for 10–15 s (bradycardia, asystole), then sympathetic peaking at 3-5 minutes (SBP up 30-40%, HR up 20%)
- Q: What is the propofol and methohexital dose for ECT?
  - A: Propofol 0.75–2.5 mg/kg; methohexital 0.5–1.5 mg/kg
- Q: What suxamethonium dose is usual for ECT?
  - A: 0.5 mg/kg, up to 1.5 mg/kg in cachexia, osteoporosis or skeletal injury
- Q: What is the mortality of ECT?
  - A: About 1 per 10 000 patients (1 per 80 000 treatments)
