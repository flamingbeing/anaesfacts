# Tracheo-oesophageal Fistula and Oesophageal Atresia

Neonatal anomaly where the key anaesthetic risk is ventilating the stomach through the fistula; keep spontaneous breathing or low pressures until it is tied.

## Epidemiology
- 92% of neonates with oesophageal atresia (EA) have a TOF
- 1:3,000 births

## Gross classification
- Gross A (8-10%): pure EA, no fistula
  - Often a long gap between upper and lower oesophagus
  - CXR: nasogastric tube coiled in the upper pouch, no gastric air bubble
- Gross B (1%): EA with a proximal TOF
- Gross C (85%, most common): EA with a distal oesophageal fistula into the trachea, usually about 1 cm above the carina
  - Blind upper pouch; lower oesophagus joins the trachea near the carina
  - Orogastric tube will not pass; CXR shows it coiled in the upper pouch; gastric air bubble present
- Gross D (1%): EA with double TOF
- Gross E (2-4%): H-type TOF without EA; often diagnosed late and with difficulty

## Prognosis
- Depends on weight and associated cardiac problems

## Clinical features
- Usually diagnosed antenatally now
- Classic presentation within hours of birth: frothy secretions, as the baby cannot swallow
- Late diagnosis: the 3Cs (choking, coughing, cyanosis), especially with feeds
- Ongoing aspiration risk, pneumonitis, recurrent chest infections
- Abdominal distension from gas entering the stomach through the fistula; can lead to gastric rupture and pneumoperitoneum
- Confirmed when a nasogastric tube will not pass and CXR shows it coiled in the blind upper pouch; gastric bubble absent in isolated EA
- After repair many have later reflux, oesophageal stricture or tracheomalacia

## Associated conditions (30-50%)
- Prematurity
- Cardiac anomalies
- Other gastrointestinal anomalies
- Renal and genitourinary anomalies
- VATER: vertebral, anal, tracheo-oesophageal, radial or renal
- VACTERL: vertebral, anorectal, cardiovascular, tracheo-oesophageal, renal, limb
- Tracheomalacia and other tracheal anomalies

## Initial stabilisation
- Avoid positive pressure ventilation; preoperative intubation is rarely needed
- IV fluids containing glucose
- Nurse head up
- Replogle tube on continuous low-pressure suction
- Haemodynamic monitoring
- Early repair gives a better outcome but it is rarely a surgical emergency

## Surgery
- A fistula must be closed soon after birth
- Usually right thoracotomy in the lateral position (thoracoscopic is possible): ligate the fistula, join the oesophageal ends
- Bronchoscopy may be done first to locate the fistula
- Primary repair: small gap, ends sewn together at fistula closure
- Long-gap EA: oesophageal repair delayed, gastrostomy placed when the fistula is closed
- After repair: oesophageal dysmotility and strictures needing contrast studies and balloon dilatation

## Preoperative assessment
- Radio-opaque nasogastric tube and CXR
- Echocardiography for cardiac anomalies (cardiology referral)
- Bronchoscopy (respiratory referral)
- Renal ultrasound
- Limb anomalies may make venous and arterial access difficult
- Routine bloods (especially electrolytes), group and crossmatch

## Intraoperative management
- Aspirate the upper pouch via the Replogle; leave in or remove as the surgeon prefers, to prevent regurgitation
- Invasive arterial line: needed for metabolic monitoring and to detect surgical compression of great vessels

### Airway management
- Main concern: gastric distension from ventilation through the fistula
- Keep spontaneous ventilation until the fistula is ligated, or give gentle low-pressure mask ventilation
- Induction options
  - Inhalational induction, intubation under deep volatile with spontaneous breathing (avoids mask IPPV)
  - IV or inhalational induction, then neuromuscular block and intubation
- Tube position
  - Ideally the ETT tip lies beyond the fistula; if the fistula is above the carina, a cuffed tube at the carina can seal it off
  - Fistulas often sit at or near the carina, risking bronchial intubation, fistula ventilation or the tube slipping above it
  - Options
    - Intubate a bronchus on purpose, then withdraw until both lungs ventilate, and secure there (tip expected beyond the fistula)
    - Fibreoptic-guided placement into the left bronchus for one-lung ventilation, ruling out passage into the fistula
    - Fogarty catheter occlusion for large distal fistulas

### Maintenance
- Right thoracotomy
- Rarely a needle gastrostomy is needed to decompress the stomach before the fistula is tied

## Postoperative management
- Usually back to ICU for a period of IPPV
- Avoid neck extension: keep neutral or slightly flexed to reduce anastomotic tension

## Complications
- Anastomotic leak: 10-20%
- Oesophageal stricture needing dilatation: 20-40%
- Stridor and tracheomalacia: 20%
- Reflux: antacids first, fundoplication later if needed
- Aspiration, atelectasis, recurrent pneumonia and respiratory infections

## Flashcards
- Q: Most common Gross type of TOF and its anatomy?
  - A: Gross C (85%): EA with distal fistula into the trachea about 1 cm above the carina
- Q: Main airway concern at induction for TOF repair?
  - A: Gastric distension from ventilating through the fistula; keep spontaneous breathing or gentle low-pressure ventilation until it is ligated
- Q: How common are associated anomalies with TOF?
  - A: 30-50% (prematurity, cardiac, VACTERL)
- Q: Rate of anastomotic leak and stricture after repair?
  - A: Leak 10-20%; stricture needing dilatation 20-40%
