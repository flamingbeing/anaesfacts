# Laryngotracheal Airway Surgery

Anaesthesia for surgery on the larynx and trachea (polyps, cancer, masses, foreign bodies), including tubed and tubeless ventilation options.

## Key considerations

- Surgery to diagnose and treat airway disease (vocal cord polyp, oral cancer, laryngeal mass) is core ENT work
- Difficult airway more common; awake intubation sometimes needed
- Airway fire risk with diathermy or laser
- Postoperative swelling, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury or bleeding can compromise the airway
  - High-dose dexamethasone
  - Postoperative intubation and ventilation may be needed
- Little pain afterwards, but intense stimulation during surgery (suspension laryngoscope, laryngeal manipulation)
- Head and neck surgery: shared airway, limited access, surgical disruption

## Ventilation strategies

- Tubed or tubeless: usually decided by what the surgeon needs

### Tubed techniques (tracheal tube with controlled ventilation)

- Advantages
  - Most secure airway
  - Reliable IPPV
  - Accurate ETCO2, so ventilation can be judged
  - Accurate end-tidal volatile measurement
  - Cuff protects against aspiration of blood, debris and gastric contents
- Disadvantage: blocks the surgeon's view, especially of posterior cord lesions
- Tube options
  - Standard ETT: may obstruct the glottis
  - Microlaryngoscopy tube (MLT)
    - Better (though still limited) access to the cords
    - Long narrow tube, so high resistance: use slow, long inspiration
    - Peak inspiratory pressure reads high, but pressure in the airway beyond the tube is lower
    - Can be damaged or ignited by laser
  - Laser-resistant tube: protects against damage and ignition in laser surgery
  - Reinforced tube: less kinking, e.g. during suspension
- Intubation approach
  - Standard: videolaryngoscopy (VL)
  - Difficult airway: awake fibreoptic intubation (alternative: awake VL); safest if the airway is judged difficult

### Tubeless (open) techniques

- General
  - Best surgical view; needed for some procedures, e.g. rigid bronchoscopy
  - Still, motionless field
  - Less secure airway; may need rapid intubation if oxygenation fails
  - ETCO2 and FiO2 cannot be measured reliably; volatiles cannot be used
  - Airway unprotected; aspiration risk
- Spontaneous ventilation
  - Oxygen through the scope side-port
  - Anaesthetist assists ventilation by hand during apnoea or desaturation
  - Cord function can be assessed
  - Keeps negative-pressure breathing, which helps hold an obstructed airway open
  - Needs higher FiO2, so fire risk
  - May not oxygenate or ventilate enough in obesity or long cases
  - Hard to balance depth against respiratory drive
    - Hypoventilation and hypercapnia common
    - Cannot paralyse or go deep; movement or coughing can injure the trachea
- Apnoeic oxygenation
  - Oxygen mass flow, e.g. high-flow nasal oxygen (THRIVE)
  - As for spontaneous ventilation, but paralysis is possible, so no movement or coughing
  - Hypercapnia: strictly time-limited by rising respiratory acidosis
- Apnoea with intermittent IPPV
  - Surgeon removes and replaces the ETT through the operating laryngoscope, giving a still, clear field between breaths
  - Paralysis possible
  - Ventilation breaks can interrupt surgery
- Side-port IPPV through a rigid bronchoscope
- Jet ventilation
  - Paralysis possible
  - Adequate oxygenation and ventilation for long procedures
  - Barotrauma risk (pneumothorax, subcutaneous emphysema)
  - Equipment intensive

### Other options

- Laryngeal mask airway: rarely used for laryngeal surgery; possible for flexible-scope procedures such as laryngeal or subglottic biopsy
- Conventional intubation then tracheostomy

## Specific procedures

- Direct laryngoscopy, microlaryngoscopy and panendoscopy: see microlaryngeal surgery
- Rigid bronchoscopy: tubeless technique needed

## Airway foreign body

- Inhaled foreign body
  - Usually young children or adults with learning difficulties
  - Often not fasted
  - Removed by rigid bronchoscopy
  - Positive pressure can cause a ball-valve effect (gas trapping beyond the object) or push the object further down
  - Inhalational induction with spontaneous breathing is often kept until the object is out
- Swallowed foreign body
  - No cricoid pressure

## Flashcards

- Q: Why use slow, long inspiration through a microlaryngoscopy tube?
  - A: The long narrow tube has high resistance
- Q: Two risks of positive pressure ventilation with an inhaled foreign body?
  - A: Ball-valve gas trapping beyond the object, and pushing the object further down
- Q: Main limit of apnoeic oxygenation for tubeless airway surgery?
  - A: Hypercapnia; time-limited by rising respiratory acidosis
- Q: Main risk of jet ventilation?
  - A: Barotrauma (pneumothorax, subcutaneous emphysema)
