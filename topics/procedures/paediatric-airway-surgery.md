# Paediatric Airway Surgery

Microlaryngoscopy, bronchoscopy and choanal atresia repair in children: shared obstructed airways, dense topical anaesthesia and often spontaneous ventilation.

## General
- Dexamethasone 250 mcg/kg IV may reduce postoperative airway oedema
- Ventilation strategies include spontaneous ventilation (see laryngotracheal surgery)

## Microlaryngeal surgery and microlaryngobronchoscopy (MLB)
### Background
- For laryngeal papillomatosis and congenital or acquired laryngeal lesions (e.g. laryngomalacia, laryngeal web)
- Done with a suspension laryngoscope and operating microscope
- Often repeated procedures; may have marked airway obstruction
- Many have a tracheostomy
- Principles as for bronchoscopy
- Common technique: spontaneous ventilation with volatile in oxygen plus topical anaesthesia
- Often laser surgery: laser precautions apply
- Camera and video screen let the anaesthetist watch the airway and field

### Preoperative assessment
- Many have obstructive symptoms (especially recurrent papillomatosis) and may obstruct completely after induction

### Anaesthetic technique
- With tracheostomy: standard technique, induction as the child prefers; connect tracheostomy to the circuit; spontaneous ventilation or IPPV
- Without tracheostomy: avoid sedative premedication
- Inhalational induction with sevoflurane or halothane in 100% oxygen
- Spray larynx with lidocaine (up to 4 mg/kg) for dense topical anaesthesia
- Maintenance options:
  - Intubation and IPPV with a narrow ETT
    - Larynx view still impaired, so other methods often preferred
  - Spontaneous breathing of volatile in oxygen via an ETT (nasal or via the laryngoscope side channel), tip in the oropharynx above the laryngeal inlet to keep the field clear
  - Apnoeic insufflation: child paralysed; volatile and oxygen insufflated via an ETT placed as above
    - Arterial CO2 rises during apnoea; long cases need pauses in surgery for IPPV
    - Theatre pollution with volatile
  - Propofol infusion to supplement anaesthesia, prevent awareness and cut pollution
  - Jet ventilation with TIVA in a paralysed child; risk of barotrauma

### Postoperative care
- As for bronchoscopy (below)

## Bronchoscopy
### Indications
- Diagnostic:
  - Recurrent pneumonia
  - Tracheo-oesophageal fistula
  - Airway obstruction
  - Laryngomalacia
  - Haemoptysis
  - Biopsy or brushings
  - Failure to wean from ventilator
- Therapeutic:
  - Foreign body removal
  - Mucus plugs (e.g. cystic fibrosis)
  - Lobar collapse
  - Refractory atelectasis
  - Balloon dilatation
  - Stent insertion
  - Laser treatment
- Most children have some respiratory compromise
  - Stridor, wheeze, hoarseness, recurrent infection, cyanosis, persistent cough, dyspnoea, tachypnoea

### Equipment
- Rigid: Storz ventilating bronchoscope is the most used in children
  - Metal tube with a removable optical telescope
  - Side holes ventilate the opposite lung when the tip sits in a bronchus
  - Side arm has a 15 mm connector for a T-piece circuit
  - Gas flows in the space between the bronchoscope lumen and the telescope
  - In small scopes the telescope fills most of the lumen and impedes ventilation in infants
  - With telescope in, the system is closed so controlled ventilation is possible, but the breathing cross-section is smaller
  - Oversized scope compresses tracheal mucosa: postoperative oedema and stridor
- Venturi bronchoscopes: common in adults; in children only if >40 kg (barotrauma risk)
  - Jet insufflation of oxygen with entrained air via a Sanders injector
  - Maintenance with IV agents
  - CO2 retention more of a problem
- Fibreoptic: wider field of view than rigid; small diameter reaches distal airways
  - Smallest in general use: external diameter 1.8 mm distally, 2.2 mm proximally

### Preoperative assessment
- Previous anaesthetic charts: laryngoscopy grade, obstruction, ETT size, any difficulty
- History and examination of airway and breathing: how symptoms change with position, crying, feeding
- Targeted tests: CXR for suspected foreign body, CT for lower airway obstruction

### Anaesthetic technique: rigid bronchoscopy
- No sedative premedication if airway obstruction or respiratory compromise
- IV anticholinergic at induction (antisialogogue, prevents bradycardia from airway handling):
  - Atropine 10-20 mcg/kg or glycopyrronium 5-10 mcg/kg
- Keep spontaneous ventilation with possible obstruction and in diagnostic cases needing functional airway assessment
- Inhalational induction with sevoflurane or halothane in 100% oxygen
  - Sevoflurane: faster induction but more ventilatory depression than halothane
- Gentle CPAP helps overcome upper airway obstruction
- Dense topical anaesthesia of the larynx is essential (prevents coughing, breath holding, laryngospasm)
  - Once deep enough, spray lidocaine (up to 4 mg/kg) on epiglottis, larynx and between the cords
- Supine with a roll under the scapulae to extend the neck and bring the trachea forward
- Maintenance: volatile in 100% oxygen via T-piece on the bronchoscope side port, or TIVA
  - Propofol +/- remifentanil suppresses airway reflexes, allows fast emergence and less theatre pollution
- Telescope in place reduces cross-sectional area and raises work of breathing (a big problem in infants with narrow scopes)
  - May need to remove the telescope intermittently for uninterrupted ventilation
- Assisted ventilation: use a long expiratory time to avoid air trapping
- Dexamethasone

### Anaesthetic technique: fibreoptic bronchoscopy
- Broadly as for rigid
- Airway choice depends on the indication:
  - Possible laryngomalacia: facemask (angle piece adapted for the scope) with spontaneous breathing to see the larynx during normal breathing; an LMA distorts the larynx and may limit cord movement
  - Trachea: can be examined through an LMA
  - Bronchial tree or bronchoalveolar lavage: ETT can be used
- With facemask or LMA: keep spontaneous ventilation; CPAP helps obstruction
  - Endoscopist may look with and without CPAP (e.g. tracheomalacia)
- A suitably sized LMA takes a larger scope than an ETT
- With spontaneous ventilation, give local anaesthetic through the scope injection port before passing the larynx

### Postoperative care
- Oral paracetamol +/- an NSAID is usually enough
- IV fluids and antibiotics as needed
- Nil by mouth for 2 hours after laryngeal topical anaesthesia
- Postoperative stridor: further steroid and nebulised adrenaline
- SpO2 monitoring for at least 2 hours (hypoventilation-related hypoxia)
- Check temperature: fever from bacteraemia after the procedure

## Choanal atresia correction
### Background
- Congenital anterior skull base anomaly closing one or both nasal passages; membranous or bony
- Incidence 1:7000 live births; unilateral to bilateral 2:1; more common in girls; up to 50% have other congenital anomalies
- Most neonates breathe through the nose for the first few months
  - In quiet inspiration the tongue is drawn up against the palate, blocking the mouth; crying relieves it
- Bilateral: neonatal respiratory distress
  - Suspect if a fine suction catheter cannot pass through the nostrils to the pharynx; CT usually confirms
- Unilateral: may go unnoticed for years, then one-sided nasal discharge
- Immediate care: oropharyngeal airway or McGovern nipple (teat with a hole that holds the mouth open)
  - Orogastric tube for feeding
  - Ongoing distress: oral intubation until surgery
- Surgery usually within the first week

### Preoperative assessment
- Bilateral atresia may be part of CHARGE syndrome (coloboma, heart defects, choanal atresia, retarded growth, genitourinary and ear anomalies); look for other anomalies

### Anaesthetic technique
- Usually arrives with IV access, oropharyngeal airway and orogastric tube
- Atropine or glycopyrronium if wanted
- Aspirate and remove the orogastric tube
- Inhalational induction with the oral airway in place
- Confirm facemask IPPV, then paralyse and intubate orally, usually with a south-facing preformed RAE tube
- Already intubated: check with the surgeon the tube suits surgery; may need changing to a south-facing RAE
- IPPV; volatile in oxygen and air or nitrous oxide
- Endonasal or oral transpalatal approach; choanae opened and stents placed from nostrils to nasopharynx
  - Stents stay 6-12 weeks so the choanae heal open
  - Restenosis 25-30% in bilateral atresia
- Analgesia: morphine 25-50 mcg/kg IV or fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg IV; IV paracetamol 15 mg/kg helps as it has no airway or respiratory effects

### Postoperative care
- Most extubated and return to the neonatal ICU
- Maintenance fluids until feeding established; orogastric tube if slow to feed orally
- Analgesia: paracetamol 15 mg/kg plus oral opioid (e.g. morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly), oral or via orogastric tube
- Stents need suction every few hours (especially before feeds, when the baby breathes through them); parents are taught to do this at home

  - A: Up to 4 mg/kg
- Q: Dexamethasone dose to reduce postoperative airway oedema in children?
  - A: 250 mcg/kg IV
- Q: Above what weight can Venturi bronchoscopes be used in children?
  - A: >40 kg (barotrauma risk)
- Q: How long nil by mouth after laryngeal topical anaesthesia?
  - A: 2 hours
