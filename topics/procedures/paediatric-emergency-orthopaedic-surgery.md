# Paediatric emergency orthopaedic surgery

Mostly healthy children with forearm, supracondylar or tibial fractures; main risk is aspiration (trauma and opioids delay gastric emptying regardless of fasting time); limb blocks usually avoided where nerve function must be monitored.

## Overview
- Large share of non-elective paediatric work; many cases wait for the next daytime list, some need out-of-hours surgery
- Most children are healthy except those with pathological fractures
- Main safety issue: regurgitation and aspiration
  - Trauma plus opioids means risk persists whatever the time since last oral intake
- Children often distressed; parents worried and shocked
- Common: forearm, supracondylar humerus and distal tibia fractures
- Femoral fractures and septic arthritis: small but significant workload
- Pathological fractures: classically osteogenesis imperfecta; also fibrous dysplasia, Ollier's disease, severe cerebral palsy, metabolic bone disease from renal or hepatic disease

## Forearm fractures
- Commonest non-elective paediatric orthopaedic case and one of the commonest indications overall
- 70% of children's fractures involve the upper limb; 50% the forearm and hand
- Mostly children over 5 years, falls in play or sport
- Greenstick fractures in younger children; complete fractures in older children
- Heal fast with remodelling and little overgrowth, so most are treated by cast or MUA and cast
- Some need percutaneous Kirschner wires; a few need open reduction and plating

### Preoperative assessment
- Often deferred until fasted and done on the next trauma list as elective
- A technically fasted child who is pale, frightened, in pain or given opioids still has a full stomach: treat as a regurgitation risk
- Some fractures need urgent surgery whatever the fasting status
- Discuss fracture type and planned surgery with the orthopaedic team (affects technique)
- Note recent analgesia
- Regional instead of GA (e.g. brachial plexus block) is poorly tolerated by children and not usually considered

### Anaesthetic technique
- Fasted: IV or gas induction
- Regurgitation risk: RSI
- Airway depends on surgery:
  - MUA: LMA with spontaneous ventilation
  - Longer cases or young children: ETT with or without IPPV
- Laryngospasm may occur during fracture manipulation: prevent by deepening anaesthesia or a short-acting opioid bolus (fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg IV or alfentanil 10-20 mcg/kg IV)
- Tourniquet used for ORIF: minimal blood loss
- Opioid needed for operations; consider even for simple MUA
  - Surgeon first increases the deformity before reducing it, so a pain-free child in a back slab may wake in pain
  - Morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV, fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV
  - If opioid given shortly before surgery, titrate to comfort in recovery instead
  - Add NSAID or paracetamol and an antiemetic
- Brachial plexus block usually avoided: numbness and motor block confuse postoperative neurological checks
- Surgeon can infiltrate LA at the ORIF incision

### Postoperative care
- Early eating and drinking
- Paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly, ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly, oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly
- After MUA: usually same-day discharge if day surgery criteria are met
- After long anaesthetics or complex out-of-hours surgery: may stay overnight

## Supracondylar fracture
- Commonest elbow fracture in children: about 75% of fractures there
- Mechanism: hyperextension (95%) or hyperflexion (5%)
- Implies significant force: look for other injuries
- Management: closed reduction and cast, closed reduction and K-wires (CRPP), or commonly ORIF
- Significant neurological and vascular complications:
  - Nerve injury: most often anterior interosseous branch of the median nerve, then radial and ulnar
  - Iatrogenic injury, especially ulnar nerve, during reduction
  - Most fracture-related nerve lesions are neuropraxias recovering over 6-18 months
  - Distal ischaemia (weak radial pulse, slow capillary refill) from brachial artery impingement is fairly common; usually resolves with reduction
  - Rarely arterial laceration needing exploration and repair

### Preoperative assessment
- Reduced urgently because of nerve and vessel risk
- Very painful: ideally IV cannula and IV morphine in the emergency department
- Significant force: treat as a regurgitation risk; exclude other injuries

### Anaesthetic technique
- RSI with preoxygenation and cricoid pressure by a trained assistant
- Usually 1-1.5 hrs, may be longer
- Usually supine; occasionally lateral for a posterior approach
- Analgesia:
  - If IV morphine given just before surgery, may withhold and assess in recovery
  - Otherwise morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV
  - Paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV or 40 mg/kg rectal plus NSAID (opioid sparing)
  - Do not withhold analgesia for fear of masking compartment syndrome; if concerned, insert a pressure monitor at operation
- Upper limb blocks not usually done: delay reduction and confuse distal neurological assessment
- Tourniquet used; blood loss usually minor
- LA infiltration of the wound
- Surgeon dresses the wound and applies a triangular bandage

### Postoperative care
- If lateral, fracture side up
- IV maintenance fluids until drinking
- Paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly, ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly, oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly
- Not discharged the same day: arm elevated in a sling to reduce swelling; monitor distal nerve and circulation

## Femoral shaft fractures
- 1.6% of paediatric fractures in the developed world; male to female 2.3:1
- Bimodal: 4-7 years and teenagers
- Young children: weak bone breaks with little force; older children: needs high-velocity trauma, so resuscitation and other injuries may come first
- Treatment by age:
  - Under 4 years: traction or hip spica
  - Older: traction, external fixation (compound fractures), intramedullary nailing, TENS (titanium elastic nails) or plating
- Severe pain: treat on arrival and to allow traction
  - Morphine 200 mcg/kg IV, then 50 mcg IV boluses every 5-10 min until comfortable
  - May add femoral nerve block, usually done blind without a nerve stimulator and may fail, so IV morphine comes first
- Ongoing analgesia for 7-14 days: paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly, ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly, oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly
- Anaesthesia may be needed for splint fitting, traction adjustment, or manipulation with or without ORIF

### Preoperative assessment
- Urgent case: other injuries, volume status, fasting, analgesia given
- More often a planned procedure a day or two later to adjust traction: fasted, treated as elective

### Anaesthetic technique
- Urgent cases: full stomach even if fasted; RSI and intubation
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
- Extra morphine if several hours since the last dose, or titrate morphine 50 mcg/kg IV boluses in recovery
- Central blocks (epidural, caudal) hard to position for: most leave the child supine and do a femoral nerve block (with nerve stimulator) or fascia iliaca block
- Planned cases: IV or gas induction; LMA may suit; anterior blocks (femoral or fascia iliaca) preferred over central blocks in traction

### Postoperative care
- Pain falls markedly once splinted or fixed
- After surgery: IV morphine infusion or PCA for 24 hours, then paracetamol, NSAID and oral opioid

## Septic arthritis
- Pyogenic joint infection; indication for urgent surgery
- Incidence 5-10/10000 children per year
- Over half are under 2 years
- Commonly hip, knee, shoulder, elbow; single joint in over 90%
- Usually haematogenous; also direct (trauma, surgery) or local spread
- Neonates and infants: hip most common; older children: knee
- May follow a febrile illness
- Presentation subtle and non-specific; may look septicaemic
  - Abnormal posture, pain on passive movement, reduced spontaneous movement
- Differentials: osteomyelitis, acute arthritis, post-traumatic effusion, Perthes' disease, cellulitis
- Anaesthetic involvement may begin with venous access in a septic infant
- Common organisms: Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Streptococcus pyogenes
- Treatment: urgent drainage of pus and aggressive antibiotics
  - Pus rapidly damages cartilage; delay risks long-term deformity and disability
  - Arthrotomy and generous washout usually preferred to aspiration; arthroscopy for the knee

### Preoperative assessment
- Some anaesthetise as soon as possible regardless of fasting, to protect the joint
- Can wait for fasting, but septic infants often have poor intake already
- Older children may be systemically well; infants may be febrile, tachycardic and dehydrated, often with respiratory symptoms
- IV access may already be in place for antibiotics; preoperative IV fluids may be needed
- Note recent paracetamol or NSAID (cannot be repeated intraoperatively)

### Anaesthetic technique
- IV or gas induction as appropriate
- Well older child: LMA, spontaneous ventilation, volatile in oxygen with nitrous oxide or air
- Toxic or unwell infants and children: intubation and IPPV
- Short procedure but large volumes of irrigation fluid soak drapes: monitor temperature (infants cool quickly)
  - Warming mattress in place; may not need switching on if febrile
- Minimal blood loss; crystalloid for fasting deficit and maintenance
- Fever increases insensible losses: increase maintenance by 10% per °C rise
- Likely had paracetamol and NSAID preoperatively; fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg IV intraoperatively

### Postoperative care
- IV maintenance until drinking
- Paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly; add oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly if needed
- Leave secure venous access for IV antibiotics; infants may need central access

## Nerve and tendon repairs
- Classically hand injuries from broken glass

### Preoperative assessment
- Usually healthy children; wait until technically fasted
- If opioids given in the emergency department, often treated as a regurgitation risk

### Anaesthetic technique
- RSI if regurgitation is a concern
- IPPV may be better than spontaneous breathing for long cases and young children
- Long surgery: pressure area care, temperature monitoring, maintenance fluids
- Tourniquet; minimal blood loss
- Opioid: morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV; surgeon infiltrates LA
- Nerve blocks relatively contraindicated: hinder postoperative neurological assessment

### Postoperative care
- Priority: recovery of function; frequent motor and neurological checks
- IV maintenance until drinking
- Paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly, ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly, oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly

## Replantation after amputation
- Traumatic amputations are rare in children
- Regional block can stop the child thrashing postoperatively

## Flashcards
- Q: Why is a fasted child with a fracture still treated as a full stomach?
  - A: Trauma, pain and opioids delay gastric emptying regardless of fasting time
- Q: Opioid bolus to prevent laryngospasm during fracture manipulation?
  - A: Fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg IV or alfentanil 10-20 mcg/kg IV
- Q: Which nerve is most often injured in supracondylar fracture?
  - A: Anterior interosseous branch of the median nerve
- Q: Why are upper limb blocks usually avoided for supracondylar fractures?
  - A: They delay reduction and confuse postoperative neurological assessment
- Q: How much should maintenance fluid increase per °C of fever?
  - A: 10% per °C
