# General Anaesthesia

Core perioperative topics around general anaesthesia: risk grading, ERAS, TIVA, oxygenation, blood management, airway, positioning, recovery problems and consent.

## ASA physical status

- ASA 1: normal healthy patient
  - Healthy, non-smoker, little or no alcohol
  - Mortality 0.06-0.08%
- ASA 2: mild systemic disease
  - No substantive functional limitation
  - Examples: current smoker, social drinker, BMI 30-40, pregnancy, well-controlled DM or hypertension, mild lung disease
  - Mortality 0.4%
- ASA 3: severe systemic disease
  - Substantive functional limitation
  - Examples: poorly controlled DM, hypertension or COPD, BMI >40, active hepatitis, alcohol dependence or abuse, cardiac implantable electronic device, moderately reduced EF, end-stage renal failure on dialysis, MI, stroke or TIA more than 3 months ago
  - Mortality 2-5%
- ASA 4: severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life
  - Examples: MI, stroke or TIA within 3 months (<3/12), severe valve disease or severely reduced EF, shock, sepsis, DIC, end-stage renal disease not on dialysis
  - Mortality 8-23%
- ASA 5: moribund, not expected to survive without the operation
  - Examples: ruptured thoracic or abdominal aortic aneurysm, massive trauma, intracranial haemorrhage with mass effect, ischaemic bowel with major cardiac disease or multi-organ dysfunction
  - Mortality 10-50%
- ASA 6: brain-dead organ donor
- Suffix "E" marks an emergency operation

### Usefulness for risk assessment

- Purpose: assess and communicate preanaesthetic comorbidity; mainly a record-keeping tool
- Not designed to predict perioperative risk, but helps when combined with surgery type, frailty and deconditioning
- Postoperative complications track ASA class and emergency surgery
  - ASA 3-4 predicts blood loss, duration of postoperative ventilation, ICU stay, complications and mortality
- ASA 3 or above predicts more blood loss and transfusion in total abdominal hysterectomy, and more delirium after hip fracture in the elderly
- Strong predictor of resource use and mortality; ASA 3 patients have higher mortality after elective hip or knee replacement

## Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS)

- Multimodal, multidisciplinary, evidence-based pathway covering the whole perioperative journey

### Aims

- Standardise and optimise perioperative care
- Blunt the surgical stress response (metabolic, endocrine, inflammatory) and limit protein breakdown
- Shorten hospital stay and speed return to normal life
- Reduce complications
- Good pain control so patients mobilise and avoid immobility complications
- Better gut function

### Benefits

- Fewer complications (smaller stress response, fewer transfusions)
- Shorter stay and earlier return to function
- Less hospital resource use
- Higher patient satisfaction

### Problems ERAS addresses

- Stress response: inflammatory (cytokines), neuroendocrine (cortisol, ADH), metabolic (insulin resistance driven by IL-6)
- Fluids, pain, blood management, thromboembolism, ileus, orthostatic intolerance, cognition, psychological factors

### Interventions: overall

- Multidisciplinary planning across surgery, anaesthesia and recovery
- Standard order sets as part of the pathway

### Interventions: preoperative

- Education and counselling
  - Agree milestones and discharge criteria in advance: eating and drinking, lower gut function back, pain controlled on oral analgesia, mobile, self-caring, no complications, support at home, willing to go home
  - Stoma education, siting and advice on avoiding dehydration
- Assess and optimise chronic disease and medicines
  - Diabetes management
  - Treat anaemia with oral or IV iron and/or erythropoietic agents; aim Hb >12 before surgery
- Prehabilitation for patients with multiple comorbidities or deconditioning, to build functional capacity
  - Physiotherapy, nutrition, respiratory work, stop smoking and alcohol, psychological support
- Before surgery
  - Avoid long fasts; clear fluids up to 2 hours
  - Carbohydrate loading
    - Clear fluids until 2 hours before GA are safe and improve wellbeing
    - 100 g carbohydrate (maltodextrin, slowly metabolised): 800 ml the night before and 400 ml 2 hours before surgery
    - Puts the patient in a fed state with full glycogen stores; reduces insulin resistance, hyperglycaemia and protein loss, improves muscle function, reduces complications and stay
    - Not for diabetics; avoid with reflux risk
    - Iso-osmolar so gastric emptying is not slowed
    - Added glutamine may help immune function
  - No sedative premedication
  - No bowel preparation (dehydration, harder fluid management)

### Interventions: intraoperative

- Antibiotic prophylaxis 30 min before incision, as part of a surgical site infection bundle
- Thromboprophylaxis
- Goal-directed fluids (e.g. oesophageal Doppler)
  - Avoid fluid overload; excess maintenance fluid harms organs, increases morbidity and lengthens stay
  - Use balanced, chloride-restricted crystalloid for maintenance
  - Goal-directed therapy (cardiac output monitor, SVV >10-15%, PVV >10-15%, SPV) in high-risk patients and major colorectal surgery with large losses
    - Based on the Frank-Starling curve in patients without heart disease
    - Linked to shorter stay, fewer ICU admissions, fewer gut, kidney and cardiovascular complications
    - Still unknown: best approach in laparoscopy, best fluid type, best monitor and targets, duration, bolus versus infusion, best markers (lactate, ScvO2)
  - Accept intraoperative oliguria (aim >0.3 ml/kg/h)
- Lung-protective ventilation and optimal oxygenation
- Normothermia (hypothermia causes shivering, vasoconstriction, immunosuppression, arrhythmias, coagulopathy)
- Normoglycaemia
- PONV prophylaxis with IV dexamethasone and ondansetron so patients eat and do physiotherapy early
- Multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia with acute pain team input
  - Spinal anaesthesia where not contraindicated: less blood loss and a 50% fall in 30-day complications
  - Periarticular infiltration without PCA (bupivacaine with adrenaline, ketorolac, morphine, triamcinolone, vancomycin if not contraindicated): as effective as PCA or femoral block in an RCT, without opioid side effects or femoral-block falls
  - Paracetamol, etoricoxib, gabapentin; careful opioid use
- Blood management: preoperative optimisation; tranexamic acid, hypotensive anaesthesia and cell salvage during surgery
- Surgical
  - Minimally invasive approaches to cut pain and stay
  - Careful haemostasis
  - No routine nasogastric tubes or drains; drains do not reduce 30-day complications and hinder physiotherapy

### Interventions: postoperative

- Remove urinary catheter and lines early
- Early feeding (less muscle loss, shorter stay, possibly fewer infections)
  - Normal diet straight after elective colorectal surgery; feeding within 24 h speeds gut recovery and reduces stay, complications and mortality
  - Chewing gum (sugar-free, >10 min, 3-4 times a day) is safe, slightly speeds gut recovery, may shorten stay
  - Alvimopan recommended to speed recovery after open colorectal surgery
- Stop IV fluids soon after leaving recovery
- Early mobilisation with physiotherapy
- Opioid-sparing analgesia; remove epidural on day 2-3
- Good glucose control
- Follow-up after discharge

### Principles and setting up a programme

- Principles: fast-track surgery, better outcomes, multidisciplinary, multimodal across pre-, intra- and postoperative phases
- Setting up: multidisciplinary team including administrators and quality staff; train staff in the pathway; a rollout timeline; ongoing interactive audit

## Total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA)

### Indications and advantages

- Volatile agents absolutely contraindicated (history of malignant hyperthermia) or relatively contraindicated (high PONV risk)
- Neuromonitoring (e.g. spine surgery): propofol plus opioid affects evoked potentials less than volatile agents or nitrous oxide
  - MEPs very sensitive to volatiles; SSEPs moderately affected
  - Propofol still reduces EEG amplitude and frequency with dose, giving burst suppression and electrical silence at high doses
- Propofol: fast onset and offset; antiemetic, anticonvulsant, bronchodilator, antipruritic; usable in renal and hepatic failure; anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and immunomodulatory

### Six procedures where propofol TIVA helps

- Spine surgery with neuromonitoring
- Posterior fossa surgery with neuromonitoring
- Rigid bronchoscopy (volatile delivery unreliable)
- Cancer surgery, e.g. mastectomy
- Neurosurgery, to reduce ICP
- High PONV risk surgery (e.g. hysterectomy, middle ear)

### Disadvantages

- Awareness
  - Blood and effect-site levels cannot be measured in real time, unlike end-tidal volatile
  - Raw or processed EEG can guide dosing but may not reliably exclude awareness
  - A 2017 meta-analysis found processed EEG (e.g. BIS) guided TCI propofol kept target depth better than manual control on standard monitoring
- Dose-dependent loss of airway reflexes and respiratory depression, as with volatiles
- Dose-dependent vasodilation and myocardial depression causing hypotension
- IV delivery failure: a tucked or draped arm can hide extravasation (compartment syndrome) or disconnection (awareness)
- Pump errors (wrong drug, concentration, rate or dose) causing underdosing and awareness, or overdosing when soft limits are overridden
- Cost: usually dearer than volatile; needs TCI pumps and BIS

### How TCI keeps a steady blood concentration

- Based on a three-compartment model
- V1 (central): plasma plus tissues that behave like plasma for that drug; loading bolus = target concentration x V1 (Cp x V1)
- Drug then moves down its gradient into vessel-rich (V2) then vessel-poor (V3) tissue, so two exponentially falling infusion rates replace these losses
- At equilibrium the only loss is metabolism and excretion, so a constant final rate matches elimination
- Compartment sizes and rate constants depend on the drug and model, and on age, sex, height and weight entered into the pump
- Summary: bolus, then three superimposed infusions (two decaying, one constant)

### Effect-site targeting

- Aims to remove hysteresis between plasma level and brain effect
- Effect-site volume is negligible, so its rate constant (keo) runs one way, plasma to effect site
- keo is found by experiment: peak effect occurs where the falling plasma curve after a bolus meets the rising effect-site curve; the trial keo whose effect-site curve matches plasma at the observed peak effect is taken as the real keo

### TCI versus manual infusion

- Bolus and rate set by a computer model of drug and patient, not guesswork
- Reaches steady state faster; a fixed-rate infusion takes 5 half-lives
- Target changes are reached faster using pauses and boluses
- Less work than repeated manual rate changes
- Correct dosing should reduce awareness and cardiovascular and respiratory side effects
- Faster wake-up because overdosing is avoided

### Infusion devices

- Non-electrical: elastomeric pumps, manual clamps (e.g. roller clamp)
- Electrical: syringe pumps, volumetric pumps
- General pump features: mains and rechargeable battery with low-power alarm; clear display and buttons; clamp to mount near the patient; secure syringe fit; accurate in the short and long term; fine rate steps over a wide range; accurate boluses; purge; high-pressure occlusion alarm with minimal post-occlusion bolus; syringe misplacement alarm; end-of-infusion alarm
- TCI-specific: built-in algorithms; entry of age, weight, height and sex; display of drug, model, plasma and effect-site concentrations; specified compatible syringes

### Making sure the drug gets in

- Organisation: standard pumps across the unit, regular servicing, kept on charge, all staff trained, anaesthetists trained before using TIVA alone
- Before use: right drug correctly prepared and double-checked by a second person; line primed (no air, less backlash); syringe intact and seated (no siphoning); pump self-test; correct model and patient data; dedicated line or correctly oriented anti-reflux valve
- During use: cannula visible at all times; pump at patient level (avoids under-delivery or siphoning); pump on mains power; check volume delivered at intervals; act on alarms

## Oxygenation and preoxygenation

### What sets the speed of desaturation in apnoea

- Oxygen reservoir
  - FRC size: smaller when supine, larger head-up; reduced by anaesthesia, in children, and by encroachment (obesity, pregnancy, ascites, bowel obstruction, kyphoscoliosis)
  - Oxygen content: FiO2 and PACO2 before apnoea (alveolar gas equation PAO2 = FiO2 - PACO2/RQ); shunt reduces the benefit of preoxygenation; high PACO2 lowers PAO2
- Oxygen consumption
  - Physiological: pregnancy, neonates, infants and children
  - Pathological: thyrotoxicosis, sepsis, critical illness
  - Drug: suxamethonium fasciculations
- Airway patency
  - At rest, oxygen leaves the lung at about 250 ml/min and CO2 enters at about 200 ml/min (RQ 0.8)
  - In apnoea oxygen uptake continues but CO2 entry falls as PACO2 rises, so lung volume shrinks
  - With a patent airway, gas is drawn in by apnoeic mass flow (100% oxygen if the mask stays sealed), greatly extending safe apnoea time

### Optimising preoxygenation (including RSI)

- Explain and gain consent to improve cooperation
- Head-up to increase FRC
- Treat FRC reducers (decompress bowel obstruction with an NG tube, NIV to recruit atelectasis)
- Tight-fitting mask with 100% oxygen, tidal breathing for 3-5 minutes
- Fresh gas flow above minute ventilation, so the circuit stays at 100% and supports apnoeic mass flow
- Keep mask seal and airway patent until intubation
- Consider high-flow nasal oxygen after mask removal (apnoeic oxygenation plus positive airway pressure)
- Measure success by expired oxygen fraction (FeO2) above 0.9

### Preoxygenating a fit adult

- For: longer time to desaturation in unexpected difficult airway or severe laryngospasm
- Against: low risk anyway; adds about 5 min to induction; mask claustrophobia; absorption atelectasis and postoperative hypoxaemia

### High inspired oxygen (>80%) during maintenance

- For: safety margin against unexpected events (bronchospasm, laryngospasm, accidental extubation); may be needed in V/Q mismatch, interstitial lung disease or pulmonary oedema to prevent hypoxic harm (myocardial ischaemia, AKI, stroke)
- Against
  - Oxygen toxicity
    - CNS: twitching (perioral, hand muscles), tinnitus, vertigo, nausea, convulsions
    - Lung: tracheobronchitis, ARDS, interstitial fibrosis
    - Eye: reversible myopia and visual field narrowing, cataract
    - Red cells: abnormal shape, possibly reduced red cell mass
  - Absorption atelectasis
    - Nitrogen normally splints alveoli; with 100% oxygen alveoli collapse as oxygen is absorbed
    - Leads to shunt and hypoxaemia; hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction raises PVR and can cause right heart strain or failure and capillary leak in susceptible patients
    - Collapsed lung is prone to pneumonia; recruitment risks volutrauma and barotrauma
  - Chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure: loss of hypoxic drive leads to hypoventilation, worsening hypercapnia and obtundation
  - Premature neonates and infants: retinopathy of prematurity, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, intraventricular haemorrhage

### Oxygen saturation targets

- Supplemental oxygen after open cholecystectomy with opioid analgesia may be needed in recovery and on the ward for up to 72 hours
- Previously healthy adults: SpO2 94-98%
- COPD
  - Before ABG: Venturi 24% or 28%, or nasal cannula 1-2 L/min; target 88-92% if hypercapnia risk factors without prior respiratory acidosis
  - After ABG with normal CO2: raise FiO2, target 94-98%
  - Repeat ABG; if CO2 rises or ventilation falls on oxygen, lower the target
  - Raise Venturi flow by 50% if RR >30

### NPSA 2009 themes in oxygen-related deaths

- Equipment: empty cylinders, faulty or missing kit
- Prescribing: none or wrong
- Administration: oxygen confused with medical air, wrong flow, disconnection
- Monitoring: not monitored, low SpO2 not acted on

## Fluid status and responsiveness

### Clinical assessment of volume status

- History: thirst, low or concentrated urine, drowsiness or agitation, postural dizziness, palpitations, breathlessness
- Examination: tachycardia, hypotension, narrow pulse pressure, tachypnoea, mottling, poor skin turgor, capillary refill >3 s, sunken eyes, dry mucosa, no tears
- Tests: lactate, raised urea:creatinine ratio, hyperuricaemia, raised creatinine

### Monitoring for fluid management

- Goal-directed therapy loads fluid to maximise stroke volume, finding patients on the steep part of the Frank-Starling curve where preload increases SV; reduces complications and ICU stay
- Static measures
  - CVP and pulmonary artery occlusion pressure: poor marker of LV end-diastolic volume (non-linear compliance); only predict response at the extremes
  - TOE LV end-diastolic area: tracks LVEDV; sets an endpoint for filling but does not predict responsiveness; limited by dilated or poorly contracting ventricles
- Dynamic measures
  - Stroke volume variation: >10% suggests responsiveness
    - SVV = (SVmax - SVmin)/SVmean over the breath; reliable
    - Needs arterial line, IPPV with tidal volume 8 ml/kg, RR <35, HR <150, closed chest, sinus rhythm
    - Errors with arrhythmias, IABP, aortic regurgitation, changing SVR or intense vasoconstriction, damped line; not valid in spontaneous breathing
  - Pulse pressure variation: >13% suggests responsiveness
    - Pulse pressure = SBP - DBP; PPV (%) = (PPmax - PPmin)/PPmean
    - Correlates well; in stiff arteries (elderly) a small SV change gives a large PPV
  - Aortic peak velocity variation by pulsed-wave Doppler (aortic valve or descending aorta)
    - Assumes constant annulus diameter
    - (max peak velocity - min peak velocity)/mean peak velocity over 1 breath; >12% suggests responsiveness
  - Passive leg raise
    - Works in spontaneous breathing and arrhythmias; non-invasive, repeatable
    - Start 45 degrees semi-recumbent, lower trunk flat and raise legs to 45 degrees
    - Peak effect at 30-90 s; look for a 10% rise in SV (or pulse pressure on the arterial line)
    - Limits: positioning may be contraindicated, unreliable in severe hypovolaemia, other interventions must pause, not useful with raised intra-abdominal pressure

## Prolonged surgery

- Example: 18-hour lower limb reconstruction in a fit 30-year-old
- Monitoring: arterial line even in healthy patients (fluid shifts, ABGs, avoids nerve injury from repeated cuff inflation)
- Fluids and blood
  - Avoid hypovolaemia and high viscosity (poor flap perfusion); avoid excess fluid (oedema, flap failure, difficult closure)
  - Cardiac output monitoring (e.g. FloTrac), regular ABGs, haematocrit target 30% for flaps
- Temperature
  - Heat loss by evaporation, conduction, convection, radiation; risk of overheating with forced air and warmed fluids
  - Measure core and peripheral temperature; keep the gap <1 degree C to limit peripheral vasoconstriction
- Positioning: careful padding; move limbs and relieve pressure points every 2 hours where surgery allows
- Pharmacokinetics
  - Accumulation of drugs with large distribution (context-sensitive half-time)
  - Remifentanil (constant context-sensitive half-time); TCI propofol (three-compartment model)
  - Desflurane: low blood:gas partition coefficient, fast offset even after long cases
  - BIS or SedLine to avoid excess depth
  - Atracurium infusion guided by TOF, keeping 1-2 twitches
- Airway: long cases with fluid shifts raise risk of laryngeal oedema and tracheomalacia; right-sized ETT, regular cuff pressure checks, slight reverse Trendelenburg, loose ties so jugular drainage is not obstructed
- PONV: long volatile exposure and large opioid doses; multimodal antiemetics; propofol TIVA if high risk
- Team fatigue: more errors; plan breaks and rotate staff

## Patient blood management

### Anaemia

- Example: 57-year-old for colonic cancer resection in 3 weeks with Hb 10.1 g/dL

- Definition: red cell oxygen-carrying capacity insufficient for physiological needs; Hb <13 g/dL in men and <12 g/dL in women
- Preoperative
  - Check FBC before all major elective surgery, ideally 4-6 weeks ahead
  - Treat iron deficiency
    - Absolute: depleted stores or chronic loss
    - Functional: inflammation raises hepcidin, which blocks gut iron absorption and release of stored iron
    - Oral iron (ideally 3 months) or IV iron (ferritin peaks 1 week after infusion; Hb rises within 2-3 weeks)
  - Replace B12 and folate; consider erythropoietin with haematology once nutritional causes are treated
  - Refer as needed; delay elective surgery until optimised
  - Bleeding risk: personal history (after surgery, trauma, childbirth, menorrhagia) and family history; haematology review
  - Review antiplatelets and anticoagulants; bridge if needed
  - Minimally invasive surgery
  - Compare expected blood loss with the patient's tolerable loss; prehabilitation; individual plan with conservation options (e.g. autologous transfusion)
- Intraoperative
  - Surgery: careful technique and haemostasis, topical haemostatics
  - Anaesthesia: neuraxial block; positioning to reduce venous congestion; permissive hypotension; low CVP with restrictive fluids in liver resection; adequate depth and analgesia to avoid hypertension
  - Avoid the lethal triad; prophylactic TXA; viscoelastic-guided coagulopathy treatment
  - Normovolaemic haemodilution, cell salvage
  - Optimise oxygenation, ventilation and cardiac output; evidence-based transfusion thresholds
- Postoperative
  - Iron and folate; monitor and treat bleeding; autologous salvage; avoid the lethal triad
  - Restart antiplatelets or anticoagulants only once haemostasis is secure
  - Minimise blood sampling; treat coagulopathy
  - Maximise oxygen delivery; reduce oxygen consumption (analgesia, treat infection); evidence-based thresholds

### Jehovah's Witness adolescent for scoliosis surgery

- Consent
  - Minor, so parental consent needed
  - GA: IV induction, oral intubation, prone turn; risks include bruising, injection pain, dental trauma, drug reactions, prone injuries (pressure, nerve, postoperative visual loss), PONV
  - Arterial line after induction for monitoring and ABGs (risks: bruising, nerve injury, infection, arterial injury)
  - Surgical risks: prone, long, major blood loss; high-dependency care afterwards
  - Morphine PCA: how to use it; side effects (nausea, vomiting, drowsiness, urinary retention, constipation, itch)
  - Explore which products are acceptable (likely not whole blood, red cells, FFP, platelets or cryoprecipitate); discuss cell salvage and fractions (e.g. fibrinogen, activated factor VII)
- Reducing transfusion
  - Preoperative: treat iron, B12 and folate deficiency; haematology for erythropoietin; delay surgery until Hb optimised (target Hb >12 g/dL); bleeding and drug history; discuss staged surgery; work out maximum allowable blood loss
  - Intraoperative: careful surgery and topical haemostatics; slight head-up, low intrathoracic pressure and free abdomen to reduce venous and epidural venous pressure; adequate depth and analgesia; avoid the lethal triad and keep calcium normal
  - TXA 10 mg/kg then 1 mg/kg/h
  - ROTEM-guided fibrinogen or PCC if acceptable; cell salvage
  - Maintain oxygenation, ventilation and cardiac output

### Intraoperative cell salvage

- Patient indications: preoperative anaemia or bleeding risk; rare blood group or antibodies; refusal of donor blood (some Jehovah's Witnesses accept it if the circuit stays continuous with their circulation)
- Surgical indications
  - Any surgery with expected loss >1 L or >20% of blood volume
  - Orthopaedics: major spine, bilateral or revision arthroplasty, pelvic, major trauma
  - Cardiothoracic and vascular: complex cardiac surgery, any bypass case, trauma thoracotomy, vascular trauma, open aortic aneurysm repair
  - Paediatrics (less donor exposure): cardiac, scoliosis, major orthopaedics
  - Major obstetric haemorrhage: separate suction for amniotic fluid; leucocyte depletion filter on reinfusion to reduce amniotic fluid embolism risk
- Advantages: avoids donor blood complications (immune, allergic, infection); returned at room temperature (less hypothermia); normal 2,3-DPG and oxygen carriage; lower processing and storage costs; useful for rare groups and those refusing donor blood
- Disadvantages: equipment and training; costly disposables; no clotting factors or platelets (dilutional coagulopathy); haemolysis (fewer cells returned, free haemoglobin and AKI); air embolism, infection from poor technique, fluid overload, hyperkalaemia, hypocalcaemia

## Aspiration with a supraglottic airway

### Risk factors for airway events with a SAD

- Patient
  - Abnormal anatomy (craniofacial anomalies, oral or head and neck tumours); irritable airway (asthma, recent chest infection); OSA
  - Raised intragastric or intra-abdominal pressure: full stomach, pregnancy after 18-20 weeks, ascites, obstruction, obesity, acute abdomen, pain or high-dose opioids, gastroparesis (autonomic neuropathy from DM, GBS)
  - Weak lower oesophageal sphincter: hiatus hernia, previous gastric or oesophageal surgery, NG tube
  - Bulbar weakness: stroke, Parkinson's disease, myasthenia gravis, Lambert-Eaton syndrome, muscular and myotonic dystrophy
- Surgical: urgent case, inadequate fasting, pneumoperitoneum, steep head-down, abdominal surgery, shared airway, long surgery
- Anaesthetic: inexperience or poor supervision, poor patient selection, SAD in known difficult airway, high airway pressures, wrong size, light anaesthesia, first-generation device

### Recognising aspiration

- Bile-stained, particulate or gastric fluid in the mouth or SAD
- Coughing, laryngospasm, crackles
- Bronchospasm, wheeze, rising airway pressure, falling tidal volume
- Hypoxaemia and cyanosis; pulmonary oedema

### Management

- Crisis: tell surgeons, call for help, intubation trolley, bronchoscope, 2 large-bore Yankauer suckers
- Head-down at once; 100% oxygen high flow; check SpO2
- If material is in the mouth or SAD: suction, remove SAD, intubate at once (RSI with cricoid pressure)
  - Suction the trachea and send aspirate for pH, Gram stain and culture, ideally before positive pressure (oxygenation comes first)
- Ventilate with 100% oxygen and add PEEP
- Particulate aspiration: lavage with suction or bronchoscopy
- Cancel elective surgery not yet started, or finish quickly with the minimum safe procedure
- ICU and supportive care
  - Crystalloid rather than colloid
  - Stress ulcer prophylaxis with PPI or H2 antagonist (famotidine 20 mg, ranitidine 50 mg)
  - Intermittent pulmonary toilet; no large-volume lavage via the ETT
- Antibiotics guided by Gram stain; prophylaxis if gastric contents likely colonised (PPI or H2 antagonist use, small or large bowel obstruction)
- No steroids; bronchodilators; lung-protective ventilation (ARDS); ECMO and lung transplant in extremis

### Cricoid pressure and RSI

- Cricoid pressure pushes the complete cricoid ring back to compress the oesophagus against the vertebral body and stop passive regurgitation
- Indication: aspiration risk needing intubation (upper GI surgery, pregnancy after 18-20 weeks, full stomach)
- Contraindications: cricotracheal injury, cervical spine injury, active vomiting (oesophageal rupture), poor laryngoscopic view
- For: may reduce aspiration; no aspiration deaths in UK obstetric anaesthesia since the 1960s (though positioning, antacids and spinal anaesthesia also improved); no rise in failed intubation in elective patients
- Against
  - Never shown to reduce aspiration; often done badly
  - Uncomfortable awake (10 N awake, 30 N asleep) and may cause coughing or vomiting
  - Oesophagus lies lateral to the cricoid 90% of the time
  - Can worsen the view, obstruct the airway with too much force, block tube passage, and risks oesophageal rupture if the patient vomits

## Difficult airway

- Definition: a conventionally trained anaesthetist has difficulty with mask ventilation, tracheal intubation or both

### Predictors by task

- Difficult mask ventilation: poor seal (beard, bleeding, fractures); obesity; airway obstruction (tumour, abscess, redundant tissue); age >55 (loss of airway tone); edentulous; stiff lungs (asthma, COPD, interstitial disease)
- Difficult SAD insertion: mouth opening <2.5 cm difficult, <2 cm impossible; obesity, obstruction, distorted anatomy; stiff lungs
- Difficult front-of-neck access: previous neck surgery or radiotherapy; anterior neck mass; short or fixed flexed neck, obesity, surgical emphysema

### History

- Congenital: Down's, Pierre Robin, Treacher Collins, Klippel-Feil syndromes, goitre
- Pregnancy
- Acquired: infection (Ludwig's angina, retropharyngeal abscess); tumour; endocrine (acromegaly, obesity); rheumatological (RA, ankylosing spondylitis, scleroderma); facial fractures; cervical spondylosis; previous head and neck surgery, radiotherapy or cervical fixation
- Past anaesthetics: severe sore throat or hoarseness; known difficult or failed intubation; charted difficult mask ventilation, Cormack-Lehane grade 3-4, multiple attempts

### Examination and tests

- General: pregnancy, obesity, stridor, hoarseness
- Mouth: retro- or micrognathia, maxillary hypoplasia, beard, large tongue, high-arched palate, opening <3 fingerbreadths, stiff TMJ, poor jaw protrusion, Mallampati 3-4
- Teeth: prominent upper incisors, edentulous, poor dentition
- Neck and chest: short thick neck, neck mass, large breasts, thyromental distance <6 cm, extension <90 degrees, limited flexion
- CT neck and thorax for compression or invasion; lateral flexion-extension C-spine films for atlanto-axial instability

### Difficult airway equipment

- Routine: face masks; oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways, 3 sizes each; 2 working laryngoscope handles; Macintosh 3 and 4 blades; range of ETTs and LMAs; bougie and stylet; Magill forceps
- Difficult airway trolley
  - Algorithm chart
  - ProSeal LMA; intubating LMA sizes 3, 4 and 5 with tubes and pushers
  - Reinforced ETTs; microlaryngeal tubes size 5 and 6
  - McCoy and Miller blades; videolaryngoscopes (McGrath, C-MAC)
  - Flexible bronchoscope with portable light source; rigid fibreoptic (Bullard type)
  - Aintree catheter, Cook exchange catheter; retrograde intubation kit
  - Transtracheal jet ventilation; cannula and surgical cricothyroidotomy kits
  - Suction, CO2 detector, oesophageal detector
- Extras worth adding: fibreoptic access within 5 min including videolaryngoscopes; Combitube; non-standard direct (McCoy) and indirect (C-MAC, GlideScope) laryngoscopes; light wand; retrograde kit; rigid ventilating bronchoscopes
- Safe provision: dedicated stocked trolleys; staff know contents and location; departmental training (airway lead, training room, airway training lists)

## Extubation

### Problems after extubation

- Airway: sore throat, hoarseness; foreign material (blood, clot, secretions, throat pack, debris); laryngospasm; laryngeal oedema (long case, head-down or prone); cord trauma or palsy (traumatic or repeated attempts, long intubation, RLN injury); subglottic or tracheal stenosis; tracheomalacia (long intubation, large goitre or thymic tumour); compression from haematoma or swelling
- Respiratory: cough, bronchospasm, aspiration, sputum retention and collapse with V/Q mismatch, basal atelectasis, negative pressure pulmonary oedema, diffusion hypoxia, respiratory failure (residual drugs, inadequate reversal)
- Cardiovascular: catecholamine surge (tachycardia, hypertension), myocardial ischaemia, infarction or arrhythmias

### High-risk extubation factors

- Patient: known difficult airway; limited mouth opening or prior radiotherapy; obstruction (Down's, acromegaly, tumour, infection); C-spine disease (ankylosing spondylitis, myelopathy, trauma); large neck tumour; asthma, COPD, OSA, restrictive disease, smoking, recent URTI; IHD or unstable arrhythmia; aspiration risk (reflux, unfasted, hiatus hernia); neuromuscular or neurological disease (stroke, posterior fossa tumour, head injury, myasthenia, GBS, muscular or myotonic dystrophy)
- Surgical: airway, head and neck, C-spine or posterior fossa surgery; swelling or haematoma, double-lumen tube, high cuff pressure; long intubation; prone or steep head-down; major intraoperative events (difficult ventilation, instability needing large transfusion or vasoactive drugs, hypothermia, fluid shifts, acid-base or electrolyte disturbance)

### Strategies when difficult extubation is expected

- Follow DAS extubation guidance
- Extubation criteria
  - Good effort: adequate tidal volume, RR <30, RR/tidal volume ratio <105
  - Stable haemodynamics; full reversal (TOF ratio >0.9); normothermia
  - ABG after big fluid shifts or transfusion (Hb, acid-base, electrolytes acceptable)
- Clear the airway under direct laryngoscopy, look for laryngeal oedema, suction the trachea
- NG decompression if aspiration risk; exclude haematoma or ongoing bleeding
- Cuff leak test; consider bronchoscopy for tracheomalacia
- Best position (sitting or lateral); 100% oxygen; extubate fully awake with reflexes back
- Supplemental oxygen; extended monitoring in recovery and high dependency
- Extra techniques
  - Airway exchange catheter if reintubation would be hard
  - Smooth emergence: remifentanil for awake extubation, swap to SAD while deep, deep extubation with an oral or nasal airway
  - Extubate awake to CPAP or high-flow nasal oxygen if obstruction or apnoea risk
- Delay extubation in ICU until reversible problems are corrected (instability, acid-base or electrolyte disturbance, hypothermia, airway oedema or cord paresis)
- Tracheostomy if problems are not readily reversible, e.g. major head and neck resection with flap

## Awake fibreoptic intubation

### Sensory supply

- Nose: anterior ethmoidal nerve (front half); nasopalatine branches of the sphenopalatine ganglion (back half and nasopharynx)
- Mouth: anterior 2/3 of tongue via the lingual nerve (trigeminal)
- Oropharynx: glossopharyngeal nerve (posterior 1/3 of tongue, front of epiglottis, oropharynx, tonsils and fauces)
- Larynx: internal branch of superior laryngeal nerve above the cords; recurrent laryngeal nerve below the cords and trachea

### Agents

- Cocaine 5% or 10% solution or paste; anaesthesia plus vasoconstriction; 1.5 mg/kg; caution in coronary disease, hypertension, pseudocholinesterase deficiency
  - Moffett's solution: 2 ml 10% cocaine, 1 ml 1:1000 adrenaline, 2 ml 8.4% sodium bicarbonate, 5 ml saline
- Lidocaine 4% or 10% spray
- Vasoconstrictors (oxymetazoline, phenylephrine): less bleeding, shrunken mucosa, more room for scope and tube

### Topical techniques

- Direct spray; soaked ribbon gauze; cotton applicators
- McKenzie technique: 20G cannula joined to oxygen tubing via a 3-way tap, oxygen at 2-4 L/min, LA injected slowly from a 5 ml syringe to give a directed jet
- Mucosal atomisation device on a syringe for a fine mist
- Nebulised lidocaine: 5 ml of 4% with oxygen for up to 30 min, reaching the trachea; useful with limited mouth opening
- Spray-as-you-go: 16G epidural catheter through the scope channel, 5 ml syringe of 4% lidocaine dripped onto the cords before entering the trachea

### Nerve blocks

- Glossopharyngeal: after topical LA, retract tongue medially; 22G or 25G needle at the base of the posterior tonsillar pillar, 0.5 cm lateral to the tongue edge; 2-5 ml 2% lidocaine submucosally after aspiration; repeat other side
- Superior laryngeal: supine, neck extended; 25G needle onto the greater horn of the hyoid, walk off inferiorly and advance a few mm through the thyrohyoid membrane; 2 ml 2% lidocaine after aspiration
- Recurrent laryngeal (transtracheal): supine, neck extended; 20G or 22G needle through the cricothyroid membrane in the midline aspirating until air bubbles appear; inject 5 ml 4% lidocaine quickly; coughing spreads it

### Indications

- Limited mouth opening; abnormal anatomy or mass blocking the view of the cords; unstable C-spine; airway trauma needing inspection first; rescue intubation in prone or lateral position

### Contraindications (all relative)

- Severe acute obstruction (scope may "cork" the airway); grossly distorted anatomy; cords not seen on nasendoscopy
- Raised ICP or IOP worsened by coughing; basal skull fracture; coagulopathy (nasal bleeding); LA allergy; refusal or poor cooperation; operator inexperience

### Complications

- Airway: sore throat, hoarseness, epistaxis, cord erythema or haematoma, flu-like symptoms
- Cardiovascular: tachycardia, hypertension
- Gastric: aspiration; gastric rupture with oxygen insufflation down the channel (use high-flow nasopharyngeal oxygen instead)
- Drugs: LAST, allergy; oversedation and hypoxia

## Cricothyroidotomy

- Cricothyroid membrane: bounded above by thyroid cartilage, below by cricoid, laterally by the cricothyroid muscles
- Thyroid cartilage at C3/4 and houses the cords; cricoid at C6
- Membrane lies about 2 cm below the laryngeal prominence, just above the cricoid
- Nearby structures: vocal cords, superior thyroid and cricothyroid arteries
- Complications: failure and hypoxaemia; false passage, surgical emphysema, pneumomediastinum, pneumothorax; bleeding; infection; injury to cords, oesophagus, carotid, IJV or vagus; fistula; scarring; subglottic stenosis

## Positioning

### Prone position

- Airway: accidental extubation, airway oedema, post-extubation stridor
  - Secure tube carefully and check immediately after turning; trolley stays until tube position confirmed so the patient can be turned back and reintubated
  - Recheck after any head or body move, and if the tube is free-hanging (e.g. Mayfield pins)
  - If dislodged prone: plan A SAD (decide whether to continue); plan B SAD as conduit for scope and tube; plan C fibreoptic intubation prone
  - Neutral head, no external compression, leak test before extubation
- Respiratory: better V/Q matching (heart weight off the lungs, freer diaphragm)
- Cardiovascular: dislodged lines; reduced preload (dependent pooling, caval compression, high intrathoracic pressure from chest compression, IPPV and PEEP); venous air embolism; cardiac arrest
  - Position to keep chest and abdomen free; maintain preload
  - Prone CPR: hands over each scapula or over the thoracic spine, with or without sternal counter-pressure; open cardiac massage if chest already open
  - Defibrillation pads (AP, posterolateral, or right axilla-apex) placed before turning and checked before surgery starts
- Neurological: head rotation reduces cerebral blood flow (partial carotid or vertebral occlusion); poor venous drainage raises ICP; hypotension leads to stroke or cord injury; neck flexion or extension can compress the cord; peripheral nerve injury from compression or stretch (risk factors: extreme body size, DM); keep head neutral, pad carefully
- Musculoskeletal: direct and indirect pressure injury
- Eyes: corneal abrasion or ulcer, central retinal artery or vein occlusion, ischaemic optic neuropathy, cortical blindness
  - Optimise vascular risk (atherosclerosis, DM, hypertension), control glaucoma, no pressure on eyes, avoid hypoxia, hypotension and anaemia

### Pneumoperitoneum and steep Trendelenburg

- Airway: lungs move cephalad, ETT may go endobronchial; upper airway oedema may cause stridor afterwards
- Cardiovascular (pneumoperitoneum)
  - Brief autotransfusion from splanchnic vessels on insufflation
  - IVC compression pools blood in the legs, reducing venous return, cardiac output and BP (worse if hypovolaemic); IPPV and a raised diaphragm add to this
  - Lung compression increases PVR
  - SVR increases from aortic compression and neurohumoral factors (catecholamines, renin-angiotensin, vasopressin): limits the BP fall but raises afterload, wall stress and ischaemia risk
- Cardiovascular (head-down): less pooling in the legs, partly offsetting the fall in venous return; diaphragm pushed further up
- Respiratory: splinted diaphragm, lower compliance, atelectasis, V/Q mismatch, hypoxaemia
- CNS: higher intra-abdominal and intrathoracic pressure impair cerebral venous drainage and raise ICP, risking oedema, lower CPP and ischaemia; hypercapnia dilates cerebral vessels and raises ICP; long steep head-down laparoscopy can cause temporary neurological dysfunction on emergence
- Gut: intra-abdominal pressure >20 mmHg can cut mesenteric and mucosal flow by up to 40%, with lactic acidosis; more reflux
- Kidney: lower arterial inflow and venous outflow reduce renal blood flow, GFR and urine output, risking AKI; pressure >20 mmHg cuts GFR by about 25%
- Liver: dysfunction, lactic acidosis
- Blood: venous stasis, DVT risk
- Musculoskeletal: sliding on the table; pad pressure points, protect eyes
  - Well-leg compartment syndrome: raised legs poorly perfused plus venous compression from supports and pneumoperitoneum
  - Presents with disproportionate leg pain, rhabdomyolysis and possible myoglobin-induced renal failure; high morbidity and mortality
  - Risk factors: surgery >4 h, muscular legs, obesity, PVD, hypotension, steep head-down
  - Reduce risk: no compression stockings, move legs at intervals, heel or ankle supports rather than calf or knee supports

## Perioperative hypothermia

- Complications
  - Cardiovascular: early sympathetic drive with higher oxygen demand and output; arrhythmias; shivering increases oxygen demand
  - Respiratory: more pneumonia
  - Endocrine: less insulin and more insulin resistance, causing hyperglycaemia
  - Gut: reduced motility (poor enteral feeding); rarely pancreatitis
  - Blood: thrombocytopenia (liver and spleen sequestration), platelet and clotting factor dysfunction
  - Kidney: cold diuresis with electrolyte loss, hypovolaemia and arrhythmia risk
  - Immune dysfunction and sepsis; lactic acidosis
- Exam focus includes consequences of a recovery temperature of 34 degrees C

## Peripheral nerve injury

### Postoperative ulnar neuropathy

- Patient risk factors
  - Rheumatological disease (e.g. RA): compression neuropathy
  - Endocrine (acromegaly, DM, obesity): compression neuropathy, mononeuritis multiplex
  - Vascular risk (smoking, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, PVD): mononeuritis multiplex, peripheral neuropathy
  - Neurological or musculoskeletal (old stroke with contractures, cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy): hard positioning, compression or stretch
- Surgical: manipulation (e.g. shoulder surgery) causing stretch
- Anaesthetic: ischaemia (hypoxia, hypotension, anaemia); compression (poor padding and arm support); stretch (poor positioning)
- Prevention: identify and optimise risk; preoperative neurological examination to record deficits; continue antispasmodics; find a comfortable position while awake; plan positioning with surgeons; non-operative arm padded on an arm board; maintain perfusion; careful surgical handling

### Foot drop after knee replacement (spinal plus femoral block)

- Causes
  - Pre-existing palsy (DM causes neuropathy and mononeuritis multiplex); should have been documented before the block; acute peroneal palsy from DM alone unlikely
  - Surgical injury to the common peroneal nerve (direct, stretch or compression)
  - L4/L5 root trauma from spinal (usually done at L3/4 or L4/5); rare; epidural or subdural haematoma would cause cauda equina syndrome (bladder and bowel loss, bilateral leg weakness or numbness)
  - Femoral block does not cause weak dorsiflexion
  - Ischaemia: high-pressure or long thigh tourniquet (>2 h), especially in DM, causing neurapraxia; hypotension from spinal sympathectomy; anaemia (less likely with tourniquet, spinal and ERAS Hb optimisation)
  - Compression: tourniquet during surgery; tight dressings afterwards
  - Idiopathic
- Management
  - Review history, DM control and complications, anaesthetic chart (paraesthesia during spinal, hypotension, positioning) and operation notes (nerve injury, tourniquet time)
  - Full lower limb examination (sensory, motor, reflexes); upper limb and cranial nerves to exclude a central cause
  - Reassure and explain; discuss with surgeons; early nerve conduction studies and EMG; early neurology referral; follow-up alongside surgical review

## Postoperative delirium

- DSM-5: disturbed attention (direct, focus, sustain, shift); altered cognition (memory, orientation, language, perception) not explained by dementia; develops over a short time and fluctuates during the day
- Postoperative delirium persists or recurs after initial emergence

### Management

- Recognise early in high-risk patients; talk to the patient and exclude immediate threats (airway obstruction, respiratory failure)
- Correct causes
  - Respiratory: SpO2, oxygen to 94-98%; listen for pneumothorax, wheeze (bronchospasm, anaphylaxis), crackles (oedema, aspiration, atelectasis); ABG for hypoxia, hypercapnia, electrolytes and acid-base
  - Cardiovascular: ECG and BP; treat hypotension with fluid or vasopressors and investigate (12-lead ECG, troponin); treat arrhythmias per ACLS; keep MAP near baseline
  - Neurological: GCS, pupils, focal signs; exclude residual block (TOF ratio 0.9); assess pain (IV fentanyl boluses if pain cannot be assessed); urinary retention; withdrawal (alcohol, opiates, barbiturates)
  - Exclude hypothermia and hypoglycaemia
- Non-drug: glasses and hearing aids back, reorientation, family present, quiet area
- Drugs
  - Alpha-2 agonists: IV dexmedetomidine 1 mcg/kg over 10 min, then 0.2-0.7 mcg/kg/h; or IV clonidine 1-2 mcg/kg; watch for bradycardia and hypotension
  - IV haloperidol 0.5-1.0 mg every 10-15 min; watch QTc and extrapyramidal effects
  - Avoid benzodiazepines
- Safety: bed rails up and padded; physical restraint only as a temporary measure

## Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV)

### Pathophysiology

- Vomiting centre inputs
  - Chemoreceptor trigger zone (muscarinic, H1, D2 receptors); triggered by the gut (5-HT3) and emetogenic drugs (5-HT3, D2)
  - Vestibular apparatus (muscarinic, H1), e.g. ear surgery such as tympanoplasty
  - Gut via 5-HT3 and vagal afferents: surgical trauma, blood or toxins release substance P and serotonin from enterochromaffin cells
  - Higher centres: memory, fear, anxiety, pain, sight, smell
- Opioids and anaesthetics act on the CTZ
- Memory aid: middle, side, low, higher

### Risk factors

- Patient: female; non-smoker; previous PONV or motion sickness; preoperative anxiety or nausea; migraine; age 3-50 (in children risk rises after age 3, then falls through adulthood)
- Anaesthetic: GA rather than regional (possibly via more opioid); volatile agents (versus propofol TIVA); nitrous oxide; intra- and postoperative opioids; longer anaesthesia
- Surgical: strabismus; ENT (adenotonsillectomy, tympanoplasty); dental; abdominal (cholecystectomy, gynaecological, laparoscopic, inguinoscrotal or penile)
- Apfel score (adults): female, non-smoker, previous PONV or motion sickness, expected postoperative opioids; 0,1,2,3,4 factors give about 10,20,40,60,80% risk

### Consequences

- Poor satisfaction; delayed recovery and hospital discharge or unplanned admission (cost, poor resource use)
- Aspiration; delayed oral intake (important in diabetes); dehydration, electrolyte loss, metabolic alkalosis
- Oesophageal rupture; wound dehiscence; worse pain

### Non-drug measures

- Avoid GA (no anaesthesia or regional); avoid dehydration; acupuncture or acupressure; aromatherapy

### Antiemetic classes

- Anticholinergic (hyoscine): central muscarinic antagonist; central anticholinergic syndrome (excitement, hallucinations, odd behaviour, drowsiness, ataxia)
- Antihistamine (H1), cyclizine: mild anticholinergic (tachycardia), sedation, extrapyramidal effects
- Dopamine D2 antagonist, droperidol 0.625-1.25 mg: sedation, extrapyramidal effects (acute dystonia, akathisia), long QTc, hypotension from alpha-blockade, hyperprolactinaemia
- 5-HT3 antagonist, ondansetron: headache, flushing, long QTc, bradycardia
  - Mechanism: enterochromaffin cells release serotonin (5-HT) after drugs or mechanical stimulation; it stimulates vagal fibres ending in the CTZ and vomiting centre; the CTZ also has 5-HT3 receptors; ondansetron blocks these
- Dexamethasone: anti-inflammatory, reduces prostaglandins; hyperglycaemia, psychosis
- NK1 antagonists, aprepitant (oral) and fosaprepitant (IV): block substance P in the dorsal vagal complex of the brainstem
- Cannabinoid, nabilone: blocks CTZ input to the vomiting centre

### Plan for a high-risk patient (e.g. hysterectomy with previous severe PONV)

- Stratify risk: patient (sex, PONV history, smoking, preoperative or chemotherapy nausea); surgery (gynaecological; laparoscopic or open); anaesthetic (GA for laparoscopic, perhaps spinal for open; opioid needs)
- Reduce baseline risk: education and reassurance; minimal fasting; carbohydrate loading 2 hours before per ERAS; acupuncture referral; consider scopolamine patch; oral paracetamol premedication; experienced surgeon to shorten the case
- Propofol and remifentanil TIVA (propofol antiemetic, no volatile, no long-acting opioid)
- Opioid-sparing analgesia: paracetamol, NSAIDs if safe; bilateral TAP blocks if open, rectus sheath block or wound infiltration if laparoscopic
- Adequate hydration
- Multimodal prophylaxis: IV dexamethasone 4 mg at induction, IV ondansetron 4 mg at the end; consider cyclizine or droperidol
- Treat breakthrough PONV with a different class

## Residual neuromuscular blockade

### Assessment

- Airway obstruction, poor pharyngeal function, weakness, blunted hypoxic drive
- Tachycardia, hypertension; uncoordinated movement; cannot sustain head lift
- Fade on tetanic or train-of-four stimulation
- Maximum inspiratory force <25-30 cmH2O

### Complications

- Airway and breathing: obstruction, hypoxaemia, hypoxic brain and organ injury; weak airway reflexes and aspiration; hypoventilation with hypoxaemia and hypercapnia (agitation, drowsiness); weak cough, sputum retention, atelectasis, pneumonia
- Cardiovascular: tachycardia, hypertension, myocardial ischaemia
- Musculoskeletal: weakness and incoordination, falls
- Unpleasant awareness of weakness, PTSD

### Reducing the risk

- Find increased sensitivity
  - Neuromuscular disease: prejunctional (GBS, motor neurone disease), junctional (myasthenia gravis, Lambert-Eaton), postjunctional (myotonic and muscular dystrophy)
  - Drugs: magnesium (pre-eclampsia, severe asthma), aminoglycosides, calcium channel blockers
  - Electrolytes: high magnesium, low calcium, high potassium, metabolic acidosis
- Reduced clearance: rocuronium in liver disease, hypothermia
- Avoid relaxants where possible (remifentanil for immobility, regional techniques); correct electrolytes; stop potentiating drugs if possible
- Intraoperative: short-acting organ-independent agent (atracurium) or one with a specific reversal (rocuronium); weight-based dosing with nerve stimulator monitoring (1-2 twitches is enough for most surgery); avoid hypothermia; check K, Ca and Mg
- Reverse only once 4 twitches are present: neostigmine 50 mcg/kg with glycopyrrolate 8 mcg/kg, or sugammadex 1-2 mg/kg
- Postoperative: monitor and give more reversal if needed (neostigmine maximum 70 mcg/kg)
- Example scenario: atracurium 25 mg in a 50 kg patient, surgery finished after 25 minutes

### Routine reversal: pros and cons

- For: avoids residual block complications (obstruction, aspiration, hypoventilation, sputum retention, distressing weakness)
- Against: reversal side effects
  - Sugammadex: cost, anaphylaxis, interactions (binds progesterone and reduces contraceptive effect; flucloxacillin and fusidic acid can displace relaxant and cause recurarisation)
  - Neostigmine: muscarinic effects (bradycardia, gut stimulation that may stress anastomoses, bronchospasm, secretions)
  - Glycopyrrolate or atropine: tachycardia, dry mouth; atropine can cause central anticholinergic syndrome (confusion, drowsiness, agitation)

### Sugammadex

- Cyclodextrin (hydrophilic outside, lipophilic core) that encapsulates rocuronium and vecuronium; no effect on benzylisoquinoliniums
- Dose by depth of block: after RSI dose of rocuronium (1.2 mg/kg) give 16 mg/kg; routine reversal 1-2 mg/kg
- Advantages: can't-intubate-can't-oxygenate rescue; routine reversal; shorter-than-expected cases; faster turnover; few cardiovascular effects; usable when neostigmine should be avoided (e.g. myotonic dystrophy)

## Problems in recovery

### Agitated, breathless patient in recovery: differentials

- Airway: residual block, cord oedema, cord palsy (RLN or intubation trauma), laryngospasm, bronchospasm
- Breathing: sputum plugging and atelectasis; aspiration; pneumothorax; pulmonary oedema (negative pressure from biting the tube, fluid excess, heart failure); diffusion hypoxia
- Circulation: hypotension, ongoing bleeding, anaemia
- Neurological: stroke, intracranial haemorrhage, hypertensive encephalopathy
- Hypoglycaemia; sodium disturbance; pain, full bladder, hypothermia; drug use or withdrawal; psychiatric (PTSD)

### Delayed awakening

- Causes
  - Absolute or relative overdose: volatile or IV anaesthetics, benzodiazepines, opioids, adjuncts (ketamine, dexmedetomidine)
  - Residual neuromuscular block
  - Metabolic: hypoxaemia, hypercapnia; hypo- or hyperglycaemia (DKA, HHS); Na, K, Mg, Ca or acid-base disturbance; hepatic or uraemic encephalopathy; hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency
  - Hypotension
  - Neurological: ischaemia, infarction, haemorrhage, oedema; seizures or postictal state; CNS infection
  - Recent alcohol, prescribed or illicit drugs; hypothermia; carbon monoxide in the circuit
- Management
  - Check FiO2 and ETCO2; feel pulse, treat hypotension
  - Confirm all agents stopped; 100% oxygen at high flow to wash out volatile; check expired agent
  - Stimulate (voice, touch, gentle airway suction)
  - TOF ratio >0.9
  - Glucose, electrolytes, ABG, temperature
    - Hypoglycaemia: 50% dextrose 1 ml/kg
    - Hyperglycaemia, DKA, HHS: IV insulin 0.05-0.1 units/kg and fluid resuscitation
    - Hyponatraemia: 3% saline 3 ml/kg
    - Treat metabolic acidosis
  - Neurological examination: pupils and light reflex, corneal and gag reflexes, response to pain (sternal rub, nail bed), limb reflexes and plantars; focal signs mean urgent CT and neurology or neurosurgery input
  - Look for drug errors; urine and blood toxicology
- Example: unresponsive 45 minutes after a 2 hour VATS lobectomy; or 40 minutes after laparoscopic appendicectomy

### Dyspnoea with frothy secretions after nasal surgery

- Crisis: assess and treat together, inform surgeons, call for help, 100% oxygen by non-rebreather, SpO2
- Bleeding from the nose: watch for epistaxis and bloody sputum; sit up and reassure; oxygen; check pulse and BP, resuscitate with fluid, blood or vasopressors; back to theatre if packing or topical vasoconstrictors fail
- Secretions from neostigmine given without anticholinergic: check the chart, talk to the anaesthetist; glycopyrrolate 8 mcg/kg (0.2-0.4 mg)
- Retained throat pack: check chart; RSI and laryngoscopy if likely
- Aspiration pneumonitis: risk factors (obesity, reflux, hiatus hernia), crackles, wheeze, coughing or laryngospasm at extubation; oxygen; intubate if severe; lavage or bronchoscopy for particulate matter; ICU; antibiotics per Gram stain or colonisation risk
- Negative pressure pulmonary oedema: biting the tube without a bite block; oxygen, NIV, high-flow or intubation
- Acute heart failure or fluid overload: supportive care, NIV, high-flow or intubation; urinary catheter and fluid balance; diuretics

### Hypertension in recovery (BP 190/110 mmHg after sleeve gastrectomy)

- Check trend and exclude artefact (cuff size, other arm)
- Talk to the patient; exclude obstruction, distress, instability, altered consciousness
- Treat causes
  - Pain: IV fentanyl 25 mcg boluses to a pain score of 4 or less
  - Urinary retention: palpate, catheterise
  - PONV: rescue antiemetic of a different class
  - Residual block: TOF ratio >0.9, maximum inspiratory force >25-30 cmH2O, more reversal if needed
- Oxygenation: exclude obstruction, SpO2 target 94-98%
- Fluid overload: IV furosemide 5-10 mg
- GCS and focal signs (stroke); exclude hypo- or hyperthermia including malignant hyperthermia (tachycardia, hypertension, tachypnoea)
- Glucose, ABG, electrolytes; 50% dextrose 1 ml/kg for hypoglycaemia; treat anaemia, electrolytes and acidosis
- Exclude drug error; check usual BP control, adherence and whether morning antihypertensives were taken
- Drugs: IV labetalol 5-10 mg every 5-10 min, or IV hydralazine 5-10 mg every 20 min
- Target BP 20% of baseline

### Desaturation in recovery (64-year-old, SpO2 85% two hours after laparoscopic partial nephrectomy, 1 litre blood loss)

- Airway: obstruction from laryngeal oedema or cord palsy
- Lungs: aspiration, atelectasis, sputum plugging and collapse, negative pressure pulmonary oedema, TRALI, hypoventilation (residual block, opioids), pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, bronchospasm
- Circulation: hypovolaemia from under-resuscitation or ongoing bleeding; transfusion-associated circulatory overload; acute heart failure
- Management: 100% oxygen by non-rebreather; confirm the reading (other finger or hand, good trace); exclude obstruction; auscultate

## Postoperative pulmonary complications

- Types: atelectasis, pneumonia, aspiration, bronchospasm, worsening chronic lung disease, unplanned oxygen, NIV or ventilation, ARDS
- Patient risk factors: age >65; functional dependence; ASA 2 or more; heart failure; COPD; smoking in the past 8 weeks; current respiratory infection; preoperative hypoxaemia or hypercapnia; albumin <30 g/L; anaemia <10 g/dL
- Surgical: emergency; open thoracic; aortic; upper abdominal; head and neck; neurosurgery; surgery >3 hours
- Anaesthetic: GA (versus spinal, epidural or regional); neuromuscular blockers
- How anaesthesia contributes: aspiration; lower FRC falling below closing capacity, causing atelectasis, V/Q mismatch, hypoxaemia and pneumonia; ventilator-induced lung injury; incomplete reversal
- Prevention
  - Preoperative: stop smoking ideally 8 weeks before (shorter is not harmful); optimise asthma and COPD per guidelines; treat exacerbations and chest infections and delay elective surgery; chest physiotherapy and incentive spirometry; oral care (dental hygiene, chlorhexidine mouthwash); antacids if high aspiration risk
  - Intraoperative: keep surgery <3 hours if possible; minimally invasive if it does not greatly prolong surgery; regional over GA; opioid-sparing regional analgesia; avoid long-acting relaxants; avoid PA catheters unless needed; salbutamol MDI 2-4 puffs 30 min before intubation
  - Postoperative: deep breathing or incentive spirometry in high-risk patients; epidural or regional analgesia instead of opioids; avoid NSAIDs in asthma or COPD; consider high-flow, CPAP or NIV; no routine NG tube after abdominal surgery

## PACU discharge

- Post-anaesthesia discharge score: each item scores 2, 1 or 0
  - Consciousness: fully awake; rouses to voice; unresponsive
  - Activity: raises 1 arm on command; no purposeful movement; no movement
  - Breathing: deep breaths and free cough; dyspnoea or limited breathing; apnoea
  - SpO2: >95%; 90-95%; <90%
  - BP: within 20% of baseline; 20-50% from baseline; >50% from baseline
  - HR: 50-100; 45-49 or 101-120; <45 or >120
  - Pain score: 0-3; 4-6; 7-10
  - PONV: none; mild; severe
  - Temperature (degrees C): <36; 35-35.9; <35
- Score of 16 or more for discharge to the ward

## Accidental awareness under GA (AAGA)

- Example: 61-year-old with previous awareness during laparoscopy, now for total laparoscopic hysterectomy
- Definition: recalled consciousness during a period the patient expected to be under GA
- Types
  - Explicit: spontaneous or prompted recall, with or without pain; can cause anxiety, depression, sleep problems, nightmares, PTSD
  - Implicit: no conscious recall but later behaviour or performance may be affected
- Risk factors
  - Drugs: TIVA (extravasation, pump not started, failure, wrong programme); neuromuscular blockers (no monitoring, no reversal, wrong ampoule, unlabelled or unchecked syringes); thiopentone; RSI
  - Patient: young, female, obese (difficult airway, prolonged intubation, dosing), difficult airway, previous AAGA (possible genetic tendency), sick patients given reduced doses
  - Surgery: obstetrics, especially emergency caesarean (anxiety, no premedication, RSI, relaxant, out of hours, junior staff, incision straight after intubation); cardiac (high-dose opioid, low-dose hypnotic); thoracic (rigid bronchoscopy with interrupted volatile, single- to double-lumen tube change); neurosurgery
  - Organisation: junior anaesthetist, emergency, out of hours
- Monitoring: full machine, vaporiser and pump checks; HR, BP, RR and tidal volume if not paralysed; end-tidal agent; TIVA plasma and effect-site concentrations; nerve stimulator for TOF-guided reversal and extubation; processed EEG (e.g. BIS) or EEG (e.g. SedLine, 16-channel EEG)
- Consequences: immediate, delayed or no recall; anything from neutral feelings to severe distress and PTSD (flashbacks, anxiety, depression, effects on life and work); avoidance of healthcare or anaesthesia and loss of trust; implicit memory may cause unexplained anxiety; explanation and CBT help
- Modified Brice questionnaire
  - Last thing remembered before sleep?
  - First thing remembered on waking?
  - Anything in between?
  - Any dreams, and were they disturbing?
  - Worst thing about the operation?
  - Problems going to sleep or waking up?

## Informed consent

- Permission before a healthcare intervention, research, or disclosure of a person's information
- Capacity
  - Presume capacity unless shown otherwise
  - Must understand, retain, use and weigh information and communicate a decision
  - Patients may consent to some parts of anaesthetic care and not others
  - Age of consent in Singapore is 21 (18 for those in National Service)
  - Gillick competence for under-16s in urgent situations without parents: parental right ends once the child understands enough to grasp the proposal fully
- Best interests: if capacity is lacking, record the grounds, the treatment and why it is in the patient's best interest
- Voluntariness: free of coercion, persuasion or manipulation; patients may withhold, withdraw or later give consent
- After opioids (e.g. morphine for a fracture): incapacity may be temporary; do not assume incompetence; assess and reassess capacity, especially before non-urgent decisions

## Flashcards
- Q: What are the Apfel risk factors and the risk with 0 to 4 factors?
  - A: Female, non-smoker, previous PONV or motion sickness, postoperative opioids; 10, 20, 40, 60, 80%.
- Q: What SVV and PPV thresholds suggest fluid responsiveness?
  - A: SVV >10% and PPV >13% (with IPPV at tidal volume 8 ml/kg, sinus rhythm, closed chest).

  - A: 16 mg/kg.

  - A: Expired oxygen fraction above 0.9 after 3-5 minutes of tidal breathing on 100% oxygen.

  - A: 10 mg/kg then 1 mg/kg/h.
