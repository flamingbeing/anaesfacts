# Orthopaedic Surgery

Summary of anaesthetic considerations for orthopaedic operations: elderly patients, hidden blood loss, careful positioning and frequent use of regional anaesthesia.

## Patient factors
- Mixed population, mostly elderly
- More rheumatoid arthritis: risk of atlantoaxial instability

## Surgical factors
- Blood loss can be large
  - Much can collect in the soft tissues of the thigh or upper arm, so loss is hard to estimate
  - Cell salvage works in joint replacement; avoid if the wound is infected or cement is about to be used
- Implants: surgeons avoid in-out urinary catheterisation for implant cases
- Complications
  - Tourniquet problems
  - Cement problems
  - Fat embolism
  - DVT

## Positioning
- Positions risk nerve and musculoskeletal injury
  - Pad all pressure points
  - Avoid stretch or compression of joints and nerve plexuses (e.g. axillary roll protects the brachial plexus)
- Positioning at the start takes time; surgeons often want specific positions

## Anaesthetic technique
- Many operations suit regional anaesthesia
  - Reduces surgical-site bleeding without drug-induced hypotension, giving the dry field needed for good cement and prosthesis fixation
  - Blocks also give postoperative analgesia and reduce PONV

## Flashcards
- Q: Why is the airway a concern in orthopaedic patients with rheumatoid arthritis?
  - A: Risk of atlantoaxial instability.
- Q: When should cell salvage be avoided in joint replacement?
  - A: If the wound is infected or cement use is imminent.
- Q: Name four complications specific to orthopaedic surgery.
  - A: Tourniquet problems, cement problems, fat embolism, DVT.
- Q: Why is blood loss hard to estimate in thigh or upper arm surgery?
  - A: Large volumes can be lost into the soft tissues.
