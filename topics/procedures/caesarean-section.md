# Caesarean Section

Neuraxial is the technique of choice; GA (RSI) for the most urgent cases or when neuraxial is contraindicated. Plan by urgency category.

## Indications
- Poor progress, fetal distress, risk of maternal haemorrhage, dystocia (abnormal labour), impending maternal death

## Urgency categories (RCOG, after Lucas et al 2000; a continuum of risk)
- Category 1: immediate threat to life of mother or fetus
  - ACOG/NICE: decision-to-delivery interval (DDI) of 30 min or less
  - Of the 30 min: 5 min transfer to theatre, 5 min prep and drape, the rest for anaesthesia and delivery
- Category 2: maternal or fetal compromise that is not immediately life threatening
  - NICE: DDI <75min
- Category 3: needs early delivery, no maternal or fetal compromise
- Category 4: delivery at a time to suit mother and team
- "Emergency" = categories 1–3; "elective" = category 4
- "Crash" section = category 1

## Key considerations
- Urgency: elective vs emergency vs crash
- Higher risk of aspiration and difficult or failed intubation
  - So GA is kept for emergencies or when regional is contraindicated
  - Anticipated difficult airway: regional or awake fibreoptic intubation
- Higher risk of awareness
- Protect the fetus
- Left lateral tilt to avoid aortocaval compression

## Preoperative management
- Aspiration prophylaxis
  - Non-particulate antacid (e.g. sodium citrate), usually given on the ward
  - Consider metoclopramide and/or an H2 antagonist
- Consent for suppositories for postoperative analgesia

## Choice of technique
- Multidisciplinary: obstetricians, anaesthetist, neonatologists
- Neuraxial is most common
  - Local anaesthetic infiltration alone is possible but rarely needed
  - Long operations: catheter technique (CSE, continuous epidural, continuous spinal)
- Neuraxial vs GA
  - Neuraxial is safer and preferred
    - Avoids the three As of GA: aspiration, airway complications, awareness
    - No fetal exposure to GA drugs: baby more alert, less respiratory depression
    - Less postpartum haemorrhage
    - Better analgesia and earlier mobilisation
    - Early bonding and breastfeeding
    - Specific indication: pre-eclampsia
    - Downsides: PDPH and other neuraxial complications; must be ready to manage the airway urgently
  - GA is faster: the standard for a crash (category 1) section
    - Alternatives: epidural top-up or rapid sequence spinal
    - Also when neuraxial is contraindicated
- Neuraxial in pregnancy differs
  - Thrombocytopenia is common
  - Peritoneal traction means a T4 sensory level is ideal
    - T10 covers somatic pain; T4 is needed to block visceral pain (splanchnic nerves)
  - Less local anaesthetic needed (higher intra-abdominal pressure)
  - Lumbar flexion harder with the gravid uterus
  - Aortocaval compression worsens spinal hypotension, risking mother and fetus
  - Spinals also suit labour analgesia, manual removal of placenta, perineal repair and non-obstetric surgery in pregnancy
- Contraindications to neuraxial
  - Maternal refusal
  - Coagulopathy: INR >1.3, platelets <75
  - Local or systemic infection
  - Aortic stenosis, uncorrected hypovolaemia
  - Back disease, previous spinal instrumentation
  - Neurological deficits

## Spinal vs epidural vs CSE
- Spinal
  - For: fast onset, dense block with less sacral sparing, technically easiest
  - Against: hard to fix an inadequate block, limited duration, rapid large haemodynamic changes
- Epidural
  - For: can top up for long surgery; postoperative analgesia; gradual haemodynamic change
  - Against: slower onset; less dense, patchy or unilateral block; harder to do; large LA doses and volumes
- CSE
  - For: spinal speed and quality plus epidural top-ups and postoperative analgesia
  - Against vs spinal: technically harder
  - Against vs epidural: rapid BP and CO change; epidural catheter may be untested when topped up

## Regional anaesthesia
- Be ready to convert to GA
  - Before the spinal, give 30 mL of 0.3 M sodium citrate orally to reduce gastric acidity
- Confirm surgical block with objective testing (e.g. pin-prick) before incision

## Spinal anaesthesia
- Heavy bupivacaine 10-12mg depending on maternal size; less than a typical spinal dose
- Intrathecal lipophilic opioids help with visceral discomfort
  - Fentanyl (e.g. 10-15mcg): faster onset, denser block
  - Morphine (e.g. 100mcg): good postoperative analgesia
    - Itch, nausea, respiratory depression: follow up on the ward
- Typical mix: 2.2-2.3ml heavy bupivacaine + 10-15mcg fentanyl + 100mcg morphine

## Epidural anaesthesia
- Usually built up with intermittent boluses through an indwelling catheter
  - Lumbar epidural: 15–25 mL of LA for surgical block (0.5% bupivacaine, 0.5% ropivacaine, 1.5–2% lidocaine or 3% chloroprocaine)
  - Epidural morphine can be added for postoperative pain if monitoring is adequate; some give it at the end
  - Adrenaline reduces systemic absorption
    - A test dose of LA with adrenaline helps exclude intrathecal or intravascular placement
  - Chloroprocaine: fast onset, rapid plasma esterase breakdown, so less systemic toxicity; ideal for rapid extension in fetal distress with a catheter in place

## Epidural top-up of a labour epidural
- A symmetrical epidural that has controlled labour pain well can be extended with concentrated LA
- Check it works: review the charts; a poorly working labour epidural (missed segments, one-sided) will not give adequate surgical block
- Confirm catheter position
  - Aspirate first: look for blood or CSF
  - Give a small test dose first, e.g. 5ml; assess side, level and density every 3-5 min
    - Reference: objective block took a mean of 1.5 min after 2 ml lidocaine 1.5% intrathecally; after 3 ml lidocaine 1.5% epidurally there was no block at 1.5 min and onset took 9 min
  - Inspect the catheter (can be done in theatre)
- Where to top up
  - Delivery room with urgent transfer: allows a test dose and shortens the DDI
  - Operating theatre: better monitoring and earlier detection of high or total block, hypotension and LAST
  - Usual approach: give a test dose in the delivery room once caesarean section is decided
  - Category 1: more aggressive top-up with larger doses in the delivery room may be appropriate
  - The anaesthetist must stay with the patient once the top-up begins
- Solutions
  - Epidural mix: 20 ml lignocaine 2% + 2ml 8.4% NaHCO3 + 0.1 ml adrenaline 1:1000 (or 1ml adrenaline 1:10000)
    - Lignocaine has the fastest onset
    - Adrenaline usually to about 5mcg/ml
    - Bicarbonate alkalinises the solution, increasing unionised lidocaine and speeding onset (mean 4.5 min faster); 2 ml bicarbonate 8.4% to 20 ml lidocaine
    - Adding epidural fentanyl 50-75mcg shortens onset further
  - Ropivacaine 0.7%: fewest intraoperative top-ups needed
    - 10 ml ropivacaine 1% + 4 ml saline = 14 ml
    - 14 ml ropivacaine 1% + 6 ml saline = 20 ml
  - Bupivacaine 0.5% and levobupivacaine 0.5%: slowest onset and poorest block

## Problems with regional anaesthesia
- Haemodynamics
  - After the spinal, supine with left uterine displacement
  - Treat hypotension promptly: IV fluid, ephedrine or low-dose phenylephrine
    - A preemptive fluid bolus may lessen spinal hypotension
  - Bradycardia with hypotension not responding to first measures: give adrenaline promptly
- High spinal (above T1) can compromise breathing
- Vomiting often signals hypotension: speed up fluids, give phenylephrine
- Shivering: IV pethidine 5mg + 5mg; can make NIBP readings difficult
- Intraoperative pain
  - Warn about pressure sensations
  - Repeat regional (spinal, CSE) if surgery has not started and it is not urgent
  - Further epidural top-up
  - Entonox before delivery
  - Opioids, midazolam after delivery
    - Airway reflexes must stay intact; if more than light sedation is needed, convert to GA and secure the airway
- After delivery: epidural morphine 3mg if not already given

## General anaesthesia
- Airway
  - Parturients desaturate fast: thorough preoxygenation is essential
  - RSI for aspiration risk
    - Cricoid pressure until tube position is confirmed, applied at 90º to the tilted table
- Drugs
  - Induce only once draped and the obstetrician is scrubbed and ready, to limit fetal exposure
  - RSI most often with thiopental and suxamethonium
    - Propofol possible, but poorer neonatal profile, shorter amnesia (awareness risk) and slower return of spontaneous breathing (BJA 2009 CME)
    - Etomidate and ketamine also used
    - Opioids traditionally left out of induction (neonatal respiratory and neurological effects)
      - Alfentanil has a role in severe cardiac disease or hypertensive disorders, to blunt the intubation pressor response
  - Non-depolarising relaxant may follow induction
  - Before delivery: volatile with or without nitrous oxide
- After induction: deliver the baby quickly
  - Some give 100% oxygen before delivery in fetal distress
- After delivery
  - Uterotonics, antiemetics
  - Add opioids and nitrous oxide for depth
  - Keep volatile concentration low: high levels cause uterine atony and bleeding
  - Small doses of non-depolarising relaxant if relaxation is needed
- Emergence
  - Empty the stomach with an orogastric tube
  - Full stomach: extubate only when awake and protecting the airway
- Postoperative: substantial analgesia needed if no working regional; IV paracetamol or parecoxib, morphine

## Management for both techniques
- Antibiotic: IV cefazolin 2g before skin incision (some omit if ampicillin already given)
- Protect placental perfusion
  - Left lateral tilt to avoid aortocaval compression
    - Some prefer 30º head-up: better FRC, less breast obstruction to laryngoscopy, less reflux
  - Maintain BP (placental perfusion is not pressure-dependent)
  - Consider supplemental oxygen or high FiO2 if the indication is fetal distress
- Aid delivery
  - Uterine relaxation with GTN 50mcg boluses, e.g. fetus low after prolonged labour
- Reduce PPH after delivery
  - Uterotonics (e.g. oxytocin) routinely given straight after delivery to contract the uterus
  - Surgeons may ask for IV 1g tranexamic acid if bleeding is significant
- Routine adjuncts after delivery
  - Prophylactic antiemetics (e.g. 4mg dexamethasone + 4mg ondansetron)
  - Paracetamol or NSAIDs, can be rectal
    - PR diclofenac 50mg
    - PR paracetamol 1300mg (as 650mg suppositories)
    - Consent for suppositories
    - Check whether paracetamol was given on the ward
- Document times: arrival in theatre, induction, anaesthesia ready, skin incision, delivery, drugs given

## Postoperative analgesia
- Simple: paracetamol, NSAIDs
- Neuraxial opioids given at the time of the block
- If no neuraxial opioid (e.g. GA)
  - TAP or quadratus lumborum blocks
  - Continuous wound LA infusion placed by the surgeon
  - Subcutaneous LA infiltration
- PROSPECT 2020 (elective caesarean under neuraxial)
  - Intrathecal morphine 50–100 mcg or diamorphine 300 mcg before surgery; paracetamol; NSAIDs; IV dexamethasone after delivery
  - Without intrathecal opioid: single-shot wound infiltration, continuous wound infusion and/or fascial plane blocks (TAP, quadratus lumborum)
  - Afterwards: regular paracetamol and NSAIDs, opioids for rescue
  - Surgical: Joel-Cohen incision, peritoneum left open, abdominal binder
  - TENS may be added
  - May not apply to unplanned or GA caesareans

## Crash caesarean section
- GA is fastest
- A rapid sequence spinal may be tried

## Flashcards
- Q: Target decision-to-delivery intervals for category 1 and 2?
  - A: Category 1: 30 min or less; category 2: <75min.
- Q: Sensory level needed for caesarean section and why?
  - A: T4, to block visceral pain from peritoneal traction (T10 covers somatic pain only).
- Q: Typical spinal for caesarean section?
  - A: Heavy bupivacaine 10-12mg (2.2-2.3ml) + fentanyl 10-15mcg + morphine 100mcg.
- Q: Epidural top-up mix for urgent caesarean section?
  - A: 20 ml lignocaine 2% + 2ml 8.4% NaHCO3 + 0.1 ml adrenaline 1:1000, plus fentanyl 50-75mcg.
- Q: Why limit volatile after delivery under GA?
  - A: High concentrations cause uterine atony and haemorrhage.
