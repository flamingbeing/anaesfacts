# Glaucoma Surgery

Glaucomatous optic nerves are fragile: avoid rises in IOP, avoid orbital pressure after local anaesthetic and keep MAP up.

## Glaucoma patients
- Higher risk of losing sight if a sight-threatening complication happens
  - Many such complications act at the optic nerve or its head
  - A glaucomatous nerve is already damaged, so pressure or ischaemia harms it more easily
- Risk of "wipe-out" (snuff syndrome)
  - Total, unexplained loss of vision in the operated eye
  - Incidence up to 1-2% in some studies
  - Higher risk with existing glaucomatous field defects
  - Milder forms: unexplained worsening of the visual field
  - Vision often returns with time
- Likely causes relate to the optic nerve itself, surgical factors or IOP changes after surgery; no anaesthetic mechanism proven
- Possible anaesthetic factors
  - Regional block: local anaesthetic pressing on a vulnerable optic nerve
    - Mass effect or compartment syndrome (local anaesthetic trapped in a fascial compartment behind the globe)
    - Pressure device applied after the block
    - Retrobulbar haemorrhage
    - Hyaluronidase orbitopathy
  - Regional block: direct optic nerve trauma
    - Needle injury
    - Optic nerve haematoma
  - Poor perfusion of the optic nerve head
    - Vasoconstrictor (such as adrenaline) in the local anaesthetic
    - Low blood pressure under GA
    - Injury to posterior ciliary vessels (posterior sub-Tenon's block)

## Glaucoma drugs
- Echothiophate eye drops
  - Irreversible cholinesterase inhibitor used for miosis
  - Can lower plasma cholinesterase and prolong suxamethonium and mivacurium
- Timolol: topical beta-blocker; may cause systemic beta-blockade
- Acetazolamide: carbonic anhydrase inhibitor that lowers IOP
  - Can cause metabolic acidosis, low potassium and low chloride
- Mannitol: sometimes used to lower IOP; osmotic diuresis can distend the bladder

## Trabeculectomy
- Standard glaucoma filtration operation
  - A small opening in the sclera lets aqueous drain more freely to the conjunctival veins
- Usually takes 20-60 min
- Usual steps
  - Stay suture pulls the eye down (risk of oculocardiac reflex)
  - Superior conjunctiva and Tenon's capsule reflected
  - Scleral diathermy to limit bleeding
  - Scleral flap ("trapdoor") above the limbus
  - Entry into anterior chamber, peripheral iridectomy
  - Scleral flap and conjunctiva sutured
  - Topical antimetabolite (mitomycin C, 5-fluorouracil) to reduce scarring
- Anticoagulants and antiplatelets usually stopped for 1-3 weeks (risk of sight-threatening choroidal haemorrhage while IOP is low early on)
- Main cause of failure: scarring (over-healing) of Tenon's layer
- Anaesthetic options
  - Regional block
  - Topical with intracameral
  - GA

## Glaucoma drainage devices
- Newer alternative to trabeculectomy
- Valved (IOP-regulating valve, most often the Ahmed tube) or non-valved
- Longer than trabeculectomy
- Steps similar to trabeculectomy, plus:
  - Posterior dissection with muscle handling: more discomfort and oculocardiac reflex
  - Plate fixed to the globe
  - Tube attached to the plate and inserted into the anterior chamber
  - Tube covered with a scleral flap or graft
- Anaesthesia
  - Peribulbar or retrobulbar block is the commonest choice internationally (akinesia plus anaesthesia)
  - Sub-Tenon's block is a good alternative
  - Topical or intracameral usually inadequate, as extraocular muscles are handled
  - GA for special cases such as claustrophobia or altered mental state

## Other procedures
- Non-penetrating surgery (viscocanalostomy, deep sclerectomy)
  - Aqueous filters through a natural membrane (trabeculo-Descemet's membrane)
  - Like trabeculectomy but the anterior chamber is not entered
  - Less risk of hypotony, but poorer long-term IOP control
- Cyclodestructive procedures (cyclodiode laser, cyclocryotherapy)
  - Often used when everything else has failed
  - Ablating the ciliary body cuts aqueous production and lowers IOP
  - Quick and easy for the patient, but higher risk of hypotony and retinal oedema
  - Needs good analgesia
  - No need to stop anticoagulants or antiplatelets
- Combined glaucoma and cataract surgery: longer operation

## Main anaesthetic considerations
- Avoid rises in IOP: no straining, coughing or hypertension
- Careful regional technique; avoid local anaesthetic mass effect behind the eye (wipe-out risk)
  - No pressure on the eye after the block: no Honan balloon, no ocular massage
- Maintain MAP for optic nerve perfusion
- Hypotony after surgery risks choroidal haemorrhage, so anticoagulants and antiplatelets are often stopped for days to weeks around surgery
- Most procedures cause little or no postoperative pain; cyclodestructive procedures cause the most

## Examination under anaesthesia
- Sometimes needed in glaucoma, most often congenital glaucoma
  - Repeated EUAs over many years for diagnosis and IOP monitoring
- Main aim: avoid anything that changes IOP significantly under GA

## Flashcards
- Q: What is wipe-out (snuff) syndrome and how common is it?
  - A: Unexplained total loss of vision after eye surgery; up to 1-2% in some studies
- Q: How long are anticoagulants and antiplatelets usually stopped before trabeculectomy?
  - A: 1-3 weeks
- Q: Which glaucoma eye drop prolongs suxamethonium?
  - A: Echothiophate (irreversible cholinesterase inhibitor)
- Q: Why should a Honan balloon be avoided after a block in glaucoma patients?
  - A: Pressure on an already damaged optic nerve risks wipe-out
- Q: Which glaucoma procedure does not require stopping anticoagulants?
  - A: Cyclodestructive procedures
