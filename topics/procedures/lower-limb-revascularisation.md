# Lower Limb Revascularisation and Critical Limb Ischaemia

Surgery for limb-threatening ischaemia in vasculopaths, often urgent and often on antiplatelets or anticoagulants.

## Indications for surgery in critical limb ischaemia
- Ischaemic rest pain
- Tissue loss (ulceration or gangrene)
- Severe claudication with distal disease
- Failed non-surgical treatment

## Surgery
- Most patients can be treated radiologically with percutaneous angioplasty
- Those who come to surgery often have extensive, diffuse or advanced disease

## Preoperative assessment
- Standard vasculopath assessment
- Effort tolerance is hard to judge
  - Claudication limits mobility, so cardiorespiratory symptoms are often hidden
- Pain control
  - Some patients are on long-term opioids and may be tolerant
  - Involving pain specialists can help
- Urgency
  - Often urgent with little time for investigations, but usually not an emergency; optimise as far as possible
- Investigations
  - FBC, renal panel, PT/PTT
  - ECG, chest X-ray
  - Group and crossmatch

## Intraoperative management
- Monitoring: 5 lead ECG
- Technique: spinal is often contraindicated because of anticoagulation or dual antiplatelet therapy
- Drugs: cardiostable induction, balanced GA

## Flashcards
- Q: What are the indications for surgery in critical limb ischaemia?
  - A: Ischaemic rest pain, tissue loss, severe claudication with distal disease, and failed non-surgical treatment.
- Q: Why is effort tolerance hard to assess in peripheral arterial disease?
  - A: Claudication limits mobility, masking cardiorespiratory symptoms.
- Q: Why is spinal anaesthesia often unsuitable for lower limb revascularisation?
  - A: Patients are often anticoagulated or on dual antiplatelet therapy.
- Q: What ECG monitoring is used intraoperatively?
  - A: 5 lead ECG.
