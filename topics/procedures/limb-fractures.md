# Limb Fractures

Widely variable cases; key issues are urgency, associated trauma, blood loss with proximal fractures, fat embolism and blocks masking compartment syndrome.

## Overview
- Duration: 5min to many hours
- Pain: variable
- Position: usually supine
- Blood loss: minimal, but up to 2000ml in open procedures
- Techniques: GA with or without a block, or regional block alone

## Type of procedure and urgency
- More urgent
  - Debridement of open fractures
  - Neurovascular compromise
  - External fixation
  - Hip fractures
- Less urgent: ORIF, nailing
- Bone grafting may be needed (e.g. from the pelvis; donor site is painful)

## Site of fracture
- Proximal fractures need special attention
  - Hip (neck of femur) fractures
  - Proximal humerus fractures
  - Pelvic fractures: high-velocity injury; GA (regional is difficult because of massive bleeding and spine injuries); fat embolism risk
- Distal fractures

## Trauma considerations
- Some fractures come with high-energy trauma and associated injuries
- Patient may be in surgical ICU

## Preoperative assessment
### Discuss with the surgeon
- Check whether more surgery is likely (manipulation under anaesthesia may become ORIF)
- If planning a block, discuss compartment syndrome risk

### Trauma
- Look for other significant chest, abdominal or head injuries
  - Recent (<24hr) moderate or severe head injury: think carefully before non-lifesaving surgery; may need ICP monitoring and postoperative ventilation in neuro ICU
  - Check cervical spine clearance where relevant
  - Chest X-ray for pneumothorax in all major trauma
- Note time of last food or drink relative to time of injury
  - Some patients, especially children, may never empty the stomach; if in doubt, treat as full stomach

## Intraoperative management
### Anaesthetic technique
- With a head injury, GA can hide a falling conscious level and may raise ICP
- Regional anaesthesia helps analgesia and can be the sole technique for some reductions, e.g.:
  - Clavicle fracture: interscalene plus supraclavicular
  - Femoral fracture: fascia iliaca or PENG block
- Blocks can mask the neurological signs of developing compartment syndrome; agree a plan with the surgeon first
- Blocks also hinder assessment when nerve damage is suspected
  - Humeral fractures with planned wire banding: avoid blocks, as the radial nerve is easily trapped and a block delays the diagnosis

### Monitoring and equipment
- Limb surgery: antireflux valve on IV line
  - No IV cannula on the injured limb
  - No BP cuff on the injured limb
- Blood loss depends on site
  - Proximal fractures (femur, humerus, pelvis) and bone grafting bleed considerably: large-bore IV, blood warmer, consider a rapid infuser
- Surgical equipment
  - May need a traction table
  - Check implants are available
  - Metalwork usually needs an image intensifier; radiation precautions

### Maintenance
- Tourniquet reduces bleeding but may be contraindicated by fracture type or site
- Proximal fractures, especially femur and pelvis: fat embolism risk

### Open fractures
- Often debridement and external fixation only
  - ORIF possible if contamination is minimal
- Consider gentamicin in addition to cefazolin depending on contamination
- Wound may be left open, needing a flap later

## Postoperative care
- Closed reductions: perioperative opioid, then oral NSAIDs, paracetamol and opioid
- Complex repairs, including external fixation: may need PCA

## Flashcards
- Q: Maximum expected blood loss in open limb fracture surgery?
  - A: Up to 2000ml
- Q: Which head-injured patients need careful thought before non-lifesaving fracture surgery?
  - A: Recent (<24hr) moderate or severe head injury
- Q: Why avoid a block for humeral fracture fixation with wire banding?
  - A: The radial nerve is easily trapped and a block delays the diagnosis
- Q: Blocks for femoral fracture analgesia?
  - A: Fascia iliaca or PENG block
- Q: Antibiotics for an open fracture?
  - A: Cefazolin, plus gentamicin depending on contamination
