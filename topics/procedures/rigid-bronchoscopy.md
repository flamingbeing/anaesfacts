# Rigid Bronchoscopy

Straight metal scope in the trachea that itself becomes the airway: shared, tubeless and highly stimulating.

## Procedure
- Straight metal tube with a bevelled tip passed into the trachea

## Indications
- Diagnostic
  - Biopsy when larger samples are needed than flexible forceps can take
- Therapeutic
  - Massive haemoptysis: better suction, and the bleeding airway can be packed or isolated
  - Foreign body removal: mainly children, or large or sharp objects
  - Airway stenting and dilation for tracheobronchial stenosis (silicone or metal stents)
  - Tumour debulking: mechanical resection, laser or cryotherapy for endobronchial obstruction
  - Intraluminal tracheobronchial repair

## Contraindications (rare)
- Extreme ventilation or oxygenation requirements
- Severe bleeding disorder

## Technique
- Position: supine at the table edge, neck extended ("shaving" or sniffing-chin position) to line up oral, pharyngeal and laryngeal axes
  - Head ring (donut)
- Insertion
  - Bite guard to protect the teeth
  - Scope passed through the mouth, thumb shielding the upper teeth
  - Epiglottis lifted to see the cords
- Ventilation via the side port or by jet ventilation
  - The scope is the airway, so surgeon and anaesthetist must coordinate closely
- Monitoring: continuous ETCO2 if the circuit is closed; otherwise watch chest movement and SpO2

## Complications
- Dental injury, lip lacerations
- Airway trauma, including tracheobronchial perforation and vocal cord injury
- Bleeding, especially with debulking or biopsy
- Laryngospasm, bronchospasm, aspiration, hypoxia
- Arrhythmias or haemodynamic instability from intense sympathetic stimulation
- Airway fire

## Anaesthetic considerations
- Underlying pathology: risk of complete obstruction with inability to ventilate
  - e.g. keep spontaneous ventilation until the scope is past the obstruction
- Tubeless technique
- Shared airway
- Unprotected airway with aspiration risk
- Very stimulating, yet immobility is needed and coughing must be avoided
- Postoperative airway oedema
- Surgical techniques used: stenting, balloon bronchoplasty, laser, endobronchial electrosurgery, argon plasma coagulation

## Preoperative assessment
- Assess the airway obstruction
  - CT thorax: site (e.g. carinal involvement) and degree of obstruction
  - Clinically: stridor, orthopnoea
  - Decide whether the morphology is safe or unsafe
- Emergency planning
  - Clear plan for failed ventilation
  - ECMO on standby if the airway is judged critical or unsafe for induction
- Usually urgent or emergency cases in physiologically unwell patients
- Look for neck pathology: the atlanto-axial joint is extended almost fully
- Check teeth

## Ventilation and oxygenation options
- All are tubeless; can be combined; choice depends on patient and procedure
- Apnoeic oxygenation
  - Relies on O2 mass flow (THRIVE)
  - For: still surgical field; nothing blocking the scope lumen
  - Against: hypercapnia and progressive respiratory acidosis make it strictly time-limited
- Spontaneous with assisted ventilation
  - Supplemental O2 via the side port; manual assistance during apnoea or desaturation
  - For: keeps negative-pressure breathing, which helps splint the airway; real-time feedback on depth and airway patency
  - Against
    - Hard to balance anaesthetic depth with respiratory drive
    - Needs constant watching of chest movement
    - Movement or coughing can injure the trachea
    - Hypoventilation and hypercapnia are common
    - Sympathetic stimulation
- Side-port IPPV (bronchoscopy only)
  - Breathing circuit connected to the side port; silastic caps seal the ports; oropharyngeal gauze packing reduces leak around the uncuffed scope
  - For: ETCO2 monitoring; volatile delivery possible; most familiar, standard circle-system ventilation
  - Against
    - Large leak needs high gas flows; may fail over long cases
    - Inconsistent tidal volumes
    - Unreliable volatile delivery and theatre pollution
    - Sealing may interrupt surgery
- Manual low-frequency jet ventilation (e.g. Sanders)
  - High-pressure O2 bursts through a narrow cannula
  - For: oxygenates even in a fully open system; no caps or seals, so surgery is continuous
  - Against: tidal volume and ETCO2 hard to measure; needs enough expiratory time to avoid gas trapping and barotrauma
- Automated high-frequency jet ventilation (e.g. Monsoon)
  - Rates of 50-500 bpm with tidal volumes smaller than dead space
  - Infraglottic jetting through the rigid scope
  - For: fairly still field with little cord movement; lower airway pressures, useful with bronchopleural or bronchoesophageal fistula
  - Against: needs specialised jet ventilator and high-pressure gas; risk of gas trapping and barotrauma

## Maintenance
- Deep general anaesthesia to blunt the strong sympathetic response
  - Remifentanil for titratable analgesia
  - Topical lidocaine to larynx and trachea, or regional airway blocks
- TIVA for precise titration
  - Volatiles: end-tidal values inaccurate and theatre pollution
- Muscle relaxation to prevent coughing or movement against the metal scope (risk of tracheal perforation)
  - Alternative: keep spontaneous ventilation until the airway is secured or the scope is past the obstruction
- Glycopyrrolate to reduce secretions
- Dexamethasone to reduce airway oedema

## Flashcards
- Q: What ventilation rates does automated high-frequency jet ventilation use?
  - A: 50-500 bpm, with tidal volumes below dead space.
- Q: What limits apnoeic oxygenation during rigid bronchoscopy?
  - A: Progressive hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis.
- Q: How is leak reduced with side-port IPPV?
  - A: Silastic caps on the ports and gauze packing of the oropharynx.
- Q: What backup should be on standby for a critical airway obstruction?
  - A: ECMO.
- Q: Why is paralysis used during rigid bronchoscopy?
  - A: To prevent coughing or movement against the rigid tube, which risks tracheal perforation.
