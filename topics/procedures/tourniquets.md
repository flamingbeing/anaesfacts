# Tourniquets

Pneumatic limb tourniquets give a bloodless field and less blood loss in limb surgery, at the cost of ischaemia, pain and reperfusion effects on release.

## Basics
- Sites: upper arm and thigh have enough muscle bulk to spread cuff pressure evenly (recommended)
- Calf tourniquet: some surgeons prefer it for short operations (<1hr) in fit patients; also useful with a popliteal or saphenous block
- Maximum duration 120mins (2hrs)
  - If surgery runs past 2 hours, deflate for 20–30 minutes to wash out metabolites and reoxygenate tissue, then reinflate
- Pressure
  - Upper limb: systolic BP + 50 mmHg (typically 200–250 mmHg)
  - Lower limb: systolic BP + 75–100 mmHg (typically 250–300 mmHg); thigh is thicker and there is often no room for a full-size cuff
- Pneumatic only: mechanical tourniquets cause unpredictable high-pressure areas
  - Usually inflated via a pressure-monitoring system

## Exsanguination
- Esmarch bandage (expressive exsanguination) contraindicated with:
  - Tumour or severe infection (risk of spread)
  - Suspected DVT (fatal PE reported)
- Compressing both legs together adds 15% to circulating volume: risk of LV failure; limit to one leg in at-risk patients
- Alternative: elevate the limb at 90° for 5min without compression

## Uses
- Limb surgery
- Less blood loss
- Bloodless operative field

## Contraindications
- Absolute
  - AV fistula
  - Severe peripheral vascular disease (PVD alone is relative)
  - Previous vascular surgery
  - Fracture at the cuff site
  - Thrombosis
  - Severe crush injury (compartment syndrome risk)
- Relative
  - Sickle cell disease: controversial; red cells sickle under anoxia and thrombose, but some surgeons use one after full exsanguination; keep as short as possible
  - Previous thromboembolism
  - Skin grafts
  - Local infection
  - Lymphoedema, post-mastectomy

## Physiological effects: during inflation
- Local
  - Muscle ischaemia
  - Nerve ischaemia: conduction block (motor and sensory) develops 15 to 45 min after supra-systolic arm cuff inflation; reverses after deflation
- Systemic
  - SVR and circulating volume rise, so BP rises at first (usually transient)
    - Bilateral thigh tourniquets add up to 15% to effective blood volume (∼750 ml in an adult); can precipitate cardiac failure
  - Later, slow gradual rise in BP and HR (tourniquet pain, below)
  - Temperature: core temperature slowly rises (less heat transfer to and loss from the ischaemic limb), ∼0.5°C after 2 h
  - Haematological: hypercoagulable, from platelet aggregation driven by catecholamines released by surgical and tourniquet pain

## Physiological effects: after deflation
- Brief reperfusion event is common; size relates to ischaemia time; all changes resolve within 30 min
- Cardiovascular: CVP and SVR fall, causing hypotension
  - From post-ischaemic reactive hyperaemia and washout of ischaemic metabolites
  - Best prevented by fluid loading before and during release
  - Usually lasts 10-15mins
- Respiratory: desaturation and hypercarbia, usually peaking at 1min
  - Spontaneous breathing: minute ventilation rises fast; FE′CO2 back to baseline in 3–5 min
  - Controlled ventilation: FE′CO2 stays raised for >6 min unless minute ventilation is deliberately increased
- CNS: hypercarbia raises cerebral blood flow and ICP; can worsen secondary brain injury
  - Hyperventilate after release to keep normocapnia
- Metabolic: hyperkalaemia, hyperlactataemia, acidosis (lactate and CO2 from the limb lower arterial pH)
- Temperature: core temperature falls (heat redistribution)
- Haematological: increased fibrinolysis
- Pulmonary embolism can occur after release

## Complications
- Skin: friction burns from poorly applied cuffs
- Muscle
  - Post-tourniquet syndrome: swollen, pale, stiff, weak (not paralysed) limb, typically lasting 1–6 weeks
  - Rarely compartment syndrome
- Arteries: pressure ruptures atheromatous plaque
- Nerves (neuropraxia)
  - Ischaemia
  - Direct compression causes a second, longer conduction block ("tourniquet paralysis")
    - Worst at the proximal and distal cuff edges, where the pressure gradient is greatest
  - Risk rises with tourniquet time
  - So some anaesthetists avoid nerve blocks with a tourniquet
- Systemic effects can lead to cardiovascular collapse

## Tourniquet pain
- Mechanism unclear; possibly nerve compression or ischaemia
- Awake: most tolerate a 250 mmHg cuff for about 20 minutes
- Under GA: after 30–60min, HR and diastolic BP rise, typically climbing over 1–2 h
- Systemic treatment (difficult)
  - Opioids
  - Deepen anaesthesia
  - Ketamine (e.g. 0.25mg/kg)
  - Nitrous oxide
  - Short-acting beta-blockers (esmolol, labetalol)
  - Short-acting vasodilators (GTN, nitroprusside)
- Regional techniques
  - Spinal or epidural abolishes tourniquet pain
  - Peripheral blocks less reliable
    - Add an intercostobrachial nerve (ICBN) block to brachial plexus blocks
    - No good block for a lower-limb tourniquet; femoral +/- lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block spares the posterior thigh

## Cuff selection and application
- Width: per the American Heart Association, a cuff 20% wider than the upper arm diameter, or 40% of thigh circumference (maximum 20cm), transmits cuff pressure fully to the artery, avoiding excessive pressures
- Modern silicone cuffs are narrower: arm 90mm (bladder 70mm), leg 105mm (bladder 75mm)
- Length should exceed limb circumference by 7–15cm
- Place at the limb's maximum circumference
- Protect underlying skin with cotton wool
- Digit tourniquets are easily forgotten and dangerous; use a rubber strip held with artery forceps

## Flashcards
- Q: What are the usual tourniquet pressures for upper and lower limb?
  - A: Upper limb systolic BP + 50 mmHg (200–250 mmHg); lower limb systolic BP + 75–100 mmHg (250–300 mmHg).
- Q: What is the maximum tourniquet time, and what if surgery takes longer?
  - A: 120 min; deflate for 20–30 minutes before reinflating.
- Q: How much does bilateral leg exsanguination add to circulating volume?
  - A: Up to 15% (about 750 ml in an adult).
- Q: How long do post-deflation changes last?
  - A: Hypotension usually 10-15 min; all changes reverse within 30 min.
- Q: Which anaesthetic technique abolishes tourniquet pain?
  - A: Spinal or epidural anaesthesia.
