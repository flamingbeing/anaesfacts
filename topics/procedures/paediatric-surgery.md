# Paediatric General and Urological Surgery

Anaesthetic considerations for common paediatric general, urological and laparoscopic operations: mostly day cases, with local anaesthetic blocks at the core of multimodal analgesia.

## General considerations
- Many (70-80%) procedures are day cases
- Most involve the penis or groin
- Multimodal analgesia, using LA techniques where possible
- Laparoscopic surgery increasing fast

## Lumps and bumps
- About 1% of excised superficial lumps in children are malignant
- About four-fifths of malignant ones show at least one of:
  - Presentation in the neonatal period
  - Rapid or progressive growth
  - Skin ulceration
  - Fixed (immobile)
  - Firm mass >3 cm across
- 5-10% of benign lumps regress; 90% persist or slowly grow and are removed (cosmesis, avoid later infection or inflammation, avoid missing cancer)
- Types: lipomas, haemangiomas, lymph nodes, naevi, dermoid cysts, calcified hair follicles; rarely skin cancer such as melanoma

### Neck lumps
- Can be hard to diagnose; cervical lymphadenopathy is commonest (25-30%)
- Grouped as infective, inflammatory or congenital (embryological)
- Branchial remnants
  - Cysts, sinuses or cartilage tags left from folding of the branchial arches (branchia = gills)
  - Usually preauricular cartilage tags or pits
  - Need formal excision
  - No airway problem unless infected
- Thyroglossal derivatives
  - Thyroid arises at the back of the tongue (posterior third) and descends in front of the trachea
  - No descent: lingual thyroid (rare)
  - Commoner: thyroglossal cyst along the line of descent; thyroid tissue can lie anywhere on this path
  - Midline swelling that moves on swallowing and tongue protrusion
  - Excised; assess the airway
- Cervicofacial dermoids
  - Cysts along fusion lines of the frontal, maxillary and mandibular processes
  - Commonest at the upper outer forehead (external angular dermoid); submental in the neck
  - Midline submental swelling; rarely affects the airway
- Cystic hygroma
  - Hamartomatous lymphatic malformation; multicystic, infiltrates tissue planes
  - 60%+ in the neck; transilluminates
  - If diagnosed antenatally, the newborn may need intubation
  - Can infiltrate pharynx and larynx, obstructing the upper airway
- Sternomastoid tumour
  - Lump in the middle third of sternocleidomastoid from birth stretch injury
  - Found weeks after birth, with or without torticollis
  - Treated with physiotherapy and manipulation
- Cervical lymphadenopathy: stable, non-tender enlarged node; usually reactive, rarely neoplastic (e.g. lymphoma)
- Cervical lymphadenitis
  - Swollen, tender, inflamed node
  - Usually streptococcal or staphylococcal; occasionally mycobacterial (long history)
  - Drain if no response to antibiotics
  - Large infective swellings may distort the airway or cause trismus

### Lumps: anaesthetic management
- Preoperative: usually unremarkable; parents may be anxious about cancer; airway problems rare
- Most operations quick; occasionally frozen section or bigger surgery than planned
- Inhalational or IV induction by preference, veins and ease of cannulation
- Usually LMA with spontaneous ventilation
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
- Surgeon usually infiltrates LA with adrenaline (analgesia and haemostasis); add an NSAID if wanted
- Postoperative: paracetamol and NSAID; PONV rare; most eat, drink and go home a couple of hours later

## Appendicectomy
- Commonest non-elective general surgical operation in children
- Mostly school age; also younger children, infants, rarely neonates
- Classic: central colicky pain moving to the right iliac fossa, low fever, vomiting
- Ultrasound sensitivity and specificity >80%; CT >90% (used less)
- Diagnosis often late in young children (atypical); more perforation, abscess and peritonitis in preschool children
- Young children compensate well for hypovolaemia and sepsis, so may be very sick by surgery
- Open muscle-splitting or laparoscopic (also diagnostic)

### Appendicectomy: preoperative assessment
- Usual history (illness, past history, anaesthetics, allergies, drugs); mostly healthy
- Often not drinking for a day or two, vomiting, febrile: assess fluid balance; IV fluids
- Analgesia as needed; FBC and U&E

### Appendicectomy: anaesthetic technique
- Usually already has IV access
- Rapid sequence induction; ETT and IPPV
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
- Analgesia depends on preoperative analgesia, open or laparoscopic, and perforation, abscess or peritonitis
  - Morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV
  - Plus wound infiltration and an NSAID if no contraindication
  - Some give less opioid until the pathology is clear
- Antiemetic, e.g. ondansetron 100 mcg/kg IV
- Young or late-presenting children may decompensate on induction: may need a couple of 20 mL/kg fluid boluses; manage like a major laparotomy (as for intussusception)

### Appendicectomy: postoperative care
- Usually surgical ward; continue maintenance fluids and antibiotics
- Analgesia by condition, peritonism and extent of surgery
  - IV morphine infusion or PCA, plus paracetamol and NSAID; or
  - Oral opioid (codeine phosphate 1 mg/kg 4-hourly) plus paracetamol and NSAID
- Some young late-presenting children need HDU: oxygen, more fluid, urine output monitoring

## Intussusception
- One segment of bowel telescopes into another, usually just proximal to the ileocaecal valve
- Compression and mesenteric traction cause lymphatic and venous obstruction and oedema; then arterial compromise and gangrene unless reduced urgently
- Incidence 2-4:1000; male to female ratio 3:2; peak age 5-18 months
- Mostly idiopathic; possible lead points: Meckel's diverticulum, lymph nodes, polyps, duplications
- Classic: intermittent colicky pain with blood and mucus in stool (redcurrant jelly stool)
- Confirmed by ultrasound or CT
- First line: air or barium enema in radiology
- Urgent laparotomy (or laparoscopy) if enema fails or sepsis or gangrene suspected

### Intussusception: preoperative assessment
- May need large fluid resuscitation (20 mL/kg repeated once or twice is common)
- Analgesia, e.g. morphine 100-200 mcg/kg IV
- FBC, U&E, group and save

### Intussusception: anaesthetic technique
- Usually has IV access
- RSI usual; young children often refuse preoxygenation, so gentle face-mask IPPV before intubation may be needed to avoid hypoxia
- Second IV cannula; nasogastric tube; antibiotics if not already given
- Neuromuscular blockade and IPPV
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen and air; remifentanil 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min reduces volatile need and stabilises the circulation
- Regional techniques other than wound infiltration usually relatively contraindicated
- Morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg, depending on prior opioid and remifentanil use
- Several 20 mL/kg fluid boluses may be needed, especially with resection of dead bowel; transfusion possible
- Warming mattress and blanket, warmed fluids; monitor central and peripheral temperature
- If bowel resected and HDU or ITU planned: place central and arterial lines and a urinary catheter at the end while still anaesthetised

### Intussusception: postoperative care
- Ward if well (no resection, stable, no oxygen need)
  - Continue maintenance fluids and IV antibiotics
  - IV morphine infusion for 24-48 hours
  - Nil by mouth; IV or rectal paracetamol
  - NSAIDs used cautiously after hypovolaemia or possible sepsis; usually withheld for 12-24 hours until clearly recovering
- HDU or ITU if oxygen-dependent, unstable, still needing fluid, or oliguric
- Same principles apply to any paediatric laparotomy

## Circumcision
- Very common; removes part or all of the foreskin; usually day case
- Indications: parental social or religious reasons, balanitis xerotica obliterans, infection, phimosis, paraphimosis
  - Religious circumcision in some communities is often at 6-12 years
  - Rarely if ever urgent
- Supine; minimal blood loss

### Circumcision: preoperative assessment
- Usually healthy
- Main issue: respiratory infection; decide whether to proceed or defer

### Circumcision: anaesthetic technique
- Older boys often anxious: sedative premedication (midazolam 0.5 mg/kg; maximum 20 mg)
- Technique depends mainly on age
- Older boys: IV induction; LMA with spontaneous ventilation
- Infants (often chubby, religious or cultural cases): inhalational induction; ETT and IPPV may be better
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
- Often no IV fluids needed, especially without prolonged fasting

### Circumcision: analgesia
- Caudal epidural or penile nerve block
- Infants: caudal (0.3 mL/kg 0.5% levobupivacaine or similar)
  - Motor block not a concern
  - Penile blocks hard to do reliably in infants
- Older boys: penile nerve block more usual (0.1 mL/kg 0.25% bupivacaine or similar each side)
  - Motor block after caudal now a problem
  - Penile block more reliable than in infants
- Both blocks can fail; usually obvious during surgery: give opioid so the child does not wake without analgesia
  - Morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV, codeine phosphate 0.5-1 mg/kg IM, or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV
- NSAID in all cases unless contraindicated (can be oral after surgery)
- Lidocaine ointment on the glans at the end as a supplement
- Bleeding after circumcision (re-exploration for haemostasis): check with the surgeon whether a penile block is acceptable

### Circumcision: postoperative care
- Eats and drinks soon after
- Home analgesia often supplied (lidocaine ointment, ibuprofen, paracetamol)
  - Paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly plus NSAID, e.g. ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly
  - Significant pain for 24-48 hours is normal: advise regular analgesia at home
- Discharge usually 2-4 hours after: passed urine, walking if appropriate, not bleeding

## Preputioplasty
- Dorsal slit with transverse closure: treats phimosis while keeping the foreskin
- Narrow preputial opening cut lengthways and closed crossways
- Less morbidity than circumcision
- Done in older boys; not an alternative to religious or cultural circumcision in infants
- Preoperative assessment as for circumcision
- Technique: sedative premedication if needed; IV induction; LMA with spontaneous ventilation; volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
- Analgesia easier than for circumcision
  - Penile nerve block 0.1 mL/kg 0.25% levobupivacaine or similar is almost always enough
  - Need not be circumferential: only the dorsal penile nerves need blocking
  - Plus NSAID unless contraindicated
- Postoperative: early oral intake; paracetamol, NSAID and weak opioid prescribed but rarely needed in hospital; home at 2-4 hours; much less pain than circumcision

## Inguinal hernia and hydrocele
- Inguinal hernia incidence 1-3% (five times commoner in boys), about 10% in premature infants, 30% if born at <28 weeks' gestation; falls with age
- The processus vaginalis (peritoneal pouch) follows the testis into the scrotum; it closes in 90%
- Persistent patency causes:
  - Inguinal hernia: distal part closed, proximal open
  - Hydrocele: proximal closed, distal open
  - Encysted hydrocele of the cord: both ends closed, middle dilated
- Paediatric inguinal hernias never close by themselves: repair needed
  - Incarceration risk up to 31% in infants and young children (mostly first months of life), threatening bowel and testis: repair soon after diagnosis
  - Premature infants: timing varies; delay risks incarceration, early repair risks anaesthetic complications including apnoea
    - Some repair as soon as possible; some wait until about 55-60 weeks post-menstrual age
  - Neonatal hydrocele repair is delayed for 12–18 months, as the patent processus is tiny and may close

### Hernia: anaesthetic management
- Hydrocele surgery and anaesthesia almost the same as for hernia
- Mostly elective; neonates and infants may need urgent surgery for incarceration or strangulation
- Laparoscopic repair increasing

### Hernia: preoperative assessment
- Elective cases usually fit and well
- Incarcerated: may be vomiting, dehydrated, at aspiration risk; often had opioid for attempted manual reduction
- Premature infants: degree of prematurity, respiratory function, oxygen need, other anomalies or illness (e.g. patent ductus arteriosus)
- Warn parents of premature infants about postoperative apnoeas and possible postoperative ventilation

### Hernia: anaesthetic technique
- Older children
  - LMA with spontaneous ventilation usual
  - Incarcerated hernia: intubate
  - Laparoscopic repair: ETT and IPPV
- Neonates and infants (often incarcerated)
  - GA with ETT and IPPV usual
  - Caudal epidural: good option if not contraindicated, especially premature neonates
    - Usable up to 20 kg; must reach T12, needing 1 mL/kg 0.25% levobupivacaine or similar (Armitage formula)
  - Spinal (subarachnoid) block: 0.3-1 mg/kg 0.5% heavy bupivacaine
    - Fewer postoperative apnoeas
    - Limits: technical failure and short duration (about 60 min); some neonatal hernias take over 60 min
    - Provide postoperative analgesia, e.g. wound infiltration

### Hernia: analgesia
- Ilioinguinal block 0.3-0.5 mL/kg 0.25% levobupivacaine or similar: technique of choice for most
  - Infants: external oblique aponeurosis poorly formed, so surgeon blocking the nerve under direct vision may work better
- Caudal for children up to 20 kg
- Many also ask for surgical wound infiltration
- Some older (especially overweight) children need opioid plus antiemetic despite the block
  - Morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV, codeine phosphate 0.5-1 mg/kg IM, or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV
- NSAID if no contraindication

### Hernia: postoperative care
- Elective: early oral intake; paracetamol, NSAID, oral opioid; home 2-4 hours after
- Emergency (incarcerated): usually overnight stay; more swelling, bruising and pain
- After ilioinguinal block, check for femoral nerve block (5%) before walking unaided
- Ex-premature infants up to 48-60 weeks' postconceptual age (per local protocol): overnight stay with pulse oximetry and apnoea monitoring for 12 hours

## Tongue tie release
- Very quick, about 5mins
- Flexible LMA enough if tongue tie is the only procedure

## Paediatric urology: key issues
- May have renal impairment with electrolyte imbalance, anaemia, hypertension or metabolic problems
- Congenital urological anomalies increase latex allergy risk
- Antibiotic prophylaxis often needed (obstruction, urinary infection)
- Lithotomy position risks nerve injury: position carefully
- Irrigation fluid hides blood loss and increases hypothermia risk

## Orchidopexy (undescended testis)
- Cryptorchidism (undescended testis, UDT): commonest disorder of sexual differentiation, about 2% of male births
  - Undescended: high scrotal, inguinal canal or intra-abdominal
  - Ectopic (off the normal path after the external ring): superficial inguinal pouch, perineum, penile root, femoral canal; usually well developed and normal
  - Anorchia: vas ends blindly at the internal ring
- Consequences: testicular degeneration, reduced fertility, more germ cell tumours
- Surgery aims: preserve fertility, reduce trauma and torsion, make tumours easier to detect; main risk is atrophy from poor blood supply
- Orchidopexy moves the testis into the scrotum
- Very high or intra-abdominal testis: laparoscopically assisted or two-stage procedure
  - First stage brings the testis to the inguinal canal; second stage six months later places it in the scrotum
  - Vessel length decides one or two stages
  - Fowler-Stephens orchidopexy: testicular vessels divided; testis then relies on collaterals from the vasal artery
- Impalpable testis (20%): diagnostic laparoscopy to find intra-abdominal or absent testis
- May be on hormonal treatment to promote descent
- Ideally done in the second year (often later); common day case
- Inguinal plus small scrotal incision; hernia sac tied, vessels mobilised to gain length, testis placed in a scrotal subcutaneous pouch

### Orchidopexy: preoperative assessment
- Mostly fit and well
- Commoner in ex-premature boys: may have bronchopulmonary dysplasia (irritable airways) and poor venous access
- Can come with other anomalies: prune belly syndrome, posterior urethral valves, neural tube defects, gastroschisis, microcephaly
- Older boys may be very anxious

### Orchidopexy: anaesthesia and analgesia
- GA with LMA common
- Laryngospasm can occur on testicular traction if anaesthesia is too light
- Unilateral: ilioinguinal block plus surgical scrotal infiltration
- Bilateral: caudal may be better
- Testicular sympathetic supply enters the CNS at about T10: even with a good incision block, traction can trigger laryngospasm or vagal bradycardia
  - Some give opioid plus antiemetic (morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV, codeine phosphate 0.5-1 mg/kg IM, fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV)
  - Others intubate younger boys

### Orchidopexy: postoperative care
- Early oral intake; paracetamol, NSAID and oral opioid; home 2-4 hours after
- Boys over about 5 years have more pain than toddlers and more PONV; some stay overnight

## Acute scrotum
- Short history of acute scrotal pain; differentials by age: epididymo-orchitis, trauma, incarcerated hernia, appendicitis, tumour
- Neonates
  - Birth trauma or torsion, often after breech delivery
  - Neonatal torsion twists the whole cord (newly descended testis not yet fixed)
  - Almost always present late with necrosis
- Infants and toddlers
  - Commonest cause acute epididymo-orchitis (bacterial or viral)
  - Torsion cannot be excluded, so diagnosis usually at exploration
  - Irreducible inguinal hernia can mimic it
- School-age boys: highest prevalence
  - Torsion of the testis, torsion of a testicular appendage (hydatid of Morgagni), epididymo-orchitis
  - Torsion: twisted cord cuts blood supply; prolonged torsion causes necrosis
  - Bell clapper testis (no normal attachment to the scrotal lining) is a common cause
  - Torsion commonest in adolescents; epididymo-orchitis in younger boys
  - Hydatids of Morgagni: Mullerian duct remnants on upper pole of testis or epididymis; torsion commonest near puberty onset
  - Rarely a tumour presents this way
- Nothing excludes torsion: urgent exploration, ideally within 6 hours of pain onset
  - Salvage 80% within 6 hours, <25% after 18 hours
  - Testis untwisted and fixed with non-absorbable sutures; other side fixed too (bell clapper often bilateral)
  - Late atrophy can occur

### Acute scrotum: preoperative assessment
- Younger boys with epididymo-orchitis: often febrile, toxic, sometimes dehydrated after days of illness; fluids usually given during surgery
- Older boys may be quiet and frightened
- Rarely properly fasted; even after 6 hours, pain and stress mean the stomach may not be empty

### Acute scrotum: anaesthetic technique
- Usually ETT with spontaneous or controlled ventilation
  - Older boys: RSI
  - Neonates and infants: RSI may not be possible; inhalational induction may be needed
- LMA only if confident there is no increased regurgitation risk
  - Febrile younger boys have more laryngospasm
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
- IV fluids if febrile and unwell before surgery
- Analgesia options
  - LA infiltration plus NSAID
  - Plus intraoperative morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV
  - Caudal: useful in older boys for scrotal (sacral) incisions, less for inguinal (lumbar) ones; useful for both in neonates and infants; usually contraindicated if toxic
- Antiemetic except in neonates and infants, e.g. ondansetron 100 mcg/kg IV
- Testicular handling may cause a vagal response: have atropine or glycopyrronium ready
- Surgeon may ask for FiO2 100% after detorsion while checking the testis is viable

### Acute scrotum: postoperative care
- Quick recovery and oral intake; IV maintenance fluids for febrile infants until drinking
- Paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly, ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly, codeine phosphate 1 mg/kg oral 4-hourly

## Hypospadias repair
- Fairly common (about 1:350 male births); other anomalies rare; renal function usually normal
- Urethral meatus abnormally placed on the underside of the penis, anywhere down to the scrotum; severity depends on its position
- Foreskin incomplete, forming a dorsal hood
- Chordee (downward curve, especially on erection) usually present
- Surgical aims: neourethra with meatus at the glans tip, release chordee, enough skin cover, acceptable appearance
- Usually done in the first or second year of life
  - Distal: MAGPI (meatal advancement and glanuloplasty) or urethroplasty
  - Complex proximal: staged repair
  - Fistula may need re-operation
  - Large defects may need grafts, usually buccal mucosa (sometimes bladder mucosa)
  - Urethral or suprapubic catheter for 24-48 hours to bypass oedema
- Surgeons may want the patient right down at the foot of the table
- Chordee correction alone: as for hypospadias

### Hypospadias: anaesthetic technique
- Usually healthy boys
- Sedative premedication helps if multiple procedures
- Usually inhalational induction at this age
- Can last several hours: ETT with IPPV usual, though LMA with spontaneous ventilation often fine
- Buccal mucosa graft: intubate (nasal tube or oral RAE to one side of the mouth) plus throat pack
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
- Erection hinders surgery: prevent with a regional block and adequate depth

### Hypospadias: analgesia
- Caudal best for most (0.3-0.5 mL/kg 0.5% levobupivacaine or similar)
  - Adjuncts to prolong it: clonidine 1-2 mcg/kg or preservative-free ketamine 0.5 mg/kg
- Penile block if minor and distal
- NSAID unless contraindicated
- Mild postoperative sedation helps stop the boy touching dressings or catheter: opioid often given despite a good caudal (morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV or codeine phosphate 0.5-1 mg/kg IM)

### Hypospadias: postoperative care
- Maintenance fluids until drinking well, to keep urine flowing through the catheter
- Usually paracetamol, NSAID and oral opioid; IV morphine or nurse-controlled analgesia after major repairs
- After complex repairs, sutures often removed under anaesthesia at about 5 days

## Posterior urethral valves (valve ablation)
- Commonest cause of severe obstructive uropathy in children
- Severe obstruction can cause significant renal failure
- May have pulmonary hypoplasia and respiratory compromise
  - Little fetal urine means little amniotic fluid; the lungs need this fluid to branch and develop, so hypoplasia and respiratory distress follow
- Mild obstruction: mild or no complications
- Management
  - Prophylactic antibiotics from birth; bladder may need decompression
  - If creatinine stays or returns to normal: endoscopic transurethral valve ablation under GA
  - If renal function stops improving: vesicostomy first to decompress the kidneys
  - Very small infants (cystoscope will not pass): temporary vesicostomy (bladder dome brought to the lower abdominal wall); ablate and close later
  - Circumcision may be added to reduce UTIs
- Cystoscopy and ablation: usually less than 1 h; GA with ETT or LMA; little postoperative pain

## Vesicoureteric reflux: ureteric reimplantation
- Backflow of urine across the vesicoureteric junction (UVJ)
- Long-term antibiotic prophylaxis, especially up to age 8 years and with frequent recurrences, until resolved or corrected
- Medical or surgical treatment
- Surgery for: breakthrough UTIs on prophylaxis, congenital UVJ anomalies, poor renal growth, worsening scarring
- Open reimplantation (submucosal tunnel) is the gold standard; blood loss can be significant
- Less invasive: laparoscopic extravesical or intravesical reimplantation
- Endoscopic injection (usually polytetrafluoroethylene or silicone) submucosally at the 6 o'clock position of the UVJ
- Anaesthesia: epidural catheter or caudal (catheter or single shot) for intraoperative and postoperative analgesia
- Bladder spasm common after surgery: belladonna and opium suppositories, oral or intravesical oxybutynin, ketorolac, or bupivacaine via epidural or into the bladder

## Pelviureteric junction obstruction: pyeloplasty
- Commonest cause of obstructive uropathy in childhood
- Usually intrinsic narrowing from a congenital aperistaltic segment lacking normal muscle; often found on antenatal ultrasound
- Usually unilateral; unilateral cases usually have normal renal function
- Pyeloplasty relieves obstruction to preserve renal function
- Open via flank or subcostal incision (or transabdominal or posterior); increasingly laparoscopic

### Pyeloplasty: preoperative assessment
- Most healthy, without chronic renal disease or fluid or electrolyte problems
- Some have spina bifida, renal artery stenosis, polycystic disease, Wilms' tumour or chronic renal failure
  - Consider anaemia, hyperkalaemia, fluid overload, hypertension, current drugs, venous access
- Often months of illness and many investigations, some under anaesthesia
- Bleeding not usually a problem with pyeloplasty or nephrectomy

### Pyeloplasty: anaesthetic technique
- Sedative premedication if requested or indicated
- ETT and controlled ventilation; two IV cannulae
- Flank approach: older children lateral with table broken; babies on a flank roll
  - Lateral renal incision does not split muscle: less analgesia needed than for lower abdominal incisions
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide, plus remifentanil (0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/minute) or morphine 100-150 mcg/kg or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg
- Ondansetron 100 mcg/kg IV
- Maintenance fluids; may need crystalloid, colloid or blood
- Open surgery suits combined GA and regional
  - Lumbar epidural bolus spreads enough for surgery but is too low for a postoperative infusion
  - Postoperative epidural infusion needs a low thoracic catheter

### Pyeloplasty: postoperative care
- Maintenance fluids for 12-24 hours; many drink the same evening after retroperitoneal or laparoscopic surgery
- Open: IV morphine, PCA or epidural LA plus opioid; paracetamol, NSAID (if renal function normal), antiemetic
- Laparoscopic: wounds infiltrated; IV morphine or PCA for 12-24 hours, or oral paracetamol, NSAID and opioid

## Nephrectomy and Wilms' tumour
- Wilms' tumour (nephroblastoma): commonest childhood renal tumour; seventh commonest childhood cancer, about 5% of childhood cancers
- Usually a painless abdominal mass noticed by parents
- May need anaesthesia for resection, MRI, bone marrow biopsy, lumbar puncture, central line (PICC) insertion or radiotherapy; usually comes for resection and staging
- Nephrectomy or heminephrectomy removes all or part of a non-functioning kidney: multicystic dysplastic kidney, renal dysplasia, severe reflux nephropathy, severe obstructive uropathy, Wilms' tumour
- Wilms' resection: bleeding is a major issue (local invasion or IVC extension); crossmatch blood

## Temporary urinary diversion
- In infants, prolonged percutaneous drainage has a 40% complication rate (unlike adults)
- So tubeless diversion (cutaneous pyelostomy, ureterostomy or vesicostomy) is considered if reconstruction is delayed beyond 6 months
- Cutaneous pyelostomy or ureterostomy: infected obstructed ureters, bladder hypoplasia or agenesis
- Cutaneous vesicostomy: decompresses bladder and upper tracts in bladder outlet obstruction

## Cystoscopy
- Commonest diagnostic procedure under GA in children
- Short; LMA with spontaneous ventilation beyond infancy
- Ensure deep anaesthesia and analgesia before insertion: urethral stimulation can cause laryngospasm
- Warm irrigation fluid to prevent hypothermia in small children
- Careful lithotomy positioning to avoid nerve and hip injury
- LA gel to the urethra helps postoperative pain

## Urolithiasis: ESWL, ureteroscopy, PCNL
- Often recurrent: look for metabolic causes (e.g. hypercalcaemia)
- Most renal stones treated with extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL)
- Endoscopic treatment for ureteric and bladder stones
- Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) possible for renal stones
- A few large complex stones need open surgery
- ESWL in children: same technique as adults but usually under GA, rarely sedation
- High-pressure irrigation in ureteroscopy and PCNL causes vomiting: give prophylactic antiemetics

## Bladder exstrophy
- One of the hardest paediatric urology conditions; part of the exstrophy-epispadias complex (from cloacal exstrophy to isolated epispadias)
- Defect of lower abdominal wall and anterior bladder wall; posterior bladder wall everted; variable pelvic diastasis
- Aims: close the bladder, achieve continence, preserve renal function, good genital appearance and function
- Single-stage closure (bladder and epispadias) or planned staged repair; pelvic osteotomies often used to bring the pubic bones together
- Multiple procedures in the first year of life; prone to latex allergy
- Anaesthesia: primary repair takes several hours with potentially large fluid and blood loss in a newborn
  - Good venous access and invasive arterial monitoring recommended
  - Postoperative intensive care needed

## Abdominal surgery and laparotomy
- Underlying disease often drives anaesthetic management
- Postoperative pain can be managed with a caudal

## Laparoscopy
- Diagnostic uses: acute abdomen, impalpable testes, recurrent abdominal pain, blunt abdominal trauma, tumour staging and biopsy, intersex anomalies, malfunctioning VP shunt catheters
- Therapeutic uses: appendicectomy, fundoplication, orchidopexy, splenectomy, pyloromyotomy, inguinal hernia repair, nephrectomy and heminephrectomy, pyeloplasty, cholecystectomy, oesophageal atresia (thoracoscopic), Hirschsprung pull-through, adhesiolysis, oophorectomy and ovarian cystectomy, adrenalectomy, varicocele, portoenterostomy, congenital diaphragmatic hernia repair
- Benefits: better cosmesis, less pain, fewer wound problems, faster recovery
- First trocar placed open; the rest under vision to avoid visceral or vascular injury
- CO2 used: does not support combustion and causes little harm if embolised
- Child's abdominal wall is more pliable: good views at lower intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) than adults
- Procedures may be long

### Physiology of pneumoperitoneum (usually benign)
- Respiratory
  - Raised IAP pushes the diaphragm up and reduces FRC
  - FRC below closing volume leads to atelectasis, V/Q mismatch and hypoxia; compliance falls, airway resistance rises
  - Neonates and infants (high closing volume and oxygen consumption) desaturate more readily
  - Trendelenburg worsens FRC loss; reverse Trendelenburg (upper abdominal surgery) may improve compliance
- Insufflation can cause bradycardia
- Cardiovascular (from IAP, CO2 absorption and position)
  - IAP below right atrial pressure (<10 mmHg): splanchnic compression increases venous return and cardiac output
  - IAP >15 mmHg: IVC compression reduces venous return and cardiac output; worse with hypovolaemia and head-up position
  - SVR rises from aortic compression and hypercarbia
- Insufflation pressure <12 mmHg keeps cardiorespiratory changes acceptable
- ICP
  - Rises reported in children with VP shunts even at low IAP and normal arterial pCO2
  - Hypercapnia raises cerebral blood flow and ICP; head-down and raised IAP add to this
  - With falling cardiac output and rising intrathoracic pressure, cerebral perfusion pressure may fall

### Laparoscopy: preoperative assessment
- Ranges from elective healthy child to emergency laparoscopy in a premature neonate
- History, examination and tests as needed
- Comorbidities, e.g. sickle cell disease (gallstones for cholecystectomy), cerebral palsy (fundoplication)
- Group and save for major laparoscopic procedures

### Laparoscopy: anaesthetic technique
- Anticholinergic premedication can prevent insufflation or traction bradycardia: glycopyrronium 100 mcg/kg orally or atropine 40 mcg/kg orally, 60-90 min before; or give IV as needed
- Standard IV or inhalational induction; RSI when indicated
- ETT with controlled ventilation usual
- Cuffed tube preferred; if uncuffed, choose a size with only a small leak at 20 cmH2O so ventilation holds as peak pressure rises
- Monitor exhaled tidal volume and peak pressure during insufflation
- Hypoxia usually corrected with extra oxygen and moderate PEEP
- Endobronchial intubation more likely (carina moves up with raised IAP and head-down): suspect if airway pressure rises and SpO2 falls on insufflation or repositioning
- Nasogastric tube decompresses the stomach for views and safer trocar entry
- Avoid nitrous oxide: bowel distension (PONV, harder surgery) and enlarges gas emboli
- Remifentanil (0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/minute) often used instead to blunt the pneumoperitoneum response
- Multimodal analgesia
  - LA infiltration of port sites before insertion
  - IV or rectal paracetamol and rectal diclofenac if no contraindication
  - With remifentanil, morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV near the end
  - Titrate opioid after adequate spontaneous breathing in neurologically or respiratory compromised children
  - Prophylactic antiemetic
- Lighting may be poor; space and access limited

### Laparoscopy: postoperative care
- Evacuate CO2 at the end to reduce pain, PONV and diaphragmatic splinting
- Early pain can be severe but falls sharply over 24 hours
- Many: oral paracetamol, NSAID and opioid for 24-48 hours
- Major surgery, peritonism or ileus: IV morphine or PCA for 12-24 hours, plus maintenance fluids

## Fundoplication
- Gastro-oesophageal reflux (GOR) from lower oesophageal sphincter failure; normal in the first year of life
- Later symptomatic GOR needs treatment: vomiting, dysphagia, failure to thrive, apnoea, chronic cough, recurrent chest infections, stridor
- Common in children with poor muscle coordination (cerebral palsy, severe developmental delay)
- Diagnosis: history, barium swallow, endoscopy with biopsy, oesophageal pH study
- First line: upright positioning, feed thickeners, prokinetics, antacids
- Surgery if medical treatment fails or to avoid lifelong drugs
- Fundoplication success 80-90%; Nissen 360° wrap commonest
- Usually improves reflux-related respiratory symptoms
- Open upper abdominal incision or laparoscopic

### Fundoplication: preoperative assessment
- Most have severe cerebral palsy
  - Reflux with or without aspiration, poor airway reflexes, recurrent chest infections, epilepsy, contractures, poor nutrition, chronic anaemia, poor venous access
  - Continue usual drugs where possible (anticonvulsants, prokinetics, antacids)
  - Often drool and struggle to swallow saliva; may have a hyoscine patch behind the ear
- Optimise respiratory function: chest physiotherapy, bronchodilators
- FBC and U&E often (poor nutrition, possible gastric bleeding); group and save
  - May be omitted in stable patients for laparoscopic surgery by an experienced surgeon (sampling is hard and distressing)
- Open surgery: discuss analgesia and chance of postoperative ventilation with parents
  - Thoracic epidural infusion usually best; cuts ITU admission and ventilation from 15-20% to 5-10%, though still likely in very impaired children
  - ITU and ventilation very rare after laparoscopic fundoplication

### Fundoplication: anaesthetic technique
- Premedication may be sedative, antisialagogue, antacid or prokinetic
- RSI often considered for reflux, but often impractical (hard IV access, poor preoxygenation, frailty)
- IV induction if veins easy; otherwise inhalational induction is often unavoidable, with cannulation after induction
- Paralyse and intubate, with or without cricoid pressure
- Venous access often a major problem
  - Open surgery needs access for 3-5 days: central line often placed
  - Shorter after laparoscopy; most drink the day after
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide; no nitrous oxide for laparoscopic cases
- Remifentanil (0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min) reduces volatile need and stabilises the circulation; or morphine 100-150 mcg/kg or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg, repeated as needed
- Antiemetic (e.g. ondansetron 100 mcg/kg, max 4mg IV) reduces early retching against the wrap
- Protect contractures, pad pressure areas, protect fragile skin
- Cerebral palsy: prone to hypothermia; warming mattress and forced-air warmer
- Epidural best placed mid-thoracic; scoliosis is common in cerebral palsy, so the epidural space may not be found reliably
- Laparoscopic stimulation is low except at incision, crural dissection and bougie passage; with head-up tilt this may cause hypotension (reduce volatile or remifentanil, or give fluid)

### Fundoplication: postoperative care
- Maintenance fluids; FBC and U&E after open surgery; restart anticonvulsants as soon as possible
- Open: high analgesic needs for 2-4 days and HDU or ITU care
  - Thoracic epidural: good analgesia, preserves respiratory function, avoids oversedation, allows physiotherapy
  - IV morphine works but large doses over days cause oversedation or respiratory depression, especially with neurological or respiratory disease
  - Plus paracetamol, NSAID (if no contraindication) and antiemetic
- Laparoscopic: much less analgesia and shorter stay
  - LA infiltration, then IV morphine for 12-24 hours (or regular oral opioid) plus paracetamol, NSAID and antiemetic
  - Usually eating on day 1; general surgical ward

## Flashcards
- Q: What caudal volume is needed to reach T12 for infant hernia repair?
  - A: 1 mL/kg of 0.25% levobupivacaine or similar (Armitage formula), in children up to 20 kg.
- Q: Which ex-premature infants need overnight apnoea monitoring after hernia repair?
  - A: Those up to 48-60 weeks' postconceptual age; pulse oximetry and apnoea monitoring for 12 hours.
- Q: What is the penile block dose for an older boy?
  - A: 0.1 mL/kg of 0.25% bupivacaine or similar each side.
- Q: How does testicular salvage relate to time in torsion?
  - A: 80% within 6 hours, under 25% after 18 hours.
- Q: Above what intra-abdominal pressure does IVC compression reduce cardiac output in children?
  - A: Above 15 mmHg; keep insufflation below 12 mmHg.
