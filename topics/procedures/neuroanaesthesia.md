# Neuroanaesthesia Summary

Key goals: tight blood pressure control, neuroprotection, control of ICP and prompt emergence for neurological assessment.

## Summary
- Blood pressure control
- Neuroprotection
- Avoid raised ICP
- Short acting drugs for early neurological assessment

## Conduct
- Preoperative: document neurological function; group and crossmatch
- Position: head turned away from the anaesthetist
- Monitoring and lines: arterial line often used for blood pressure
- Technique: GA
  - Airway: usually ETT; ensure adequate depth before intubation
- Maintenance: sevoflurane or TIVA
  - Antibiotic: cefazolin
  - Fluid: isotonic Sterofundin or Plasma-Lyte
  - Special drugs: levetiracetam (Keppra) and mannitol may be wanted
  - Analgesia: fentanyl (short acting)
- Postoperative: neurosurgical high dependency care is common

## Goals
- Tight blood pressure control
- Neuroprotection
  - Optimise the balance of cerebral blood flow (CBF) and cerebral metabolic rate (CMR)
    - Haemodynamic stability and maintained CPP
    - Control of ICP
    - Avoid hypoxia; aim for normocapnia
  - Normoglycaemia and electrolyte balance
- Control ICP and give brain relaxation for good operating conditions
- Prompt emergence for neurological assessment
- Immobility

## Blood pressure control
- Probably one of the most important ways to prevent brain injury
  - Maintains cerebral perfusion pressure: keep haemodynamics stable, aim CPP 60-70mmHg
  - A rise in blood pressure during aneurysm clipping raises pressure inside the aneurysm and the risk of rupture with handling
  - Keep BP within 10-20% of baseline
- Targets depend on the pathology
  - Ischaemic stroke: SBP <220 if thrombolysis contraindicated; <180 if thrombolysis given
  - Haemorrhagic stroke (depends on SAH or ICH)
    - SAH: SBP <160 until the aneurysm is secured
    - Other ICH: SBP <140 (INTERACT 2 trial)
  - TBI (including traumatic SAH): SBP > 100
    - With an ICP monitor, aim CPP >60-70
- Arterial line, with or without a central venous catheter, to monitor and control blood pressure
  - Arterial line also allows frequent blood sampling
- Drugs on standby
  - Hypotension: phenylephrine, ephedrine, noradrenaline, adrenaline
  - Hypertension
    - Esmolol
    - Labetalol 5-10 mg IV
    - GTN
    - Nicardipine 1-2 mg IV

## Neuroprotection
- Maintain normotension, normoxia, normocarbia, normothermia and normoglycaemia
- Temperature
  - Avoid hyperthermia
  - Mild intraoperative hypothermia gives no proven benefit in elective neurosurgery; aim for normothermia with air and fluid warmers
- Glucose
  - Hyperglycaemia greatly worsens injury in both global and focal ischaemia; even moderate rises worsen outcome
  - In incomplete ischaemia, glucose supply without enough oxygen drives anaerobic metabolism and brain lactic acid rises
- Seizure prophylaxis if the surgeons request it

## Brain relaxation and ICP control
- Achieve brain relaxation before the dura is opened
- Position
  - Head up 15-20 degrees to help venous drainage
  - Neck neutral; avoid excessive head rotation
  - Avoid ETT ties; tape the tube instead
- Adequate anaesthetic depth, analgesia and paralysis; avoid agitation on emergence
- Ventilation
  - PaCO2 30-35 mm Hg during surgery
  - Normoxia, normoglycaemia, normothermia (core temperature 36-37°C)
  - Avoid PEEP > 10 cm H2O, as higher levels impair venous drainage and raise ICP
- Osmotherapy: mannitol or hypertonic saline 3%
  - Mannitol (0.25-1 g/kg) relaxes vascular smooth muscle and briefly raises ICP, so give it slowly (over ~ 10 mins); can cause hypotension
- Alternative in cardiac disease: furosemide 10-20mg instead of mannitol or hypertonic saline, as these patients may not tolerate a volume load
- Dexamethasone 8mg for tumours, if indicated

## Drugs

### TIVA versus volatile
- Volatiles (isoflurane, sevoflurane): keep 0.6-0.8MAC; stay below 1 MAC to avoid raising ICP
- Propofol TIVA is often preferred for intracranial surgery
  - Thought to keep cerebral flow-metabolism coupling and CO2 reactivity intact, so less vasodilation and ICP rise
  - Reduces cerebral metabolism in a dose dependent way, like barbiturates; can produce an isoelectric EEG at clinical doses
- Barbiturates: gold standard for cerebral protection
  - EEG activity falls until isoelectric; energy no longer used for EEG activity is left for basal neuronal needs
- Etomidate: progressively lowers cerebral metabolism to an isoelectric EEG
  - Unlike barbiturates and propofol, has little effect on blood pressure, so CPP is preserved
- Benzodiazepines cannot fully suppress the EEG, so are not used for cerebral protection

### Opioids
- Mainstay of neuroanaesthesia: little effect on cerebral metabolism and blood flow, while supporting the circulation and CPP
- Fentanyl
- Remifentanil
  - Rapidly titratable analgesia
  - Smooth, haemodynamically stable extubation
- Avoid morphine

### Muscle relaxants
- Most prefer a non-depolarising NMBD over suxamethonium

### Drugs to avoid
- Nitrous oxide: cerebral vasodilation increases CBF and ICP; expands air (venous air embolism, pneumocephalus)
- Ketamine: controversial; increases cerebral metabolism, blood flow and ICP; may cause emergence delirium
- Morphine: respiratory depression; hampers postoperative neurological monitoring
- Benzodiazepines: use sparingly to allow rapid emergence and neurological assessment

## Fluids
- Fluid of choice: 0.9% saline or Sterofundin without dextrose
  - Watch for hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis; check ABGs after several litres of saline
- Avoid hypotonic fluids (e.g. Hartmann's): worsen cerebral oedema and raise ICP
- Avoid glucose-containing fluids: worsen ischaemia and oedema
- Alternatives: Plasma-Lyte, Sterofundin
- Fluid restriction is no longer used for raised ICP: only moderately effective, and causes hypotension, cerebral and renal hypoperfusion, and electrolyte and acid-base problems

## Other monitoring
- Patients may arrive with neurocritical care monitors: external ventricular drain (EVD), jugular venous saturation (SjVO2) catheter, or brain tissue oxygen probe
- Continue these through the perioperative period where possible

## Flashcards
- Q: What PaCO2 is targeted during neurosurgery?
  - A: 30-35 mm Hg.
- Q: What is the dose of mannitol and how should it be given?
  - A: 0.25-1 g/kg, slowly over about 10 mins, as it briefly raises ICP and can cause hypotension.
- Q: What SBP target applies in SAH before the aneurysm is secured?
  - A: SBP <160.
- Q: What MAC of volatile is used in neuroanaesthesia?
  - A: 0.6-0.8 MAC, staying below 1 MAC to avoid raising ICP.
- Q: What alternative to mannitol suits patients with cardiac disease?
  - A: Furosemide 10-20mg, avoiding a volume load.
