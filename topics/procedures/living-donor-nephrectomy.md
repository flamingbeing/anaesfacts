# Living Kidney Donor Nephrectomy

Healthy altruistic donor: protect the kidneys and keep discomfort and pain to a minimum (use regional blocks).

## Key considerations
- Renal protection
- Donor is altruistic: minimise discomfort and pain; use blocks

## Preoperative assessment
- Most donors are ASA 1 and 2
- ABO match is usually needed, but with a living donor, time and immunosuppressant treatment can allow donation across ABO groups
- Hypertension is no longer a contraindication if creatinine is normal and there is no proteinuria
- Diabetes mellitus is a contraindication to donation

## Intraoperative management
- No routine prophylactic antibiotics
- Prophylactic LMWH
- GA with epidural, paravertebral block or wound catheters
- Avoid NSAIDs
- Fluid load to reduce the need for vasopressors
- Heparin before arterial clamping, then protamine after the kidney is isolated
- Aim for high-normal urine output

## Postoperative care
- Fentanyl PCA
- Complications:
  - Usual surgical complications
  - Transient rise in creatinine, usually back to normal within 1 month

## Long-term outcome
- Removing one kidney from an otherwise healthy donor does not affect mortality
- The remaining kidney hypertrophies; long-term renal function settles at about 75%

## Flashcards
- Q: Is hypertension a contraindication to living kidney donation?
  - A: No, provided creatinine is normal and there is no proteinuria
- Q: Which common condition does rule out kidney donation?
  - A: Diabetes mellitus
- Q: How is anticoagulation handled around renal artery clamping in donor nephrectomy?
  - A: Heparin before arterial clamping, protamine after the kidney is isolated
- Q: Which analgesics are avoided in a kidney donor?
  - A: NSAIDs
- Q: What is long-term renal function after donor nephrectomy?
  - A: About 75%, as the remaining kidney hypertrophies; creatinine rise usually settles within 1 month
