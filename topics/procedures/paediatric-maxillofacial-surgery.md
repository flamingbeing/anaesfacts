# Paediatric Maxillofacial Surgery

Children's facial trauma and reconstructive surgery: main problems are the difficult airway, temperature control and blood loss.

## General considerations
- Wide range of congenital and acquired conditions, all ages
- Airway and intubation problems common
- Procedures in infants can be complex and long
- Main problems: difficult airway, temperature control, blood loss
- Many children need repeated operations and anaesthetics over years; possible psychological harm and self-image concerns
- Usually done in centralised multidisciplinary units; families may travel far and spend long periods away from home

## Facial trauma
- About 70% soft tissue injury, 25% dental trauma, 5% facial fractures
- Commonest fracture sites: nasal bones and mandible, then midface
- Nasal fracture manipulation usually elective, 2-5 days after injury
- Condylar fractures are 60% of paediatric mandibular fractures
  - Injury to the condylar growth centre (beneath the articular disc) can slow growth of that side of the jaw
  - Most managed conservatively
- Le Fort midface fractures
  - I: palate and alveolus separated from the rest of the maxilla
  - II: midface separated from the skull as a free pyramidal segment, including a mobile palate
  - III: whole face separated from the cranial base (maxilla, palate, zygoma, ethmoid)
  - Signal major trauma; associated injuries are common

## Preoperative assessment
- Fracture repair waits until after the primary survey, resuscitation and more urgent injuries
- Exclude cervical spine injury
- If not urgent: fast the child and operate on a daytime trauma list
- Mandibular fractures: usually gum tears and haematomas on the buccal and lingual sides inside the mouth
- Bilateral condylar fractures: mandible displaced backwards; occasionally obstructs the airway
- Airway obstruction risks: mobile fragments, oedema, bleeding, vomit, bone fragments, foreign bodies
- Document loose teeth

## Anaesthetic technique
- Inhalational induction if facial injuries may threaten the airway
- Nasal fracture manipulation: brief; LMA with spontaneous ventilation
- Other fractures: complex, long surgery
  - Secure airway with ETT, often armoured; or north- or south-facing ETT depending on injury site and surgery
  - Throat pack if airway soiling possible
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide; IPPV is usual
- Analgesia
  - Morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV, or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV
  - Or remifentanil infusion 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min: cardiovascular stability, less volatile needed; give a morphine bolus before the end
  - Surgeon LA infiltration or nerve blocks helpful
  - NSAID plus paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV or 40 mg/kg rectal intraoperatively
- Monitor temperature; warming mattress
- Fluids: fasting deficit and maintenance as crystalloid (e.g. Hartmann's)
- Blood loss usually small unless an artery is injured; replace with colloid or crystalloid

## Postoperative care
- Ward experienced with facial injuries
- HDU if significant oral or facial swelling could threaten the airway
- ITU with ongoing intubation if airway patency is in doubt; occasionally tracheostomy until obstruction risk passes
- IV maintenance fluids until drinking
- Analgesia by extent of surgery; usually enough:
  - Paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly
  - Ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly
  - Oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly
- Sometimes IV morphine infusion or PCA (other injuries, or unable to take oral drugs)

## Elective facial reconstructive surgery
- Corrects congenital or developmental deformity, e.g. mandibular hypoplasia
- Congenital syndromes (e.g. Treacher Collins) more common than in adults, where deformity is mostly acquired
- Difficult airway from limited mouth opening or mandibular hypoplasia
  - Micrognathia is the main cause of difficult intubation in children: mask airway often fine, intubation often hard
  - The underlying syndrome hints at likely airway difficulty
  - Prefer spontaneous ventilation techniques if the airway looks difficult
- Blood loss: large losses matter more given children's small blood volumes

## Flashcards
- Q: What is the main cause of difficult intubation in children having facial reconstruction?
  - A: Micrognathia.
- Q: What remifentanil infusion rate is used for paediatric facial fracture surgery?
  - A: 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min, with a morphine bolus before the end.
- Q: What proportion of paediatric mandibular fractures are condylar?
  - A: 60%.
- Q: How is a paediatric nasal fracture manipulation usually managed?
  - A: Elective, 2-5 days after injury, with an LMA and spontaneous ventilation.
- Q: What is the paediatric paracetamol dose for facial surgery?
  - A: 15 mg/kg IV or oral (4-hourly), or 40 mg/kg rectal.
