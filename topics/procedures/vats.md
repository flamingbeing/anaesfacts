# Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery (VATS)

Thoracoscopic chest surgery through small intercostal incisions: needs one-lung ventilation (usually DLT), lateral position with the table broken, and a block for analgesia.

## Summary

- One-lung ventilation with a DLT; CXR afterwards
- Lateral, table broken
- Analgesia: intercostal block or ESP block

## Overview

- Thoracoscope passed through intercostal incisions to inspect and operate in the chest
- Position: lateral with the table broken
- Antibiotics: cefazolin
- Approach: minimally invasive
- Duration depends on procedure
  - Thymectomy: 1-2 hrs
  - Lobectomy: 1-3 hrs
  - Added mediastinal lymph node dissection: 1-2 hrs
- Blood loss: depends on procedure
- Special points
  - One-lung ventilation: DLT, McGrath videolaryngoscope, bronchoscope, TIVA
  - Keep paralysed
  - Cautious fluids
- Complications: hypoxia, hypercarbia
- Pain: less than thoracotomy; options ESP, paravertebral, epidural, intercostal
- Postoperative: CXR in PACU for pneumothorax or haemothorax

## Compared with open thoracotomy

- Less blood loss
- Less postoperative loss of lung function
- Less pain

## Indications

- Lung or pleural biopsy
- Drainage of pleural effusion
- Segmentectomy, lobectomy, pleurectomy, pleurodesis
- Anterior mediastinal mass excision, thymectomy
- Pericardial biopsy or window
- Not limited to lung, pleura and mediastinum: also heart and great vessels, oesophagus, diaphragm, spine and nerves

## Procedure

- Small (2 ± 3 cm) incisions in the lateral chest wall, patient in lateral decubitus
  - Minor procedures (thoracentesis, pleural biopsy) can use one incision; usually two or three more are added for instruments and staplers
- Trocar placed once the lung on that side is collapsed; thoracoscope through the trocar
- Chest drain at the end, then lung re-expanded
- Postoperative CXR
- Uniportal (single-port) VATS: one incision of about 4 cm

## Complications

- About 9% of patients have a complication
- Haemorrhage, surgical emphysema, empyema, recurrent pneumothorax, pulmonary oedema, pneumonia
- Tumour seeding at the drain site
- Persistent air leak is common

## Main considerations

- Surgical
  - One-lung ventilation: lung isolation and management of hypoxaemia
  - The lung must be fully collapsed; poor isolation with partial inflation can make conversion to thoracotomy necessary
  - Preserve postoperative respiratory function: cautious fluids to prevent pulmonary oedema
- Patient
  - Respiratory function and cardiovascular fitness
  - Underlying disease

## Intraoperative management

### Positioning

- Lateral with a small axillary roll
- Foam head sponge with a towel underneath to support the head
- Dependent arm flexed and held by an arm board
- Upper arm flexed and drawn forward on an arm board
- Break the table, then recheck the tube
- Clamps must not touch the face
- Arterial line in the dependent arm and IV in the non-dependent arm (dependent arm is extended, non-dependent flexed)
- Lines run straight, not doubled back
- Tape the tube towards the non-dependent lung; bite block on the dependent side to absorb secretions
- Turn lateral once tube and lines are in; wrists together while turning
- Non-dependent arm on a gel pad on the arm support, shoulder a little flexed forward and at 90 degrees, torso neutral
- Hold the head neutral while the table is broken; towels in while breaking
- Support the tube with an angle board

### Technique

- GA
- Supplementary regional: paravertebral, ESP block, intercostal block, epidural
- Monitoring: arterial line usually; may not be needed for a small procedure (e.g. biopsy)

### Airway and one-lung ventilation

- DLT or bronchial blocker to collapse the lung
  - DLT preferred: faster collapse of the operated lung
  - Opening the operated-side lumen to air and intermittent suction speed collapse further
- Sequence
  - Inflate the tracheal cuff first (5 ml); reduce oxygen flow to 2 L
  - Clamp the proximal tracheal lumen; bronchoscope down the tracheal lumen
  - See the carina and the right main bronchus leading to the right upper lobe bronchus
  - Inflate the bronchial cuff (2 ml); scope the bronchial lumen to check the left upper lobe bronchus is not blocked
  - Turn lateral with the head supported; break the table and readjust head height
  - Rescope through the tracheal lumen to confirm position, then open the operated lung to air to collapse it
  - Watch for desaturation; ventilate with 100% oxygen until one-lung ventilation is established
- Alternative: right after DLT insertion, pass the bronchoscope down the bronchial lumen into the left main bronchus and railroad the DLT to isolate the lung before ventilating

### Drugs and fluids

- Short-acting IV agents for fast emergence and return of airway reflexes
- Cautious fluids to prevent postoperative pulmonary oedema
- Paralysis can continue until the chest drain is going in

## Postoperative management

- CXR in PACU: pneumothorax, haemothorax
- Analgesia
  - PCA possible but usually not needed (except pleurodesis, the most painful)
  - Blocks: ESP, paravertebral, or epidural (bilateral VATS)
  - Intercostal block by the surgeon

## PROSPECT 2021 recommendations for VATS analgesia

- Paracetamol plus NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, started before or during surgery and continued after
- IV dexmedetomidine recommended
  - Lowers pain scores and opioid use
  - Less postoperative agitation, cognitive dysfunction and PONV
  - Better lung function (higher FEV1 and vital capacity)
- Paravertebral block or ESP block recommended, single shot or preferably catheter with continuous local anaesthetic
  - Paravertebral: effective with fewer side effects than thoracic epidural; a catheter prolongs analgesia
  - ESP: non-inferior to paravertebral in two studies, so a reasonable alternative
  - Serratus anterior plane block: second choice
- Not recommended
  - Intercostal block: no procedure-specific evidence
  - Thoracic epidural: less invasive options work as well with fewer side effects
- Opioids for postoperative rescue only

## Robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS)

- Poor access to the patient: extend the breathing circuit and drip
  - The drip may need to reach as far as the machine; keep it away from the patient for access
- Rotate or move the table so the robot clears the ceiling-mounted arms
- No arm supports (they block the robot): towels between the arms and tape the arms down
- Let the drapes fall freely
- CO2 insufflation to compress the non-ventilated lung and improve the view
  - Can cause a picture like tension pneumothorax
  - Higher airway pressure and rising EtCO2
  - Mediastinal shift, haemodynamic instability, falling systolic BP
  - Venous gas embolism
  - Can occur at pressures as low as 5 mmHg
  - Reduced by limiting gas flow to 2 l/min and insufflating slowly

## Flashcards

- Q: Why is a DLT preferred over a bronchial blocker for VATS?
  - A: Faster collapse of the operated lung
- Q: Which analgesic blocks does PROSPECT 2021 recommend for VATS?
  - A: Paravertebral or ESP block, ideally as a catheter infusion; serratus anterior second choice
- Q: Which techniques does PROSPECT 2021 not recommend for VATS?
  - A: Intercostal block and thoracic epidural
- Q: Lowest insufflation pressure that can cause tension-like changes in RATS, and how to reduce risk?
  - A: As low as 5 mmHg; limit gas to 2 l/min and insufflate slowly
- Q: Complication rate after VATS?
  - A: About 9%
