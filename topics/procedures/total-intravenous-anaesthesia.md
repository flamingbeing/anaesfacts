# Total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) and propofol TCI models

Induction and maintenance with IV agents in oxygen/air (nitrous oxide sometimes added), usually propofol by target-controlled infusion (TCI).

## Definition
- Strictly: IV agents for both induction and maintenance, with an oxygen/air mix
- Nitrous oxide is occasionally used as an adjunct

## Advantages
- Key
  - Malignant hyperthermia: volatiles are absolutely contraindicated, so TIVA is used
  - Lower ICP and no flow-metabolism decoupling (neurosurgery)
  - Less PONV (middle ear, strabismus, day surgery)
- Others
  - No bulky vaporisers or MAC monitoring; easier transfer to ICU (remote sites)
  - Preserves hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction; depth not tied to ventilation, so easier in one-lung ventilation (thoracics)
  - Smoother emergence (ENT, eye); less emergence delirium in children
  - Tubeless airway surgery: depth easier to maintain; no theatre pollution or staff exposure to vapour
  - Possibly less bronchospasm and laryngospasm in children
  - Possible benefit in cancer surgery
  - Lower environmental impact (no vapour released to the atmosphere)

## Indications
- Patient factors
  - Severe PONV history
  - Malignant hyperthermia risk
  - Long QT syndrome (QTc ≥ 500 ms)
  - Previous emergence delirium
- Surgical factors
  - Neurophysiological monitoring needed (e.g. spine)
  - Neurosurgery, to limit intracranial volume
  - Thoracic surgery
  - Tubeless airway or ENT surgery
  - Remote anaesthesia; ICU patients already on propofol infusion
  - High PONV-risk surgery (strabismus, middle ear) or day surgery

## Disadvantages and contraindications
- No point-of-delivery measurement equivalent to end-tidal agent monitoring: awareness risk, e.g. with disconnections or leaks
- Caution in long cases or obesity: propofol context-sensitive half-life becomes long
- May be contraindicated in liver surgery, as propofol is cleared by the liver
- Propofol infusion syndrome (PRIS) risk in children with some metabolic disorders (mitochondrial disease, fatty acid oxidation disorders, coenzyme Q deficiency); otherwise rare with TIVA for GA

## Delivery and titration
- Via TCI syringe pump or ordinary syringe pump
- Manual infusion, or a pump running a pharmacokinetic model targeting plasma (Cp) or brain/effect site (Ce)
- Titrate the target to clinical signs and BIS
  - No measured blood level comparable to end-tidal volatile monitoring
  - A given target may not suit every stage of surgery; adjust to effect, as with a vaporiser dial
- AAGBI: choose the starting target by patient characteristics, co-administered drugs and situation
- AAGBI: older, frail or unwell patients may do better with a low initial target and repeated small increments
  - FRCA: elderly have a smaller central volume, lower clearance, greater receptor sensitivity and slower time to peak effect; they lose consciousness at lower concentrations and develop cardiorespiratory compromise at higher ones
  - FRCA: most models are built on healthy volunteers and fit unwell patients poorly
- FRCA: note the estimated Ce at loss of consciousness and at airway manipulation to gauge the patient's sensitivity
- Opioids and other hypnotics are synergistic; propofol with remifentanil is synergistic, so factor in the remifentanil dose
- Unpremedicated patients need a higher target than premedicated ones

## Target concentrations (rough guide)
- Induction
  - Rapid: 4-6 μg/ml
  - Slower: 1-3μg/ml, then titrate up
  - Intubation needs higher levels, depending on opioid use
- Maintenance
  - Generally 2.5−7 µg/ml, varying with surgery and patient
    - Some quote 3-6 ug/ml
  - Lower with remifentanil co-infusion: propofol 2–2.5 µg/ml is common
  - ATOTW: 2-4 ug/ml with high-dose remifentanil, 5-6 ug/ml with low-dose remifentanil
- Emergence
  - Keep propofol Ce at or above 2 μg.ml-1 until the end of the procedure; the infusion can usually stop while dressings go on
  - Waking concentration: 1-2 μg/ml

## Specific situations
- Rapid sequence induction or deepening quickly
  - Set a high initial target so the bolus runs at maximum rate (e.g. 1200 ml/hr), then lower the target once the dose is in
  - A manual bolus makes the pump's displayed plasma estimate inaccurate early on
  - Alternative: induce with thiopentone or etomidate, then maintain with TCI propofol
  - Ketamine can paradoxically raise the pEEG index value
- Changing from volatile to TIVA (e.g. for ICU transfer)
  - Awareness has been reported, mostly from fixed-rate propofol infusions that were too low once the volatile wore off
  - Prefer a TCI pump; raise propofol Cp/Ce stepwise as end-tidal volatile falls, watching clinical signs
- Pump failure or loss of IV access: 3 options
  - Option 1: switch to volatile; safest if volatiles are not contraindicated; do not delay if the fault is not fixed at once
  - Option 2: restart the pump in manual ml/hr mode; if steady state was reached and the rate at a given target was recorded, use that (usually between 30 and 50mls/hr); the Bristol scheme can also guide this
  - Option 3: restart in TCI mode; unsafe, as the pump does not know about drug already at the effect site

## Specific populations
- Elderly, ASA 3 – 5 or haemodynamically compromised: increase the target in small steps to avoid instability
- Children: Schnider accepts only patients >16 yo
- Obesity: titrate to clinical effect with pEEG monitoring
  - Unclear whether total body weight or another scalar (e.g. adjusted body weight, TBW - 20%) is better
  - Above the model's BMI limit, consider entering the maximum allowed weight for the patient's height

## Preventing awareness under TIVA
- AAGBI: use TCI propofol rather than manual infusion when maintaining with propofol
- Infusion set (AAGBI)
  - Luer-lock connector at each end
  - Antisiphon valve on drug line(s); anti-reflux valve on any fluid line
  - TIVA-specific sets recommended
  - Join drug and fluid lines as close to the patient as possible to minimise dead space
- IV cannula
  - FRCA: a dedicated cannula is preferable
  - Check the access runs well
  - Keep the IV arm out and visible (AAGBI: cannula or central line visible throughout where practical)
  - FRCA: watch the site closely for disconnection and tissuing
- AAGBI: use processed EEG (e.g. BIS) when a neuromuscular blocker is used with TIVA; risk is high with concurrent NMBs
- Others
  - Equipment: pumps programmed correctly, alarms on, on mains power; extra vigilance in obesity
  - Drugs: stock only one propofol concentration
  - People: training on the pumps

## Combined intravenous volatile anaesthesia (CIVA)
- Aim: combine the benefits of both
- Adding propofol to volatile may
  - Reduce PONV
  - Smooth awakening
- Adding volatile to TIVA may
  - Reduce intraoperative movement
  - Give cardioprotection
  - 0.3 to 0.5 MAC volatile may lower awareness risk
  - Lower risk of the anaesthetic stopping for mechanical reasons (kinked or tissued cannula or tubing)
  - 0.5 MAC volatile allows a smaller propofol dose, so faster waking and earlier neurological examination
- Two elimination routes (lungs and liver) may speed emergence
- Problem: harder to judge depth
  - NAP5: awareness reports with TIVA often involved mixed IV and volatile techniques, sequential or concurrent
- Sequential use: some swap between TIVA and volatile in long cases to limit build-up of either and speed emergence

## Manual TIVA: Bristol model (10-8-6)
- Aims for blood propofol about 3 µg/ml within 2 minutes, then holds it through surgery
- Derived from patients premedicated with temazepam, given fentanyl 3 µg/kg, then propofol bolus 1 mg/kg
- Infusion: 10 mg/kg/h for 10 minutes, 8 mg/kg/h for 10 minutes, then 6 mg/kg/h
- Patients also had nitrous oxide and were ventilated
- If a higher level is needed, adjust the scheme or add a supplement such as remifentanil infusion
- TCI uses less total propofol than manual infusion, with better haemodynamic stability and faster waking

## Target-controlled infusion principles
- Microprocessor pump running a three-compartment pharmacokinetic model
- Three superimposed infusions: one constant (replaces elimination) and two exponentially falling (match distribution to peripheral compartments)
- Targets are calculated, not measured
  - Real levels vary between patients; displayed plasma level is usually within 25% of actual
- Induction: rapid bolus up to 1200 ml/hr (or 20ml/min) to fill the central compartment
  - Uptitrate Ce slowly in haemodynamically unstable patients
  - Some newer pumps bolus at 1800–2200 ml.h
- Then a much lower variable rate matching exponential transfer and uptake
- Raising the target: pump gives a small automatic bolus
- Lowering the target: infusion pauses until the level falls, then restarts at a lower rate

## Decrement time
- Some pumps display predicted time for plasma level to fall to a set value (default 1.2 µg/ml), when waking is expected
- Can be longer with other hypnotics (especially opioids) or liver impairment

## Compartments and effect site
- All models are 3-compartment
  - V1 (central): brain, heart, liver
  - V2 (rapid peripheral): muscle
  - V3 (slow peripheral): fat
- The effect site is separate from the three compartments
  - Plasma-to-brain transfer is first order, rate constant ke0
  - Hence a lag to time to peak effect (Tpeak)
- Effect-site targeting aims to equilibrate plasma and effect site as fast as possible
  - Plasma level briefly overshoots to steepen the gradient
  - Infusion then pauses, plasma falls with a small undershoot, then a slower infusion resumes

## Adult models
- Marsh (1991)
  - Compartment volumes scaled linearly to total body weight only; age is entered but not used
    - Large induction bolus, especially in morbid obesity (large predicted V1)
    - Alternative: enter lean body weight
  - Fixed rate constants, unaffected by age, sex or height
  - Plasma targeting: lag before Ce equilibrates, so slower induction and depth changes (time to peak effect 4.5 min)
    - Modified Marsh targets the effect site, time to peak effect 1.6min (similar to Schnider)
  - Limits: weight 150kg; patient over 16
  - Best for healthy average adults; simple, quick induction
- Schnider (1993), 24 volunteers
  - Overshoots plasma level to raise Ce fast (time to peak effect 1.6min), mimicking an IV induction
  - Larger bolus than Marsh but less total drug, less overshoot and undershoot, so faster recovery
  - Fixed V1 (Marsh scales V1 linearly)
  - Needs sex, age, height and total body weight; pump calculates lean body weight (James equation)
    - Uses more covariates, so recommended for elderly and less robust patients (BJA Education)
  - Limits: BMI < 35 for females or < 42 for males (accurate up to BMI 42 in men and 37 in women; beyond that lean body mass paradoxically falls)
  - Best for frail or lean patients, e.g. elderly
- Eleveld (2018): over 10,000 individuals, 30 studies
  - Continuous covariate adjustment for age, sex, height, weight and fat-free mass
    - Elderly: smaller central volume and clearance
    - Children: maturation
    - Morbid obesity (BMI 50+): lean mass and volume of distribution
    - Retrospectively performs as well as or better than dedicated population models
  - Obesity: allometric (not linear) scaling of both volume and clearance
    - Marsh scales linearly to total body weight (overdose risk); Schnider relies on fixed lean body mass equations (inaccurate at extremes)
    - Only 3 individuals with BMI >50 were included
  - ke0 falls with obesity and age

## Obesity
- Use Eleveld (weights 680g to 160kg)
- Titrate to BIS

## Paediatric models
- Children have a larger central volume and faster clearance than adults
- Paedfusor: Marsh-based, for 1–16 yr
  - Weight 5 kg - 61 kg; age 1 - 16 years
- Kataria
  - Weight 15kg - 61 kg; age 3 - 16 years
- Eleveld
  - Ages 27 weeks postmenstrual age up to 88 years; weights 680g to 160kg
- Children >61kg under 16 may be underdosed by an adult model
  - Options: paediatric model with a fictitious weight, or adult model with a fictitious age
  - Post-pubertal children >61kg can generally use adult models
- Fewer parameter estimates are published for neonates and infants >1yo

## Non-propofol models
- Minto: remifentanil TCI only

## Flashcards
- Q: What does the Bristol 10-8-6 scheme deliver after a 1 mg/kg bolus?
  - A: 10 mg/kg/h for 10 min, 8 mg/kg/h for 10 min, then 6 mg/kg/h, aiming for about 3 µg/ml
- Q: Typical propofol maintenance target, and with remifentanil?
  - A: 2.5-7 µg/ml; as low as 2-2.5 µg/ml with remifentanil
- Q: Time to peak effect with Marsh (plasma) vs Schnider?
  - A: 4.5 min vs 1.6 min
- Q: Weight and age ranges for Paedfusor and Kataria?
  - A: Paedfusor 5-61 kg, 1-16 years; Kataria 15-61 kg, 3-16 years
- Q: When does AAGBI recommend processed EEG with TIVA?
  - A: Whenever a neuromuscular blocker is used
