# Dental Surgery and Tooth Extraction

Shared-airway day surgery, usually with nasal tube or flexible LMA, throat pack and surgeon-given local anaesthetic.

## Key points
- Shared airway: nasal intubation or flexible LMA
- PONV risk; throat pack
- Local anaesthetic given by surgeons
- Infective endocarditis prophylaxis when indicated

## Types of surgery
- Simple extractions
- Oral rehabilitation
- Dental abscess (managed as for oral and maxillofacial infections)

## Common considerations
- Supine; table position:
  - Adults: head towards the anaesthetic machine
    - Secure tube and circuit well (especially the ETCO2 line)
  - Children: table orientation varies by unit
- Antibiotics: +/- Augmentin; not all need it
  - Endocarditis prophylaxis if prosthetic heart valve or certain congenital heart disease
- Airway: often nasal tube (north-facing RAE), but flexible LMA or oral tube possible; agree with the surgeon
  - Nasal preparation: ask the patient which side; co-phenylcaine; Ivory, armoured or north-facing RAE tube
  - Throat pack: must be removed; use sticker reminders
    - Less blood reaches the stomach, so less PONV (swallowed blood is very emetogenic)
  - Protect eyes with tape, pads and lubricant
- Intraoperative:
  - Rubber dam can be stimulating
  - Higher risk of arrhythmias from trigeminal nerve stimulation

## Analgesia
- Local anaesthetic works well; fentanyl alone is often enough, morphine also an option
  - E.g. inferior alveolar nerve block, local infiltration
- Lidocaine 2% with adrenaline 1:80,000: lasts about 90 min; max dose 7 mg/kg, roughly 10 x 1.8 ml cartridges
  - Watch for hypertension and tachycardia from adrenaline
- Bupivacaine 0.25-0.5% plain: up to 8 h of anaesthesia

## Day surgery
- PONV risk (swallowed blood)
- Must meet day surgery criteria; avoid late-finishing lists

## Procedures under local anaesthesia or sedation
- Most done under local anaesthesia without an anaesthetist
- Sedation only for patients who will not tolerate local alone (see GA indications)
  - Oral or IV sedation
  - Inhaled nitrous oxide by nasal mask (relative analgesia)
- Sedation safety: patient monitored by trained staff other than the dentist
- Nitrous oxide regimen:
  - 10% in oxygen for 1 min
  - 20% for 1 min
  - Then increase in 5% steps to a maximum of 50% until sedated
  - 100% oxygen at the end

## Dental extractions under general anaesthesia
- Ranges from simple wisdom tooth extraction to extensive oral rehabilitation
- Usually with local anaesthetic as above
- Indications for GA:
  - High-volume work (e.g. extraction of permanent molars)
  - Uncooperative patients (children, learning disability)
  - Dental phobia

### Preoperative assessment
- Premedication helps in anxious or uncooperative patients (often the reason they need oral rehabilitation)
- Syndromic patients may have complex medical problems, e.g. Down syndrome

### Airway
- Wisdom tooth extraction can be done with a flexible LMA
  - Shared airway: check it sits well and tape it securely
  - Insert a bite block before the patient leaves theatre

### Maintenance
- Antibiotics optional (usually Augmentin)
- Long-acting opioids not needed (local anaesthetic given)

### Postoperative care
- Bleeding: gauze packs on sockets; tape the string down with eye tape
- Pain usually minimal if local anaesthetic works
- Day surgery discharge criteria must be met

## Flashcards
- Q: Maximum dose and duration of lidocaine 2% with adrenaline 1:80,000 in dental work?
  - A: 7 mg/kg (about 10 x 1.8 ml cartridges); lasts about 90 min
- Q: Nitrous oxide sedation regimen for dental work?
  - A: 10% for 1 min, 20% for 1 min, then 5% steps to max 50%; 100% oxygen at the end
- Q: Why does a throat pack reduce PONV?
  - A: Less swallowed blood, which is highly emetogenic
- Q: Why are arrhythmias more likely during dental surgery?
  - A: Trigeminal nerve stimulation
- Q: Three indications for dental extraction under GA?
  - A: High-volume work, uncooperative patients, dental phobia
