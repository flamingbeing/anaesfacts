# Adult Cardiac Surgery with Cardiopulmonary Bypass

Anaesthesia for on-pump heart surgery: manage the underlying cardiac lesion, TEE and invasive lines, protect heart and brain on bypass, and control bleeding.

## Key issues
- Underlying cardiac condition
- TEE monitoring and invasive lines
- Heart and brain protection on bypass
- Bleeding risk

## Preoperative assessment
- Cardiorespiratory assessment
- Clotting assessment
- Diabetes: likely to need insulin during bypass
- Chronic kidney disease
- Neurological disease: may need near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS)
- Oesophageal problems: may contraindicate TEE

## Investigations
- FBC, PT/APTT
  - Follow up every abnormal clotting result
  - A falling platelet count on heparin may point to heparin-induced thrombocytopenia
- Group and crossmatch; blood ready in the fridge
- Cardiac investigations
  - TTE
    - LV function: ejection fraction, LVEDP, pulmonary artery pressures; poor function may need adrenaline, IABP or ECMO
    - RV function and PASP: may need RV support (milrinone, inhaled nitric oxide) and a PA catheter
    - Valve lesions: set the haemodynamic goals
  - Coronary angiogram
- CT thorax
- Carotid ultrasound

## Counselling
- Risks of TEE
- Awareness

## Risk stratification
- Risk factors fall into three groups
  - Patient: age, sex, lung disease, renal impairment, extracardiac arteriopathy, poor mobility, previous cardiac surgery, active endocarditis, critical preoperative state, diabetes on insulin
  - Cardiac: functional class, recent MI, LV function, pulmonary hypertension
  - Operative: urgency, size of the procedure, thoracic aortic surgery
- Scores: EuroSCORE II (European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation), STS score (Society of Thoracic Surgeons)

## Monitoring, lines and equipment
- Large-bore 16G IV cannula on the right
- Arterial line on the left before induction
- CVC in the right internal jugular, before or after induction depending on disease severity
- Consider a PA catheter (pull it back before bypass to avoid PA rupture)
- 5-lead ECG
- Urinary catheter
- Temperature probes x2 (nasal and rectal)
- Orogastric tube to aspirate and help TEE probe passage
- Forced-air warmer
- BIS; consider cerebral oximetry
- TEE after induction
- Cell salvage
- Defibrillator pads for minimally invasive, MIDCAB or redo surgery
- IABP or ECMO if needed

## Drugs
- Emergency drugs including calcium chloride
- Vasopressors and inotropes: noradrenaline, adrenaline
- Vasodilators: GTN infusion, sodium nitroprusside
- RV support: milrinone, dobutamine
- Insulin infusion in diabetics
- Propofol infusion if needed

## Airway
- Default: high-low pressure cuff ETT unless early extubation is planned
- Double-lumen tube or bronchial blocker for minimally invasive surgery

## Induction
- Cardiostable technique, tailored to the lesion (e.g. ischaemic or valve disease)
- Vasopressors drawn up and ready

## Positioning
- Sternotomy: roll under the upper back to lift the chest and extend the neck
  - After tucking the arms, check the drips still run
- Minimally invasive surgery: left chest tilted up 20-30 degrees, with a roll under the left lateral chest and left buttock
  - Risk of left brachial plexus injury
  - Recheck double-lumen tube position after positioning

## Haemodynamic management
- Hypotension: think of surgical handling of the heart when the chest is open, and tamponade when it is closed
- Fluids: avoid IV fluid early, as the bypass circuit will add a large volume
- Targets by pathology
  - Ischaemic heart disease: avoid tachycardia and hypertension, keep perfusion
  - Valve disease
    - Keep preload normal
    - Afterload reduction helps MR and AR; keep afterload for stenotic lesions
    - Heart rate 60-80 for stenotic valves (slow), 80-100 for regurgitant valves (fast)
    - Tachycardia in severe mitral stenosis can be life-threatening
  - Dissection: limit shear, keep BP and HR low
- Aortic cannulation: SBP <100 to avoid dissection
- On bypass: MAP 50-70mmHg during the cold phase (cross-clamp on) and 60-80mmHg during warm bypass (cross-clamp off)

## Transoesophageal echocardiography
- Detects air, ASD, VSD, AS, AR, MR, MS, volume status, aortic plaque, ischaemia, regional and global ventricular function, valve function and anatomy
  - A regional wall motion abnormality appears within 5 to 10 seconds of inadequate myocardial flow
  - Best ischaemia view: short-axis mid-papillary view
- Probe is costly (about $65,000) and fragile: always a bite block and plenty of lubricant
- Probe can overheat: freeze the image when not in use (the machine also auto-freezes)

## Coagulation
- Antifibrinolytic to reduce bleeding: tranexamic acid 1g before and 1g after bypass
  - Others: aprotinin, aminocaproic acid (Amicar)
- Bypass causes a platelet defect: low count and poor function
  - Check ROTEM or TEG after bypass

## Electrolytes and metabolic
- Potassium: aim K >4-4.5
  - Rises on bypass from cardioplegia
  - Falls fast during rewarming and separation (higher metabolism, catecholamine surge, adrenaline, insulin)
  - The perfusionist can dialyse on the circuit if needed
- Calcium: often needs supplementing when coming off bypass
- Glucose often high on bypass: monitor, consider insulin infusion

## Preparing for bypass
### Sternotomy
- Sternotomy and sternal retraction are stimulating
- Stop ventilation during sternotomy (lungs down) to avoid tearing pleura or pericardium

### Heparinisation
- Always give heparin via the central line, through a different line from the ACT sampling line; flush the port afterwards
- Dose 300-400 U/kg (e.g. 20000U for 50kg); supplied as 1000 units per ml
- Check ACT 3 min after the dose; ACT must be over 400 seconds before bypass
  - If under 400 seconds: tell surgeon and perfusionist, give more heparin 100U/kg at a time (e.g. 5000 U) until above 400 seconds
  - Needing more than 500 IU suggests heparin resistance: give FFP or antithrombin III concentrate

### Activated clotting time
- Draw every ACT from a port not exposed to heparin
- Timing
  - Baseline after induction
  - After heparin, before bypass
  - On bypass (perfusionist)
  - After protamine

### Great vessel cannulation
- Usually right atrium for venous drainage to the pump, ascending aorta for arterial return to the patient
  - Femoral vessels as an alternative (e.g. minimally invasive surgery)
- ACT over 300 sec is safe for cannulation
- Aortic cannulation
  - Lower wall stress to avoid intimal tear and dissection: SBP <100, avoid tachycardia
  - MAP tracks wall tension better; many centres aim for MAP 60–70 mmHg
- Venous cannulation (usually right atrium)
  - Can trigger AF or atrial flutter
  - Brisk blood loss through the atriotomy is possible: keep the patient filled before and during
  - Let SBP rise a little after aortic cannulation in readiness
  - Two-stage cannula: through the apex of the right atrium into the IVC; default for CABG
  - Bicaval cannulation: separate SVC and IVC cannulae for mitral and ASD/VSD cases, so the right atrium can be opened without losing drainage
- Retrograde cardioplegia catheter
  - Passed through the right atrium into the coronary sinus
  - The surgeon lifts the heart to guide it, which often causes haemodynamic instability and may force early bypass
  - A manometer line may be handed over for cardioplegia pressure: zero at the right atrium; do not flush it unless asked (air risk)

## Starting bypass
- ACT over 400 sec before going on bypass
- HAD2SAVE checklist before bypass
  - Heparin given
  - ACT checked (400 seconds)
  - Drugs: top up muscle relaxant
  - Drips: inotropes and infusions off
  - Swan: pull the PA catheter back 5 cm to avoid occlusion or rupture
  - Alarms: silence ECG, BP and CO2 alarms
  - Ventilator: off once at full flow
  - Vaporiser: off
  - Emboli: check the arterial cannula for bubbles
- Top up relaxant so the cooled patient does not shiver (raised oxygen use, muscle breakdown, myoglobinuria, renal failure)
- Sequence
  - Surgeon calls "go on bypass"
  - Perfusionist unclamps the venous line; blood siphons from the right atrium and IVC into the reservoir, then is pumped back into the aorta
  - "Full flow" means pump flow of about 2.2-2.8L/min/m2
  - Then silence alarms and stop the ventilator

## On bypass
- Perfusionist runs pump flow
  - The anaesthetist may still titrate noradrenaline or GTN for MAP
  - BP may dip briefly with cardioplegia
- MAP 50-80mmHg
- Electrolytes and glucose: insulin as needed
- Rewarming: GTN
- Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest: ice packs, NIRS
- Before coming off: draw up the off-bypass drugs, check the pacing box

## Rewarming
- Ensure adequate anaesthesia at the start of rewarming
- Consider extra muscle relaxant
- Consider a benzodiazepine: awareness is most common during rewarming
- GTN for even rewarming
- Prepare to separate: order platelets, start adrenaline

## Defibrillation
- After cross-clamp removal, ventricular arrhythmias are common: internal cardioversion 10–20 J

## De-airing
- Needed when a main chamber (atrium, aorta, PA or ventricle) was opened, especially the left side, to stop air reaching the brain
- TEE shows air as a snowstorm of small bubbles in the ventricle
- Manoeuvres
  - Ballotting the chambers, LV aspiration, needle vent in the aorta
  - Valsalva or positive-pressure breaths to clear air from the pulmonary veins
  - Head-down during cross-clamp removal
  - CO2 insufflation into the pericardium at two litres per minute (more soluble than air)
- Coronary air shows as ischaemia (ST elevation) in the right coronary territory or vein grafts on the aorta, as these arise from the anterior ascending aorta

## Epicardial pacing
- Commonly placed for reliable rate and AV synchrony; may be omitted when risk is low, as wires have their own complications
- Uses
  - Raise cardiac output: after bypass, impaired relaxation makes CO rate-dependent
  - Prevent late severe bradycardia from conduction injury
- Risks: myocardial injury, bleeding and tamponade
- Indications: valve surgery, older age, diabetes, poor LV function, structural heart disease, arrhythmia history, preoperative beta-blocker or digoxin
- Settings for separation: VOO, rate 80-90bpm, A and V output 10 mA
- Modes
  - VOO or DOO (asynchronous): separation from bypass, pacing-dependent patients during diathermy, emergencies such as reopening
  - AAI: no AV block, e.g. sinus bradycardia
  - DDD or DDI: high-grade AV block
- Sensing threshold (A and V)
  - Lower sensitivity (raise the mV value) then bring it back until every P wave is sensed
  - Set sensitivity to half this value for a safety margin
- Capture threshold (A and V)
  - Pace at least 10 beats per minute above the intrinsic rate
  - Raise output in mA until a pacing spike captures (electrical capture); mechanical capture can also be checked
  - Set output to double the capture threshold

## Separation from bypass
- Checklist
  - Breathing: lungs re-expanded and recruited, adequate tidal volumes at acceptable pressures; avoid overinflation, which can stretch or tear the IMA graft
  - Circulation: suitable rate and rhythm (no VT/VF), pacing checked, no vasoplegia; heart filled and contracting on the field, bleeding controlled
  - Anaesthesia restarted
  - Normothermia: temperature 36-37
  - Metabolic (ABG): normal pH, PCO2 and PO2; potassium aim 4-4.5; calcium (avoid in early reperfusion, it can worsen reperfusion injury); magnesium after bypass reduces atrial and ventricular arrhythmias; adequate haemoglobin
  - Surgical: heart de-aired on TEE, de-airing manoeuvres done, haemostasis
  - Drugs: inotropes ready or running, emergency drugs
  - Equipment: monitors back on, alarms set, transducers zeroed
- Mnemonic WAAARRRRMM: Warm, Anaesthesia, Adjuvant drugs, Air, Rhythm and Rate, Resistance, Respiration, Metabolism (electrolytes, acid-base, Hb), Monitoring
- When to start inotropes (e.g. adrenaline 0.02–0.05 mcg/kg/min)
  - Low LVEF before bypass
  - Cross-clamp time over 90–120 min: stunning likely
  - TEE findings
- Weaning
  - Perfusionist gradually clamps the venous line, filling the heart
    - Watch CVP; TEE both ventricles for distension
    - The arterial trace becomes pulsatile as the LV ejects
  - Pump flow reduced in 25% steps until off
  - Optimise CO with TEE
    - Preload: 50-100ml boluses via the aortic cannula
    - Poor LV contraction: inotropes, IABP or ECMO if unable to wean
    - Vasoplegia (low SVR): vasoconstrictor such as noradrenaline
  - TEE for structural or functional problems
- Cardioplegia and venous cannulae come out first once stable
- The aortic cannula stays until protamine is under way, so the perfusionist can keep transfusing

## Protamine
- Give peripherally and slowly
- Never while still on bypass: hide the syringe
- Start once off bypass and stable
- Dose: 1 mg per 100 units heparin (e.g. 200mg); protamine 10 mg per ml and heparin 1000 units per ml, so ml for ml (30 ml heparin, 30 ml protamine)
  - Alternative: 80% of the original heparin dose, as excess protamine is itself anticoagulant
- Target ACT after reversal: normal (120-130s) or under 120% of baseline (<150seconds); give more protamine if not
- Watch for reactions: type I hypotension, type II anaphylactoid, type III pulmonary vasoconstriction (watch CVP)
- Tell the surgeons when it is all in; repeat ACT 5 minutes after
  - If ACT still raised, give a further 50mg
- Pump blood given afterwards contains heparin and may need more protamine

## Removing the aortic cannula
- Once protamine is running with no reaction and the patient stable
- Decide first whether a return to bypass might be needed; if pressure is hard to hold despite inotropes, tell the surgeons
- Lower aortic wall stress: SBP 90-100 mm Hg, avoid tachycardia
- Off bypass once both venous and arterial lines are out

## Failure to separate
- Optimise CO and SVR, including volume and vasopressors
- IABP
- VA-ECMO
- See low cardiac output syndrome and vasoplegia below

## Returning to bypass
- For severe hypotension, bleeding, low output or other problems
- If protamine given: re-heparinise at 400 U/kg and check ACT
  - May need an extra 5,000–10,000 U

## After bypass
- Surgical steps: haemostasis, drains, sternal wires, closure
- Causes of hypotension
  - Cardiogenic: ischaemia (coronary air, surgical injury to coronaries, graft failure), stunning, hibernation, hypocalcaemia, acidosis, arrhythmias
  - Obstructive: pulmonary embolism, air embolism, tamponade, myocardial oedema (worse with chest closure), pneumothorax, protamine pulmonary hypertension
  - Hypovolaemic: bleeding from coagulopathy or poor haemostasis
  - Distributive: vasoplegia (bypass or GTN), histamine release from protamine
- Bleeding and transfusion
  - 1 unit of platelets (CSP) is reasonable for routine CABG if antiplatelets were not stopped, or the surgeon asks, or the field is oozy
  - Check ROTEM, especially if bleeding
  - Most non-surgical bleeding is platelet dysfunction: platelets may be needed
  - Return cell-salvaged blood
- Keep correcting potassium and calcium
- Chest closure
  - Closing the pericardium can cause hypotension, low output and raised right-sided pressures (less preload, impaired RV function)
  - Be ready with fluid, vasopressor boluses or reopening
  - Sternal closure does the same: raise arterial pressure 10 to 15 mm Hg above the closed-chest goal beforehand
  - Lungs looking too big or heart lifting out: consider bronchospasm with air trapping; bronchodilators, ventilator and ETT adjustment
- TEE probe stays in until just before transfer; unlock it before removal

## Postoperative care
- Transfer to cardiac ICU intubated, with stable cardiorespiratory state and adequate haemostasis
- Handover: bypass, cross-clamp and circulatory arrest times; TEE after bypass; arrhythmias; blood products given

## Complications of bypass
### Cardiac
- Myocardial ischaemia, stunning, hibernation
- Arrhythmias
- Early problems needing reopening
  - Bleeding (poor surgical haemostasis or coagulopathy)
  - Tamponade: keep the patient fast, full and tight
  - Occluded graft or failed valve: unexplained poor cardiac performance

### Low cardiac output syndrome after cardiotomy
- Cardiogenic shock after cardiac surgery despite adequate preload, with systemic hypoperfusion
  - Cardiac index < 2.0 L/min/m²
  - SBP < 90 mmHg, or vasopressors needed to keep SBP ≥ 90 mmHg
  - PCWP > 18 mmHg (cardiogenic, not hypovolaemic)
  - End-organ hypoperfusion: urine < 0.5 mL/kg/h, rising lactate, cold peripheries
- Up to 6% of cardiac surgical patients; much higher morbidity and mortality
- Risk factors
  - Patient: age, female sex, heart failure (EF < 30%), chronic renal failure, diabetes, COPD
  - Surgical: emergency or redo surgery, combined procedures (e.g. CABG plus mitral surgery), long cross-clamp or bypass time (typically > 120 minutes)
- Management
  - Treat reversible causes: optimise preload, contractility (inotropes), SVR (vasodilators or vasopressors)
  - Rhythm: epicardial pacing for HR 80–100 bpm and AV synchrony
  - Move early to IABP or VA-ECMO if high-dose inotropes fail
  - Consider delayed sternal closure

### Vasoplegic syndrome after cardiotomy
- Distributive shock after bypass: low SVR with normal or high cardiac output
  - MAP < 65 mmHg despite fluid
  - SVR < 800
- Incidence 5% to 44%
- Mechanism
  - Surgical trauma and circuit contact release cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α): direct vasodilation
  - Cytokines induce inducible nitric oxide synthase; excess NO activates guanylate cyclase and relaxes smooth muscle
  - Vasopressin stores run down on long bypass
  - Adrenergic receptor desensitisation; ATP-sensitive potassium channels open, hyperpolarising vascular smooth muscle (vasopressor resistance)
- Risk factors
  - Patient: low EF, chronic renal failure, ACE inhibitors or ARBs before surgery (RAAS interference)
  - Surgical: long bypass or cross-clamp time, complex surgery
- Management
  - First line: fluid for preload; noradrenaline; vasopressin (replaces deficiency, counters K-ATP channel opening)
  - Rescue
    - Methylene blue 1–2 mg/kg: blocks guanylate cyclase in the NO pathway
    - Hydroxocobalamin (vitamin B12): scavenges NO
    - Angiotensin II: vasoconstricts via AT1 receptors
    - Corticosteroids (e.g. hydrocortisone): damp SIRS, restore receptor response

### Non-cardiac
- SIRS from blood contact with non-endothelial surfaces
- Neurological (most feared)
  - Stroke: ischaemic, from macroemboli (calcium, air, atheroma) during aortic handling; risk with heavy aortic plaque, circulatory arrest, or MAP <50 mmHg on bypass
  - Neurocognitive decline from global ischaemia or microemboli ("pump head", post-perfusion syndrome)
  - Delirium
  - Spinal cord injury
  - Peripheral nerves
    - Brachial plexus: sternal retraction, 1st rib rotation
    - Phrenic: topical ice slush, IMA harvest
    - Recurrent laryngeal: arch handling, ETT cuff pressure
    - Ulnar: poor padding, radial artery harvest
    - Common peroneal: pressure at the fibular head
    - Saphenous: vein harvest at the ankle
    - Intercostal: thoracotomy, chest drains
    - Femoral: prolonged groin cannulation or retraction
- Lung ("pump lung")
  - SIRS-driven capillary leak and surfactant loss
  - Ranges from atelectasis to ARDS
  - Limit bypass time; lung-protective ventilation straight after bypass
- Acute kidney injury
  - Causes: pigment from haemolysis, non-pulsatile flow, emboli
  - Prevention: adequate MAP on bypass, avoid nephrotoxins (NSAIDs) early after surgery
- Coagulation and blood
  - Platelets: fewer and dysfunctional (activated by the circuit)
  - Clotting factors diluted
  - Bypass triggers fibrinolysis, hence routine tranexamic acid
  - Haemolysis from pump head and suction trauma: check urine for haemoglobin
- Metabolic and endocrine
  - Hyperglycaemia from stress-related insulin resistance: insulin infusion often needed
  - K+ and Mg2+ fall as they move back into cells on rewarming

### Awareness
- Cardiac surgery is a risk factor
- Causes
  - Starting bypass: haemodilution and drug uptake by the circuit and oxygenator lower plasma levels; propofol TCI models are inaccurate on bypass
  - Hypothermia changes drug handling and can mask light anaesthesia
  - Rewarming: metabolism rises while drug levels lag
  - Deliberately light anaesthesia in low-EF patients to avoid myocardial depression
- Prevention
  - Volatile via the oxygenator on bypass is the most reliable; oxygenator exhaust gas monitoring stands in for end-tidal
  - Midazolam before bypass for anterograde amnesia
  - Processed EEG, e.g. BIS
  - Preoperative counselling for high-risk patients

## Crashing onto bypass
- Vasopressors, head-down tilt
- Full heparin dose (300–400 units/kg) via a central line; confirm ACT > 400 seconds
- In an extreme crash the surgeon may go on before the ACT result: make sure the dose is actually in

## Redo sternotomy
- Risks: RV laceration and massive bleeding, arrhythmia or VF, injury to old grafts
- Preoperative: LV function often poor; CT thorax
- Intraoperative
  - Torrential bleeding risk (RV may stick to the sternum): large-bore access (e.g. PA introducer sheath), blood in theatre, femoral cannulation with the bypass team ready before opening
  - VF risk at sternotomy and adhesiolysis: defibrillator pads on
  - Graft injury during dissection: watch the ECG
  - Coagulopathy common: tranexamic acid, TEG
- After surgery: more bleeding; poor LV function

## Minimally invasive surgery
- Minimally invasive CABG: see CABG variants
- Minimally invasive valve surgery: smaller incision, possibly thoracotomy, still on bypass

## Flashcards
- Q: Heparin dose and target ACT before going on bypass?
  - A: 300-400 U/kg via central line; ACT over 400 seconds (over 300 sec is enough for cannulation)
- Q: Protamine dose for heparin reversal?
  - A: 1 mg per 100 units heparin, given peripherally and slowly; never on bypass
- Q: MAP targets on bypass?
  - A: 50-70mmHg on cold bypass (cross-clamp on), 60-80mmHg on warm bypass
- Q: Epicardial pacing settings for separation?
  - A: VOO, rate 80-90bpm, A and V output 10 mA
- Q: Methylene blue dose and action in refractory vasoplegia?
  - A: 1–2 mg/kg; blocks guanylate cyclase in the NO pathway
