# Breast Surgery

Sided surgery in anxious patients with high PONV risk; protect the operative arm, watch for dye effects, and use multimodal analgesia.

## Main considerations
- Sided surgery
  - No IV access or BP cuff on the operative side
  - With cuff and IV on the same arm, fit an anti-reflux valve to the IV line
  - Previous axillary clearance: avoid cannulating that arm (infection, lymphoedema risk)
    - Evidence on short-term cannulation of that arm is limited; if access is poor it may be used and removed at the end
  - Bilateral mastectomy: may need IV access and BP cuff on a lower limb
- Dyes can give false SpO2 readings
  - Methylene blue may be injected at the start for sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB); dose 1-2mg/kg
  - Patent blue: risk of anaphylaxis
- High PONV risk
- Patients often anxious
- Reconstructive surgery can be long with major blood loss

## Preoperative assessment
- Address anxiety: build rapport, discuss analgesia, prescribe anxiolysis if needed (e.g. temazepam 10–20mg)
- Recent chemotherapy
  - Possible immunosuppression: check FBC for marrow suppression
  - Expect difficult venous access
- Higher blood loss risk: reconstruction, mastectomy after radiotherapy, large breasts, breast reduction
  - Check Hb; group and screen

## Monitoring and equipment
- Standard monitoring for most cases
- Invasive monitoring may be needed for long reconstructions, including free flaps
- BIS if TIVA
- Active warming and temperature monitoring for longer cases

## Airway
- LMA suits short to medium cases
- Tracheal intubation for longer cases, especially with non-supine positioning (e.g. latissimus dorsi flap)

## Drugs
- Consider TIVA to reduce PONV

## Positioning
- Usually supine with the operative arm abducted on an armboard; useful to have the other arm out too
- Bilateral cases: both arms abducted (crucifix position)
- Check arm position to avoid brachial plexus stretch injury

## Antibiotics
- Often not needed; otherwise cefazolin
- Plastics may want co-amoxiclav (Augmentin) for reconstruction after mastectomy

## Axillary dissection
- Avoiding paralysis lets surgeons see when they are near a nerve
- Some surgeons still want paralysis

## Postoperative analgesia
- Balanced analgesia: paracetamol and NSAID if tolerated, plus systemic opioid
- Regional options: paravertebral, PECS, thoracic epidural, intercostal, intrapleural
  - Know each technique's complications; ultrasound helps paravertebral block
  - In healthy women having minor surgery, risks may outweigh benefits
  - Consider for radical mastectomy, axillary clearance and reconstruction
- Continue paravertebral catheter or epidural infusions after surgery
- Chronic pain
  - Anterior chest wall, ipsilateral axilla or upper arm
  - Risk factors: severe pain after extensive surgery, postoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy
  - Risk seems lower in Asian patients

## PONV
- High risk: avoid triggers and give two antiemetics

## Disposition
- HDU may be needed after extensive surgery

## PROSPECT recommendations
- Paracetamol and NSAID before or during surgery, continued afterwards
- Preoperative gabapentin and dexamethasone
- Major surgery: consider paravertebral or pectoral nerves block and/or wound infiltration
  - Paravertebral can continue via catheter
- Erector spinae and retrolaminar blocks need more research
- Opioids as postoperative rescue only

## Flashcards
- Q: Why avoid cannulation on the side of a previous axillary clearance?
  - A: Risk of infection and lymphoedema.
- Q: What dose of methylene blue is noted for SLNB, and what monitoring problem can dyes cause?
  - A: 1-2mg/kg; false SpO2 readings.
- Q: Which blue dye carries an anaphylaxis risk?
  - A: Patent blue.
- Q: Which preoperative adjuncts does PROSPECT recommend for breast surgery?
  - A: Gabapentin and dexamethasone, plus paracetamol and NSAID.
- Q: Why might surgeons ask you to avoid paralysis in axillary dissection?
  - A: So they can tell when they are near a nerve.
