# Cervical Spine Surgery

Fusion or fixation of an unstable neck: airway risk to the cord, cord perfusion, neuromonitoring and postoperative airway obstruction.

## Key points
- Indications: unstable neck, such as arthritis or trauma
- Duration: 2–3 hr
- Pain: moderate to severe
- Blood loss: 300–1000 ml
- Position: supine or prone
- Cervical traction already in place or applied at the start
- Very unstable necks may need awake fibreoptic intubation
- Ask about neurological symptoms on neck movement

## Procedures
- Anterior fusion or fixation of the mid and lower cervical spine
  - Anterior approach reaches the whole cervical spine below C2
- Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF)
  - For herniated discs or osteophytes pressing on the cord or roots
  - Often a left-sided approach, which lowers the risk to the recurrent laryngeal nerve
  - Route: between trachea and oesophagus medially and carotid sheath laterally, onto longus colli and the vertebral bodies
  - Level confirmed with fluoroscopy; disc removed piecemeal under the microscope
  - Fusion and instrumentation follow, to keep disc height and lordosis, stop graft extrusion, allow early mobilisation and prevent later collapse and pain
  - Bone graft or cage placed; PEEK and carbon-fibre cages are radiolucent, so fusion is easy to assess
- Cervical artificial disc replacement (ADR)
  - Same approach as ACDF, but a mobile prosthetic disc replaces the graft or cage
  - No extra stabilisation or graft; exact placement is critical, so heavy fluoroscopy use
  - Stable at once and motion preserved, so early mobilisation

## Neuroprotection
- Cord monitoring
  - Sensory and motor evoked potentials increasingly used
  - Motor potentials are suppressed by volatile agents and neuromuscular blockers
- Cord perfusion
  - Prolonged minor malposition may cause some cord injuries: any position that looks uncomfortable should be corrected
  - Most have some cord or root compression, so keep perfusion pressure up

## Airway management
- Difficult airway
  - Cervical spine disease is a major cause of difficult direct laryngoscopy
  - Disease of the occiput-atlas-axis complex is the most likely culprit
- Risk of cord injury during airway management
  - In unstable spines, chin lift and jaw thrust move the neck as much as direct laryngoscopy
  - This argues for awake airway placement, but there is no evidence awake techniques improve neurological outcome
- Traction pin insertion is very stimulating
- Antibiotics may need delaying until pins are in, as they must be within 1 hr of incision

## Postoperative management
- Airway obstruction is fairly common after anterior surgery
  - Sometimes haematoma, but often tissue swelling
  - Usually within 6 h, but can be later
  - Most likely after combined anterior and posterior surgery

## Cervical spine fracture fixation
- Anterior or posterior approach; bone graft, if needed, usually from the iliac crest
- Preoperative assessment
  - Usually major trauma; minor injury can fracture a diseased spine
  - Look for other features of the underlying disease, such as rheumatoid
  - Establish injury, level and planned approach
  - Plan for postoperative ventilation and HDU, more likely with high lesions, which may need intensive chest physiotherapy
  - Plan intubation: skull traction limits neck movement but not mouth opening; a hard collar with sandbags or tape limits both
- Airway
  - Best method is disputed
  - Awake intubation: safe in trained hands, but can cause coughing and be unpleasant and difficult
  - Direct laryngoscopy under GA with MILNS may move the neck more
  - Avoid tracheostomy before anterior fusion; discuss with the surgeon if it is likely
- Suxamethonium contraindicated in cord lesions over 72 hr old; rarely needed in practice
- Positioning
  - Anterior: supine head-up, oblique neck incision
  - Posterior: prone, longitudinal incision
  - Prone turn: surgeon holds the head and neck, at least three people turn, anaesthetist holds the ETT and coordinates
  - Some centres intubate and position awake before induction
  - C1/C2 fractures occasionally need a transoral approach
- Full neurological examination before anaesthesia, especially if turning prone
- Lines well secured against kinking; forced-air warming; urinary catheter
- Nasogastric decompression for long cases or existing cord injury
- Check pressure areas before draping; cases are long
- Acute cord lesions: neurogenic shock with bradycardia and hypotension
  - Treat with careful fluids and vasopressors, guided by CVP
  - Uncommon in practice, as surgery is rarely in the 1st few hours after injury

## Example: acute C2 fracture with mild neurological deficit
- Airway
  - Risk of cord injury during airway handling; keep the neck neutral
  - Manual in-line stabilisation with videolaryngoscopy, or awake fibreoptic intubation
- Neuroprotection
  - Keep cord perfusion up, ideally MAP above 80; may need vasopressors and an arterial line
  - Normocapnia and normoglycaemia
- High cervical injury: risk of neurogenic shock; treat bradycardia, avoid hypovolaemia
- Look for other injuries
- Surgical needs
  - Neuromonitoring: TIVA with remifentanil, no neuromuscular block
  - May need prone positioning
- Anaesthetic
  - Avoid suxamethonium: hyperkalaemia from denervation
  - Worsening neurology can impair breathing afterwards; may need ventilation, at least high dependency care
  - DVT prophylaxis
- Assessment
  - History: mechanism and date of injury, weakness, numbness, breathlessness, dizziness
  - Examination: cardiorespiratory, focusing on respiratory impairment
  - Full neurological examination with ASIA score, myotome level, baseline power and sensation, anal tone
  - Airway assessment for difficulty
  - Vital signs (heart rate, BP, respiratory rate, oxygen need) for neurogenic shock or ventilatory failure
  - Make sure a tertiary trauma survey has been done
  - Investigations: routine bloods, FBC, valid group and crossmatch

## Flashcards
- Q: Why is ACDF often approached from the left?
  - A: Lower risk of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury
- Q: When does airway obstruction usually present after anterior cervical surgery?
  - A: Within 6 h, though it can be later; often from swelling rather than haematoma
- Q: After what time is suxamethonium contraindicated in spinal cord lesions?
  - A: Lesions over 72 hr old
- Q: What MAP target protects cord perfusion in acute cervical cord injury?
  - A: Ideally above 80
- Q: Which anaesthetic drugs suppress motor evoked potentials?
  - A: Volatile agents and neuromuscular blockers
