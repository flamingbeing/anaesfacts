# Cholecystectomy

Gallbladder removal, usually laparoscopic and often day case; watch for sepsis and aspiration risk in acute cholecystitis, cystic artery bleeding and conversion to open.

## Key points
- Acute ("hot") gallbladder or not: affects aspiration risk and surgical difficulty
- Possible sepsis
- Intraoperative cholangiogram (IOC) needs the image intensifier

## Laparoscopic versus open
- Fewer than 10% of cholecystectomies start open
- Conversion to open is about 5%, up to 10% in acute cholecystitis
- Causes: adhesions (previous laparotomy), unclear cystic duct anatomy, suspected bile duct injury, uncontrolled cystic artery bleeding, common bile duct stones, acute inflammation

## Laparoscopic cholecystectomy

### Quick plan
- Preoperative: group and crossmatch
- Position: supine, then head-up and right side up once insufflated; left arm out
- Monitoring and lines: temperature; orogastric tube not needed
- GA
  - Airway: ProSeal LMA possible if not obese, no lung disease, haemodynamically stable
  - Paralyse
- Antibiotics: ceftriaxone and metronidazole
- Ventilation: PRVC or pressure control for laparoscopy
- Procedure: may need IOC (takes longer); cystic artery bleeding possible; can be as quick as 45mins
- Analgesia: morphine
- Antiemetics: two agents
- Emergence: reverse early if waking with an LMA
- Postoperative: 23hr stay or same-day admission for elective cases

### Preoperative assessment
- Classically "female, forty, fair, fat and fertile", though not always
- Haemolytic anaemia may underlie pigment stones
- Acute cholecystitis: may have mild to moderate sepsis needing resuscitation
- Consider group and crossmatch with recurrent or chronic gallbladder disease (liver adhesions)
- Admission
  - Usually day case; plans for unexpected admission must exist; list early in the day
  - Patients may ask for admission
- Equipment: standard laparoscopic set; may need fluoroscopy and choledochoscope for cholangiography or bile duct exploration

### Intraoperative management
- Duration 40-80min depending on difficulty
  - Previous infection may stick the gallbladder down
  - IOC suggests a harder case (can take hours)
- Pain: moderate to severe; pain score 3/10 (Manual of Surgical Procedures)
- Blood loss usually small, but hard to judge through the laparoscope; heavy bleeding from cystic or hepatic arteries can occur
- Position
  - Left arm out, right arm tucked
  - Supine; may start in Trendelenburg for trocar insertion
  - Then 15–20° head-up, right side up (reverse Trendelenburg)
  - Level the table once the gallbladder is out
- Technique
  - GA with ETT and IPPV
  - LMA often used, but not in obesity (airway pressures too high)
  - Regional option (e.g. obesity): subcostal TAP block plus LA to the umbilical port
- Laparoscopic issues
  - Pneumoperitoneum plus obesity can make ventilation hard
  - Muscle relaxation needed for abdominal wall relaxation
- Monitoring: unstable septic patients occasionally need more IV access, arterial line and CVP
- Maintenance
  - Continue muscle relaxation
  - Controlled ventilation limits hypercarbia from absorbed CO2
- IOC: radiation precautions
- Antibiotics: usually cefazolin for elective cases; may not be needed

### Postoperative care
- Analgesia
  - Surgeon infiltrates port sites with LA at the end
  - LA to the gallbladder bed may reduce analgesic need (20mL of 0.25% bupivacaine)
  - After conversion to open, pain can be significant: morphine or fentanyl PCA, surgical LA infiltration, consider a wound catheter
- Usually discharged within 24hrs
- High PONV risk: prophylaxis, e.g. dexamethasone and ondansetron
- Shoulder tip pain from pneumoperitoneum: usually self-limiting; NSAIDs if not contraindicated

## Open cholecystectomy
- Gallbladder removed through a right upper quadrant incision
- Duration 1hr
- Pain: severe
- Blood loss 0.5L
- Supine
- May need PCA

## Flashcards
- Q: What is the usual conversion rate from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy?
  - A: About 5%, up to 10% in acute cholecystitis
- Q: What position is used for the main part of a lap chole?
  - A: 15–20° head-up, right side up (reverse Trendelenburg)
- Q: What LA can be placed in the gallbladder bed to reduce analgesic need?
  - A: 20mL of 0.25% bupivacaine
- Q: Typical duration of a laparoscopic cholecystectomy?
  - A: 40-80min, longer with IOC
