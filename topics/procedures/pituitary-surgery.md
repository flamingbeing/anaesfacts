# Pituitary and Transsphenoidal Surgery

Usually endoscopic transnasal surgery done like FESS: think hormones, mass effect, a bloodless field, smooth emergence, and diabetes insipidus afterwards.

## Key points

- Usually transsphenoidal (rarely transcranial)
  - Like FESS: slight head up, avoid high BP; warn the patient the nose will be blocked
  - Suprasellar tumour may need pushing down into the field; consider a lumbar drain
  - Risk of CSF leak: avoid postoperative surges in ICP
- Hormones
  - Secreting (hyperprolactinaemia, Cushing's, acromegaly) vs non-secreting
  - Pituitary function: may need steroids or thyroxine; may have DI
- Mass effect: visual field loss, cavernous sinus involvement, hydrocephalus
- Rare complication: pituitary apoplexy

## Anatomy

- Pituitary fossa boundaries
  - Superior: optic chiasm
  - Inferior: roof of sphenoid sinus
  - Lateral: cavernous sinus with internal carotid artery and CN 3, 4, 5, 6
- Anterior pituitary hormones: ACTH, GH, TSH, FSH / LH
- Posterior pituitary hormones: ADH, oxytocin

## Pituitary tumours

- 15% of intracranial tumours
- Present in the 4-6th decade
- Classification
  - Microadenoma (< 1cm) vs macroadenoma (> 1cm); macroadenomas often stay silent and present later
  - Functioning vs non-functioning
- Presentation
  - Hormone excess: acromegaly, Cushing's syndrome, hyperprolactinaemia, infertility
  - Mass effect: raised ICP (headache, vomiting), bitemporal hemianopia from chiasm compression, hydrocephalus, hypopituitarism, seizures

## Surgical considerations (transsphenoidal)

- Little blood loss
- May be long
- Supine with head-up tilt: risk of venous air embolism (VAE)

## Preoperative assessment

- Tumour: micro vs macro; functioning vs non-functioning
- Complications to assess and optimise
  - Hormone excess: endocrine status, co-manage with endocrinology, optimise medical treatment
  - Mass effect: raised ICP, visual fields, focal deficits
  - Acromegaly / Cushing's comorbidities: hypertension, diabetes, obesity, OSA
  - Electrolytes: correct hyperglycaemia and hypokalaemia (Cushing's)
- Airway assessment
- Neurological examination: visual fields, focal deficits, peripheral neuropathy, proximal myopathy
- Imaging; group and crossmatch; ENT assess the nose
- Agree surgical approach with the surgeon
  - Transcranial: larger tumours; less stimulating; but more DI and anterior pituitary failure, and olfactory nerve damage
  - Transethmoidal
  - Transsphenoidal endoscopic transnasal: smaller tumours; less DI and anterior pituitary failure, magnified view, less bleeding; but CSF leak or meningitis, poor view of neural structures around a large tumour, bleeding from cavernous sinus or carotid
- Premedication
  - Continue somatostatin analogues and dopamine agonists
  - Continue thyroxine
  - Perioperative steroids
    - Check Synacthen test and 8am cortisol
    - Normal: no perioperative steroids; check cortisol daily for 1-3 days
    - Abnormal: supraphysiological glucocorticoid for 48h
      - Day 0: hydrocortisone 50mg 8-hourly (Q8H)
      - Day 1: hydrocortisone 25mg 8-hourly (Q8H)
      - Day 2: hydrocortisone 25mg at 8am

## Dexamethasone

- Avoid dexamethasone in patients with an intact HPA axis having transsphenoidal surgery (endocrinology need to test cortisol afterwards)
- Exceptions
  1. Cushing's disease
  2. Acute pituitary problems needing urgent surgery, e.g. apoplexy
  3. Long-term steroids for another condition
  4. Transcranial surgery, where short courses of high-dose steroid are usual
  5. Surgeon's request, e.g. to reduce oedema from optic nerve compression

## Intraoperative management

- Position: slight head up; table turned at a right angle, anaesthetic machine moved down towards the feet
- Monitoring: non-invasive or invasive BP; VAE monitoring
- Goals
  - Optimise cerebral oxygenation; stable haemodynamics
  - Good surgical exposure
  - Rapid, smooth emergence and early neurological assessment
- Airway
  - May be difficult (acromegaly, Cushing's)
  - GA with IPPV and short-acting drugs
  - Armoured or south-facing RAE tube secured to the left (like FESS)
  - Throat pack to stop blood reaching the glottis (airway obstruction, nausea and vomiting)
- Nasal vasoconstrictors (injected by the surgeon)
  - Adrenaline: tachycardia, arrhythmias, myocardial infarction, especially with cocaine
  - Xylometazoline is safer
- Maintenance
  - Short-acting agents for early neurological assessment
  - TIVA helps smooth emergence and reduces PONV; no proven outcome difference between TIVA and volatiles
  - Keep paralysed; stimulation is fairly low
  - Avoid N2O: pneumocephalus
  - Antibiotics: co-amoxiclav (Augmentin), as for FESS
  - Fluids: isotonic
- Ventilation
  - Avoid hypocarbia: brain shrinks, tumour pulls upwards and suprasellar part becomes harder to reach
  - Normal to high-normal PaCO2 helps bring the tumour down
  - Alternative: lumbar drain for suprasellar extension; saline can be injected to push the tumour into the field
- Neuroprotection: control BP and ICP; avoid hypertension for a bloodless field
- Bleeding risk from internal carotid artery and cavernous sinus
- Combined with ENT, who do the initial FESS-style approach; thigh may be draped for a fascia graft (dural patch)
- Analgesia: fentanyl or morphine

## Emergence

- Remove throat pack and suction the oropharynx before extubation
- Smooth, rapid wake-up to assess GCS early
- Blunt the hypertensive response (bleeding)
- Avoid ICP surges that could cause a CSF leak

## Postoperative management

- Analgesia: paracetamol, codeine phosphate
- Nothing through the nose, including NIV
- Complications
  - Airway obstruction: nasal packs, blood tracking down, acromegalic or Cushingoid airway, OSA
  - Hormone replacement: endocrinology review of HPA axis
  - Diabetes insipidus (> 80% of neuronal dysfunction): treat with desmopressin
  - Haemorrhage: cavernous sinus or carotid injury, residual tumour
  - CSF rhinorrhoea, meningitis
  - Visual loss, cranial nerve palsy (CN 3, 4, 5, 6 in the cavernous sinus)
  - Pneumocephalus

## Flashcards
- Q: Why avoid hypocarbia in transsphenoidal surgery?
  - A: Brain shrinks and pulls the tumour up, making the suprasellar part harder to reach
- Q: Steroid regimen if Synacthen or 8am cortisol is abnormal?
  - A: Hydrocortisone 50mg Q8H day 0, 25mg Q8H day 1, 25mg at 8am day 2
- Q: When can dexamethasone be given in transsphenoidal surgery despite an intact HPA axis?
  - A: Cushing's disease, apoplexy, long-term steroids, transcranial surgery, or surgeon request
- Q: Why avoid N2O?
  - A: Risk of pneumocephalus
- Q: What does a pituitary tumour make up of intracranial tumours?
  - A: 15%
