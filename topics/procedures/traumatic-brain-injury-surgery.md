# Traumatic Brain Injury Surgery

Emergency neuro-trauma: protect cerebral perfusion (CPP >60, MAP >80), avoid rises in ICP, RSI with in-line stabilisation.

## General principles
- In trauma, always consider craniotomy if there are:
  - Lateralising signs
  - Depressed skull fracture
- Also consider an ICP monitor
- CPP target in TBI >60
- Admission GCS for risk:
  - Mild TBI = 15-13
  - Moderate TBI = 9-13; GCS >8 carries low risk of raised ICP
  - Severe TBI = ≤8
    - With GCS ≤8, 25% have raised ICP (without a surgical lesion)
    - 50% have major extracranial injuries

## Possible procedures
- Sedation or anaesthesia for imaging
- ICP monitor insertion
- Craniotomy and haematoma evacuation
- Decompressive craniectomy
- Extracranial surgery

## Intracranial surgery: main considerations
- Patient factors
  - Emergency: unfasted (aspiration risk); little time to assess premorbid state or HI complications, or to optimise
  - Prevent rises in ICP: keep ICP < 20-25mmHg
    - Head up 30 degrees, neck neutral, no tight ETT ties
    - Optimise ventilation
    - Avoid high intrathoracic pressure and PEEP
    - Adequate depth, analgesia and paralysis: no agitation, gagging on the ETT or ventilator dyssynchrony
  - Neuroprotection
    - Maintain CPP: MAP > 80mmHg (assuming ICP 20mmHg); urine output 0.5-1ml/kg/h
      - Blunt hypertensive responses: intubation, pinning, opening bone or dura, skin incision, extubation
      - Avoid hypotension: isotonic fluids (no hypotonic fluids), vasopressors
    - Normoxia and normocarbia: PaO2 > 100mmHg, PaCO2 35-40mmHg
    - Normothermia and normoglycaemia
    - Seizure prophylaxis
  - Complications of severe TBI
    - Myocardial injury, neurogenic pulmonary oedema, ARDS
    - DIC, diabetes insipidus, sympathetic hyperactivity
  - Uncleared C-spine: possible difficult airway, C-spine protection (MILS, spinal nursing)
  - Other life-threatening injuries may take priority (e.g. ruptured viscus)
  - Possible difficult airway: minimal neck movement, maxillofacial injuries
- Anaesthetic factors: maintain cerebral perfusion, prevent rises in ICP
- Surgical factors: may be long
  - Temperature monitoring and normothermia
  - Careful fluid balance
  - DVT prophylaxis

## Preoperative preparation
- Patient
  - AMPLE history
  - Airway, cardiorespiratory and neurological examination
  - Investigations
    - Bloods: FBC, urea/electrolytes/creatinine, PT/PTT
    - ECG, CXR (ETT position)
    - CT brain
    - Group and crossmatch, blood products available
  - Ensure adequate oxygenation and ventilation
  - Sedation to prevent agitation, gagging and dyssynchrony
- Theatre
  - Theatre available
  - Difficult airway trolley
  - Induction, resuscitation and vasopressor drugs; drugs for raised ICP
  - Skilled assistant, senior anaesthetist
  - Monitoring
    - Standard ASA: ECG, NIBP, SpO2, ETCO2, end-tidal agent
    - Invasive: arterial line and CVP (must not delay urgent decompression)
    - Others: temperature, orogastric tube, urinary catheter, BIS
    - Large-bore IV access

## Intraoperative management
- GA
- Monitoring: orogastric tube, urinary catheter, temperature probe, BIS
- Airway: RSI with cricoid pressure; manual in-line stabilisation
- Induction
  - Thiopentone or propofol, fentanyl, suxamethonium (fasciculation-related ICP rise not clinically significant)
  - Blunt the pressor response: lidocaine, esmolol, remifentanil
- Maintenance
  - TIVA or volatile
    - TIVA lowers CMRO2, CBF and ICP; keeps CO2 reactivity and autoregulation
    - Volatiles lower CMRO2, and lower CBF < 1.5 MAC
  - ETT and IPPV: PEEP for neurogenic pulmonary oedema or ARDS, but avoid high PEEP (raises ICP)
  - Isotonic fluids; avoid hypotonic
  - Coagulopathy and DIC (thromboplastin release from brain tissue early in severe TBI)
    - Check platelets and PT/PTT; give platelets, FFP, cryoprecipitate
  - Analgesia: fentanyl, remifentanil
  - Avoid drugs that raise ICP: halothane, enflurane, nitrous oxide, ketamine

## Postoperative care
- Extubate to high dependency, or keep intubated to neuro ICU
- Need for ventilation depends on:
  - Type and severity of TBI
  - Initial GCS
  - Comorbidities
  - Associated injuries, especially chest

## Extracranial surgery in TBI patients
- Preoperative
  - Priority: rapid and complete physiological resuscitation
  - Urgent surgery: go immediately, even without CT brain
  - Non-urgent surgery: better delayed
  - May need time to stop and reverse anticoagulation
  - Proceed when GCS stable, no new deficit, CT brain stable, and enough time (~72h) for the injury to evolve
- Intraoperative
  - GA or regional
    - Regional (if no contraindication such as raised ICP): allows neurological monitoring
    - But heavy sedation hides neurology; risk of herniation if ICP raised
  - Prevent and treat raised ICP

## ICP monitor insertion
- Usually very quick

## Decompressive craniectomy
- Associated with coagulopathy (thrombin released from brain)
- BP falls when sympathetic drive is released at decompression
- Can bleed a lot once the dura is opened

## Evacuation of traumatic intracranial haemorrhage
- Types
  - Extradural: middle meningeal artery tear from skull fracture (except in children); must be done <1hr of pupil dilation
  - Subdural: bridging veins between cortex and dura; early evacuation of acute SDH gives better outcome; chronic SDH in the elderly can be drained by burr hole under LA
  - Intracerebral: in hypertensives, on warfarin, or with aneurysm
- Overall goals
  - Optimise CPP and prevent intracranial hypertension
  - Intubate if GCS ≤8 or it falls by ≥2 in any one hour
  - Adequate anaesthesia and analgesia
  - Prevent secondary injury
- Preoperative: falling GCS and rising ICP; look for associated injuries, especially spinal
- Intraoperative
  - Induction as for craniotomy; arterial line, with or without central line
  - Assume ICP = 20, so MAP target >80-90mmHg
  - Intraoperative hypotension is linked to a 3 times higher mortality
  - After decompression SBP may fall; treat with volume
- Postoperative: protocol to keep ICP low and CPP adequate

## Non-cranial surgery in a TBI patient
- Timing
  - Life-threatening injury (e.g. ruptured intra-abdominal organ)
  - Non-life-threatening injury (e.g. maxillofacial)
- Non-life-threatening injury
  - Surgery in the 1st 24 hrs falls in the risk period for low MAP and low PaO2
  - Excellent head injury care takes priority
- Tourniquet
  - Inflation: venous return and MAP rise, which (depending on autoregulation) can raise ICP
  - Release lowers CBF
    - Systemic: PaCO2 rises, vasodilation, MAP falls, CPP falls
    - Cerebral: PaCO2 rises, cerebral vasodilation, ICP rises and CBF falls
