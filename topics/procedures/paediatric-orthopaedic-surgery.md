# Paediatric Orthopaedic Surgery

Mostly healthy children, but some have syndromes or neuromuscular disease; expect repeated anaesthetics, hard IV access, careful positioning and heavy use of regional analgesia.

## General considerations
- Most children are well with an isolated orthopaedic problem; some have syndromes or neuromuscular disorders
  - IV access can be hard: contractures, thin subcutaneous tissue, small fragile veins
  - Positioning is crucial for access and to protect fragile bones, thin skin and low muscle mass; support each joint with contractures to prevent pressure sores and dislocation
  - Airway may be difficult if TMJ and cervical spine are stiff, or with poor dentition
    - Reflux is common but usually not clinically important at induction
  - Respiratory function may be reduced
  - Care with neuromuscular blockers: neurological or metabolic disease alters responses; suxamethonium may be contraindicated
- Repeated anaesthetics are common (surgery, casts, cast changes, metalwork removal)
  - Give good premedication and make the experience as positive as possible
- Most standard techniques suit, including LMA for short supine cases
- Tourniquets are common:
  - Cuff width more than half the limb diameter to spread pressure safely
  - Pressure set by blood pressure and limb size; lower limb: 150 mm Hg above systolic is enough
- Bone and tendon surgery can be very painful
- Many cases suit regional anaesthesia, though it gets harder as children with musculoskeletal disease grow
  - Turning for neuraxial block can endanger a secured difficult airway
  - Caudal or lumbar epidurals strongly recommended for major surgery
    - May be contraindicated with platelet dysfunction in osteogenesis imperfecta or abnormal anatomy in spina bifida
    - Technically hard (positioning, bone quality, scoliosis); spread unpredictable with skeletal abnormality
  - Concern that blocks may mask compartment syndrome pain
    - Ilizarov limb lengthening causes compartment syndrome distal to the site in about 2%; some teams avoid regional for this reason
    - Children may be more at risk because their lower physiological MAP gives lower compartment perfusion pressure
- Postoperative muscle spasm:
  - Continue usual antispasticity drugs (e.g. in cerebral palsy) to avoid withdrawal and worse spasm
  - Osteotomy and tendon lengthening predispose to painful spasm
  - Manage acute spasm in a critical care setting: control pain, then add IV benzodiazepine with close monitoring for oversedation and respiratory depression
    - Diazepam 0.1 mg kg-1, or midazolam infusion 10-30 mcg kg-1 h-1
    - Baclofen or dantrolene alone or with benzodiazepines

## Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH)
### Background
- Hip dislocated or dislocatable (unstable) at birth
  - About 1.5% unstable at birth; only about 1 in 400 (UK) need treatment
  - A few have an irreducible fixed (teratologic) dislocation at birth
- 4-5 times more common in girls; also after breech birth and with family history; left hip most often; incidence varies worldwide
- Ideally found by neonatal screening (Ortolani and Barlow tests); UK uses ultrasound only with risk factors, routine in some countries
- Late presentation is fairly common, classically around 1 year after starting to walk (painless limp, short leg), sometimes later
- Infants: Pavlik harness or gallows traction, then arthrogram and closed reduction under GA
  - Hip manipulated so the femoral head sits congruently in the acetabulum; often with percutaneous adductor tenotomy
  - Hip spica applied; changed under GA roughly monthly for 2-3 months (practice varies)
- Older children: open reduction (usually groin incision), then serial spicas; some need pelvic or femoral osteotomy
- Presenting at 3-4 years: open reduction with femoral shortening osteotomy and internal fixation (lateral thigh) or pelvic osteotomy
- Most feared complication: avascular necrosis of the hip

### Preoperative assessment
- Look for other congenital anomalies
- Often the start of many anaesthetics: build rapport with parents
- Blood tests rarely needed unless other disease; transfusion unlikely without pelvic or femoral surgery

### Arthrogram and closed reduction
- Usually infants found on screening; takes about an hour
- Inhalational induction usual; IV access difficult and must be in the upper limbs, so easier once anaesthetised
- Analgesia usually unnecessary; local infiltration if adductor tenotomy
- If open reduction becomes necessary: morphine 100 mcg/kg IV or caudal (1 mL/kg 0.25% levobupivacaine) before starting

### Open reduction
- Usually older children
- Premedication helps, given repeated anaesthetics
- IV access must be in the upper body; some need an internal jugular line, which has morbidity
  - Unless access is easy, gas induction then cannulate under ideal conditions
- Intubation and IPPV usual; older children may have LMA and spontaneous breathing with an epidural
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with nitrous oxide or air; remifentanil infusion reduces volatile needs
- Epidural analgesia common:
  - Up to 10-15 kg: caudal 1 mL/kg of 0.25 levobupivacaine (or equivalent) covers the inguinal dermatomes
  - Older children: lumbar epidural, 0.3-0.5 mL/kg 0.5% levobupivacaine gives a dense block
  - Plus NSAID and morphine 100 mcg/kg
- If an epidural catheter stays in, some units cut a window in the back of the spica

### Hip spica change
- Usually day case: remove spica, wash, new spica, check pelvic X-ray to confirm reduction; takes 40-60 min
- Consider sedative premedication if repeated anaesthetics distress the child
- Infants: intubation and IPPV usual; older children: LMA with spontaneous breathing
- Several position changes: secure ETT or LMA and cannula carefully
- Child is exposed; small infants get cold: warm the theatre and monitor temperature
- Analgesia usually unnecessary

### Postoperative care
- Closed reduction:
  - Eat and drink straight away; paracetamol and NSAID for spica discomfort
  - Vomiting may mean the spica compresses the abdomen: may need an abdominal window
- Open reduction:
  - IV fluids until drinking
  - Epidural infusion, IV morphine infusion, or oral opioid (morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly or codeine phosphate 1 mg/kg 4-hourly), regular or as needed
  - Plus paracetamol and NSAID

## Congenital talipes equinovarus (clubfoot)
### Background
- One of the commonest congenital orthopaedic conditions needing surgery in infancy
- Incidence 1-3:1000 live births (UK); 50% bilateral; male to female 2.5:1
- Most are otherwise healthy; may have neuromuscular disease, arthrogryposis or cerebral palsy; minor lower spine anomalies fairly common
- Bony and joint deformity, subluxation in the foot and muscle imbalance (plantarflexors and invertors versus dorsiflexors and evertors)
  - Forefoot adduction, hindfoot varus and equinus
- Goal: plantigrade foot with reasonable shape and function
- Strapping or serial casts succeed in 30-50% (depending on definition)
- Others: surgery and repeated cast manipulation before walking
  - Primary surgery usually at 3-9 months: from limited medial soft tissue release to extensive posteromedial tendon lengthening or bony surgery +/- K-wires
  - About 25% need later tendon transfers or osteotomies
  - About an hour per foot, supine or prone

### Preoperative assessment
- Check for lower spine abnormality if caudal is planned
- Look for congenital neuromuscular disease

### Anaesthetic technique
- Infants: inhalational induction; older children having secondary surgery: consider premedication, induction as requested
- Upper body IV access where possible (essential for bilateral cases); access may be hard, a few need an internal jugular line
- Infants: intubation and IPPV; armoured ETT if prone
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen and air or nitrous oxide
- Tourniquets may cause hyperthermia (less heat loss below the cuff), especially bilateral and inflated together
  - Do not deflate bilateral tourniquets together, to stagger the release of ischaemic metabolites
- Regional options:
  - Caudal for sacral dermatomes (0.3-0.5 mL/kg 0.5% levobupivacaine or equivalent)
  - Lumbar epidural with catheter
  - Sciatic block; popliteal approach suits this distal surgery
  - Plus perioperative NSAID
- Regional not possible or failed: morphine 100-150 mcg/kg plus NSAID

### Postoperative care
- Maintenance fluids until feeding
- Severe pain after bilateral or extensive surgery: epidural or IV morphine infusion plus NSAID and paracetamol
- Lesser surgery: paracetamol, NSAID and oral opioid

## Femoral osteotomy
### Indications
- Three main ones:
  - Perthes disease: improves femoral head and acetabulum congruity
  - Late DDH: reduces femoral anteversion and, if needed, shortens the overgrown femur to offload the reduced head
  - Cerebral palsy: usually with reduction of a dislocated hip, often plus pelvic osteotomy
  - Various less common indications
- Lateral approach; femur usually fixed with a blade plate
- Bleeding usually modest unless combined with other surgery (e.g. cerebral palsy)

### Preoperative assessment
- Perthes: usually healthy
- Late DDH: many prior procedures, may be anxious or uncooperative; consider premedication; parents may be disheartened after real or perceived screening failure
- Cerebral palsy: respiratory function, regurgitation, weak airway reflexes, epilepsy, medication; give usual drugs on the morning; send group and save

### Anaesthetic technique
- Premedication if needed
- Inhalational induction may be easier in young children or cerebral palsy; older children usually prefer IV
- Intubation and IPPV common; volatile in oxygen and air or nitrous oxide
  - Remifentanil infusion reduces volatile needs and gives moderate hypotension, reducing blood loss
  - Healthy older children (e.g. Perthes, isolated osteotomy): LMA and spontaneous breathing may suit
- Blood loss variable; usually replaced with crystalloid or colloid; transfusion occasionally, especially with pelvic osteotomy
- Single-shot regional options:
  - Caudal in younger children
  - Lumbar epidural
  - Psoas (lumbar plexus) block
  - Three-in-one block (high failure rate for this operation)
  - Fascia iliaca block: high success rate, long-lasting
- Lumbar epidural with postoperative infusion: especially severe cerebral palsy with combined pelvic surgery
  - Postoperative spasm can be very painful
  - Often opioid-sensitive; morphine infusion may cause marked respiratory depression
- Regional not possible or failed: morphine 100-200 mcg/kg or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg; or remifentanil with a morphine bolus before emergence

### Postoperative care
- Maintenance fluids usually overnight
- After single-shot block: IV morphine infusion or PCA ready for when it wears off, plus paracetamol and NSAID
- Epidural catheter: local anaesthetic with opioid infusion in a suitable ward
- IV or epidural analgesia usually needed for 24-72 hours depending on extent

## Pelvic osteotomy
### Indications
- Usually part of dislocated hip treatment:
  - Late DDH, to improve acetabular cover (Salter innominate osteotomy, Pemberton acetabuloplasty, Dega osteotomy)
  - Chiari medial displacement osteotomy: salvage in older children when anatomical reduction is impossible
  - Hip dislocation in severe cerebral palsy (Dega or Pemberton)
- Often combined with hip reduction or femoral osteotomy

### Preoperative assessment
- As for femoral osteotomy (late DDH anxiety and parental concerns; cerebral palsy issues; usual drugs; group and save)

### Anaesthetic technique
- Induction as requested or indicated
- Intubation and IPPV usual
- Urinary catheter helpful in most, especially teenagers (retention) and with epidural infusion; urine output guides volume status
- Significant blood loss: at least two large IV cannulae
  - Poor access (especially cerebral palsy) may need an internal jugular line, which also allows CVP monitoring
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen and air or nitrous oxide; remifentanil infusion reduces volatile and gives moderate hypotension
- TIVA suits older children
- Combined GA and regional: lumbar epidural, continued as postoperative infusion
- No epidural: generous morphine
- Transfusion sometimes needed in cerebral palsy (combined surgery, heavy loss, possible preoperative anaemia)
- Prone to hypothermia in long cases despite warming mattress, forced air and fluid warmer

### Postoperative care
- Continue forced-air warming (Bair Hugger) in recovery if cold
- Maintenance fluids for 24-48 hours
- FBC and U&E after significant blood loss or transfusion
- Epidural analgesia of choice for most, for 48-72 hours, with paracetamol and regular NSAID if no contraindication
- After epidural: oral codeine phosphate 1 mg/kg 4-hourly or morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly

## Epiphysiodesis
- Fusion of the growth plate to correct limb length inequality; ablated with drills and curettes under image intensifier
- Small incisions: good cosmesis, little skin pain (local anaesthetic injection controls it)
- Bleeding from exposed cancellous bone can be heavy; surgeon may apply procoagulant foam

## Tendon surgery
- Corrects muscle imbalance in joint deformity and/or improves gait
  - Transfer: part or all of a tendon detached from its insertion, keeping nerve and blood supply, and reattached elsewhere
  - Lengthening: two offset tendon cuts
  - Release (tenotomy): tendon fully detached
- Cast afterwards holds the joint position and limits movement, reducing (often large) analgesic needs
- Common sites:
  - Hip via groin incision, supine (e.g. adductor release for scissoring gait)
  - Hamstrings via posterior thigh incision, prone or lateral
  - Ankle (e.g. tibialis posterior transfer for foot drop), supine
- Painful postoperative muscle spasm is common

## Limb lengthening (Ilizarov)
- Preserves height in limb length inequality from unequal growth, trauma or osteomyelitis
- Long, painful process: corticotomy, distraction and external fixation
- Surgery long (typically 10 h or more) and technically hard
  - Fixator tightened periodically: about 1 cm of new bone per month of treatment
- Transient nerve palsies possible: document pre-existing deficits carefully
- High risk of compartment syndrome, especially tibial lengthening
- Very painful, but neuraxial blocks often avoided to allow monitoring of nerve and vascular recovery and compartment syndrome
  - High-dose opioid by PCA or nurse-controlled analgesia may be best; discuss a short NSAID course with surgeons
- A child with a previous painful fixator may need an epidural to agree to surgery

## Syndactyly
### Background
- Congenital failure of fingers (or toes) to separate; two or more digits
- Complete if fused to the tips; complex if phalanges fused, simple if not
- Incidence 1:2500; male to female 2:1

### Causes
- Failure of differentiation in the first trimester
- Family history in up to 50%
- Apert syndrome: craniosynostosis, midface hypoplasia, cleft palate, small nares, syndactyly, learning difficulty in 30%
- Epidermolysis bullosa
- Polydactyly
- Poland syndrome: one-sided absent or small pectoralis plus syndactyly of the same-side hand

### Treatment
- Early repair around 12 months gives the best look and function
- Complex syndactyly usually needs several staged operations
- Full thickness skin graft often needed, usually from the thigh
- Digits may be pinned to prevent contracture
- Morbidity: scarring and contractures needing revision, infection

### Preoperative assessment
- Previous anaesthetic history; syndromes (especially Apert) with airway difficulty
- Sedative premedication unless airway concerns (repeated procedures)

### Anaesthetic technique
- Usually toddlers with difficult IV access: inhalational induction common
- Apert syndrome: possible difficult airway
- LMA or ETT; ETT preferred for long complex cases
- Spontaneous ventilation or IPPV both fine
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen and air or nitrous oxide
- Tourniquet

### Analgesia
- Opioid: morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV
- Brachial plexus block for complex repairs
- Either way, NSAID improves analgesia (e.g. diclofenac 1 mg/kg rectally)

### Postoperative care
- Maintenance fluids until drinking
- Paracetamol, NSAID and oral opioid (e.g. codeine phosphate 1 mg/kg 4-hourly)
- Another GA likely about a week later for suture removal and dressing change

## Flashcards
- Q: Tourniquet pressure for a child's lower limb?
  - A: 150 mm Hg above systolic
- Q: Caudal dose for open hip reduction in a child up to 10-15 kg?
  - A: 1 mL/kg 0.25% levobupivacaine
- Q: Risk of compartment syndrome after Ilizarov limb lengthening?
  - A: About 2%, distal to the operative site
- Q: Treatment of acute postoperative muscle spasm in children?
  - A: Pain control plus IV benzodiazepine (diazepam 0.1 mg/kg or midazolam 10-30 mcg/kg/h); baclofen or dantrolene
- Q: Why stagger deflation of bilateral tourniquets?
  - A: To stage exposure to metabolites from the ischaemic limbs
