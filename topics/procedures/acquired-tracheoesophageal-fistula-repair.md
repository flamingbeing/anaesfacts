# Acquired Tracheo-oesophageal Fistula Repair

The main danger is aspiration: avoid positive pressure until the fistula is isolated and place the tube cuff beyond it under bronchoscopic guidance.

## Key points
- **Avoid positive pressure ventilation until the fistula is isolated; place the cuff distal to it under bronchoscopic guidance (consider one-lung ventilation)**
  - Similar principles to bronchopleural fistula repair
- Head up, avoid suxamethonium, avoid cricoid pressure
- Patients may have aspiration pneumonitis and poor nutrition
- **Aspiration is the main issue**

## Causes
- Iatrogenic: after oesophageal or tracheal surgery
- Malignancy: 50%, so the commonest cause
  - Usually oesophageal tumours
  - Median survival from diagnosis 1-6weeks
- Trauma:
  - ETT cuff after prolonged ventilation (tracheostomy does not change the incidence)
  - Road traffic crashes: trachea and oesophagus crushed against the spine, usually near the carina
- Infection

## Presentation and investigations
- Chronic aspiration pneumonitis
- Ono's sign: uncontrollable coughing after swallowing, especially fizzy drinks
- **Accurate localisation is the key to management**:
  - **CT of thorax and neck**
  - Barium swallow
  - **Endoscopy**: methylene blue leaking into the bronchoscopic view
  - **Bronchoscopy**: also allows bronchoalveolar lavage

## Initial management
- Head of bed up
- PPI to reduce gastric volume and acidity
- **Consider an NG tube** or a draining **gastrostomy**
- If already intubated: keep peak pressures low and aim to wean and extubate before surgery
  - Positive pressure ventilation is linked to tracheal anastomotic leak and stenosis
- Definitive options:
  - **Repair**: oesophagus sutured over an NG tube
  - **Stenting**: used in cancer, usually under sedation; self-expanding metal stents are well tolerated

## Preoperative assessment
- Isolate the defect to stop further soiling of the airway
- Major surgery: optimise cardiorespiratory function first
  - Find and treat pneumonia
  - Optimise nutrition

## Intraoperative management
- Principles:
  - **Avoid positive pressure**: awake fibreoptic intubation or RSI; paralysis with positive pressure inflates the stomach, causing aspiration or splinting the diaphragm
  - **Cuff distal to the fistula**
  - **Head up** to limit passive aspiration
- Airway plan depends on fistula level:
  - **Proximal** (most common): above the level of the ETT cuff
    - **Awake fibreoptic intubation**: advance the ETT under bronchoscopic vision until the **cuff is below the fistula**
    - **RSI** may be enough if a proximal fistula is confirmed
      - **Rocuronium** rather than suxamethonium, as fasciculations raise gastric pressure
      - **Avoid cricoid pressure** (effective against an internal fistula)
      - Bronchoscope to position the cuff below the fistula
  - **Distal or carinal**: complicates standard intubation
    - **One-lung ventilation with isolation**: endobronchial intubation or a **double-lumen tube**
- Spontaneous ventilation is possible but hard to sustain over many hours of surgery
- Arterial line
- Leak test at the end: withdraw the ETT so the cuff sits above the repair

## Postoperative care
- Minimise positive pressure ventilation; aim to extubate at the end
- Avoid high airway pressures from coughing that stress the suture line

## Oesophageal stenting
- Often palliative
- Can be done under monitored anaesthesia care

## Airway stenting
- If needed, **stent the airway before the oesophagus**, so the oesophageal stent cannot compress the airway
- Rigid bronchoscopy needs GA
- Spontaneous ventilation possible but hard to maintain
- High-frequency jet ventilation
