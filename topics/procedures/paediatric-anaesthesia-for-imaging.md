# Paediatric Anaesthesia for Imaging

Anaesthetising children for CT and MRI: a remote site with unfamiliar staff and kit, limited access to the patient, radiation, magnetism, cold and contrast reactions.

## Setting
- Purpose-built suites (piped gases, induction and recovery areas, integrated monitors, radiology-ready machines) are used for elective lists such as MRI
- Many elective scans are done under sedation without an anaesthetist
- Occasional unusual studies happen in unfamiliar places with poorer kit and staff not used to anaesthetised children: remote-site risks plus complex patients
- Talk to radiology staff beforehand about:
  - The planned procedure and its duration
  - Position: supine, prone or lateral
  - Induction in one room and transfer to another?
  - Moving the patient during the procedure?
  - Access to airway and IV cannula during scanning
  - Head or feet first into the scanner
  - What is done: risks, stimulating or not, contrast or heparin, and who gives it
  - Likely after-effects: PONV, pain, bleeding from puncture or biopsy site, cardiovascular or respiratory problems, reactions to contrast
- Oxygen often from a cylinder: have a spare
- Dedicated anaesthetic suction
- Resuscitation trolley and defibrillator present; know the drill for getting the child out in an emergency
- Cardiac arrest: usually better to move the patient to the induction room; know the protocol
- Never let the resuscitation team into the MRI room

## Staff and equipment
- Anaesthetic assistant and recovery nurse, as for any GA
- Radiology staff may help move larger children on and off the table
- Induction, maintenance and recovery equipment must meet standards and be familiar to the team

## Patients
- Assess as for any GA, with attention to syndromes and other illnesses
- CNS investigations are common; these children often have cardiovascular or respiratory conditions
- Assess the airway carefully

## Radiation
- Modern shielded, coned equipment: negligible exposure beyond >2 m from the source
- Still minimise total exposure: lead apron, thyroid shield, exposure badge if exposed often; follow radiographers' advice
- If the patient is properly monitored, step away and watch from behind leaded glass
- Shield the patient's testes, ovaries, thyroid and eyes where possible

## Patient access
- Limited space, fixed tables and equipment, dark rooms and many staff can keep anaesthetic kit away from the patient
- Long breathing circuits and IV lines snag and dislodge easily
- Ask for a trial run of the table into the scanner to judge the slack needed

## Temperature
- Superconducting MRI magnets need cooling, so rooms are cold
- Measure temperature for all but the shortest scans
- Warming blankets can degrade images

## Iodinated contrast reactions
- Dose-dependent (from the agent's physicochemical properties): heat, pain, vasodilation, cardiac depression, hypotension
- Dose-independent: nausea and vomiting, hypersensitivity
  - Minor: flushing, nausea and vomiting, itch, urticaria, arm pain
  - Moderate: severe urticaria, facial oedema, hypotension, bronchospasm
  - Severe: shock, laryngeal oedema, convulsions, cardiac arrest
- Most reactions minor; moderate 1%, severe 0.1% of administrations
- Atopy doubles the incidence
- Mostly anaphylactoid; true anaphylaxis rare
- Mortality 1:75000
- Low-osmolar agents cause fewer reactions; use them for all intra-arterial injections (less pain)

## Computed tomography (CT)
- Rotating X-ray source and detector build a 2D image of a thin cross-section from many angles
- Separates structures of different density (bone, muscle) well; similar densities less well
- Fast: head scans often <30 sec; spiral CT table moves fast too
  - Check the full range of table travel; make sure circuit and IV extensions are long enough
- Any anaesthetic and monitoring equipment can be used
- Relatively high radiation doses: monitor from the control room if possible
- Patients range from well, fasted infants (e.g. congenital cystic adenomatoid malformation of the lung) to trauma patients being resuscitated
- Head and airway access limited, especially for head scans: secure the airway before scanning
- Keep IV sites visible and reachable
- Keep metal leads (e.g. ECG cables) out of the scan field (artefact)
- Enough staff for a controlled transfer from trolley to table
- Agree position and contrast (dose, route) in advance; check for iodine allergy

### CT anaesthetic technique
- Many elective cases are infants: inhalational induction often easier than IV
- LMA sometimes suitable
- ETT often better: small child with limited access, or chest CT where brief apnoea in inspiration is requested (easier intubated and paralysed)
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide, or propofol infusion
  - Syringe pumps are small and can sit on the scan table with the patient and monitor
- Head injury with reduced consciousness: IV rapid sequence induction

### CT recovery
- Elective cases recover fast, feed and go home within 1-2 hours
- Emergencies go to theatre, ITU or HDU depending on scan and clinical state

## Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
- Principle
  - In a strong field, some hydrogen nuclei (from water) align with it while spinning (resonance)
  - Radio waves at the spin frequency add energy, tipping them to 90° to the field
  - When the radio pulse stops, they relax back into line and release energy, detected by a coil
  - Repeated many times; signals are assembled into an image
  - Tissues relax at different rates, so they look different
- Field from a superconducting magnet, always on: wire cooled by liquid helium at -269°C has no resistance, allowing a huge current

### Field strength
- 0.5 to 1.5 Tesla (T); 1 Tesla = 10000 Gauss
- Earth's field: 0.3-0.7 Gauss, so the magnet is about 10000 times stronger
- Any ferromagnetic material is affected
- Below 0.5 mT (5 Gauss) effect considered minimal; this line should be marked in the room
- No magnetic material (including syringe pumps) closer than the 0.5 mT line

### MRI equipment
- All equipment must be labelled MR safe or MR compatible: trolleys, oxygen cylinders for ward transfer, laryngoscopes
- MR safe: no risk to patient or staff, but may malfunction or degrade the image
- MR compatible: MR safe, works normally and does not affect the image

### MRI monitoring and hazards
- Usual minimum monitoring standards
- Cables: padded or kept off the skin, never coiled (burn risk)
- MR-compatible fibreoptic oximeter cables, temperature probes and carbon fibre ECG electrodes exist
- Long capnography tubing delays the reading
- ECG unreliable: field-induced currents in the heart and great vessels show as artefact
- Alarms may be missed over scanner noise, especially with ear defenders
- Many anaesthetists stay outside with a slave monitor and watch through a window
- Noise around 85-90 decibels: ear defenders on the child once asleep, and on staff in the room
- Temperature
  - Cold room from magnet cooling: real risk of heat loss
  - Forced-air warmers can interfere with the radiofrequency signal
  - Radiofrequency energy heats tissue (like short-wave diathermy); small infants may even gain heat
  - Monitor temperature in neonates, infants and long scans
- Contrast: paramagnetic agents (most often gadolinium DTPA); few reactions (nausea and vomiting, injection pain); anaphylaxis rare but reported
- Projectiles: ferromagnetic objects accelerate towards the strongest point of the field
  - Checklist for patients, parents and staff to exclude metal implants (vascular clips, coils, stents, pacemakers, implants)
  - Normal laryngoscopes and ferromagnetic oxygen cylinders are particular risks
- Quench: sudden large helium release (deliberate shutdown or fault); rising helium makes the room hypoxic
- The MRI unit must manage its own emergencies: CPR, anaphylaxis, anaesthetic complications

### MRI preoperative assessment
- Watch for conditions or syndromes with specific anaesthetic concerns
- MRI is not stimulating apart from noise and the enclosed space
- Most older, cooperative children are scanned awake with music and a parent present
- Anaesthesia or sedation for those too young to lie still, or whose condition limits cooperation

### MRI anaesthetic technique
- Inhalational or IV induction
- ETT or LMA by age, reflux risk, respiratory function and ICP
- Usually spontaneous ventilation; IPPV for neonates, poor respiratory function or raised ICP
- Maintenance: volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide; or propofol infusion (with or without a formal airway): easy to titrate, quick offset, may reduce nausea

### MRI recovery
- Elective cases recover fast, feed and go home within 1-2 hours

## Flashcards
- Q: How common are moderate and severe reactions to iodinated contrast?
  - A: Moderate 1%, severe 0.1%; atopy doubles the incidence; mortality 1:75000.
- Q: What is the 5 Gauss line in MRI?
  - A: 0.5 mT boundary; no magnetic material (including syringe pumps) should come closer.
- Q: What is an MRI quench and why is it dangerous?
  - A: Sudden release of helium from the superconductor; makes the room hypoxic.
- Q: Difference between MR safe and MR compatible?
  - A: MR safe poses no risk but may malfunction or degrade the image; MR compatible is safe, works normally and does not affect the image.
- Q: When is IPPV used for paediatric MRI?
  - A: Neonates, poor respiratory function or raised ICP.
