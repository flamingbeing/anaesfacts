# Cataract Surgery (Phacoemulsification)

Short, minor eye operation, usually under topical or regional anaesthesia with light sedation; the patient must lie flat, keep still and follow instructions.

## Overview
- Short; minimal sedation, hold the patient's hand
- Technique: MAC with propofol, or peribulbar block if not cooperative, or GA
  - May need fentanyl for the block injection, or if LA is not working
  - Oxygen via "airplane" nasal prongs
- Patients: usually elderly with multiple comorbidities
- Position: supine
- Antibiotics: none
- Duration: 15-30min
- Blood loss: minimal
- Complication: IOL dislocation
- Pain: minimal; oral paracetamol in PACU if needed
- Postoperative: protect the eye; review clinic next day
- Laser cases: go to the laser machine first without monitors (5mins), then to the OT table with monitors
- Intracapsular cataract extraction (ICCE) is an older variant
- May be combined with other procedures, e.g. pterygium excision (longer)

## Surgical steps
- With or without block; iodine drops into the eye while the surgeon scrubs
- Patient closes eye; skin cleaned; drape; Tegaderm over eye; hole cut; eye retracted
- Table raised to the microscope; more LA
- With or without trypan blue; viscoelastic
- Corneal incision and injection, capsulorhexis, capsule separation, nucleus breakdown, cortical clean-up
- IOL implantation, viscoelastic removed
- Wound closed by hydrating the corneal incision; checked for leak
- Topical antibiotic drops

## Main considerations
- Can be done under topical drops or regional block, but some steps hurt (block, eye retraction, instruments)
  - Suits sedation and well-timed analgesia
- Patient must keep head and body still and follow instructions (e.g. look in different directions)
  - Light sedation for anxiolysis only; never deep sedation
  - Talk to the patient to find why they cannot keep still (pain, anxiety, poor understanding)
- Supine and flat, head away from the anaesthetist
  - Must be able to lie flat physiologically
- Head under drapes: risk of CO2 rebreathing
- Avoid forced-air warmers: may blow lint into the eye

## Preoperative assessment
- Suitability for topical or regional technique with MAC
  - Communication and cooperation: must understand and obey commands to avoid all movement
  - Anything stopping comfortable supine lying: congestive heart failure, severe COPD, severe back pain, claustrophobia, itch, chronic cough
- Anticoagulants and antiplatelets (including DAPT): no need to stop
  - High thrombotic or embolic risk: therapeutic aspirin, clopidogrel, NOACs and warfarin usually continued throughout

## Positioning
- Head at or above heart level, to avoid raised ocular venous pressure and worse bleeding

## Topical, regional and sedation (MAC)
- Topical analgesia is the commonest technique
  - Hydrodissection pressure can be uncomfortable and is not covered by topical alone
- Regional options: peribulbar (extraconal), retrobulbar (intraconal), sub-Tenon block
- Sedation aims to reduce anxiety, not to produce unconsciousness
  - Oversedation causes restlessness, confusion, unresponsiveness or airway obstruction, and loss of cooperation
  - Even small movements are magnified under the microscope and can injure the eye
  - Eye pain: ask the surgeon for more LA; never use heavy sedation in place of analgesia
  - Options: midazolam, small doses of propofol (e.g. 10 to 20 mg), dexmedetomidine
- Analgesia
  - LA or regional block usually enough
  - Short-acting opioid (fentanyl, remifentanil) just before the block to cover needle and injection pain
- Supplemental oxygen via "airplane" nasal prongs
  - Gives ETCO2 monitoring
  - Gas flow reduces CO2 rebreathing under drapes

## General anaesthesia
- Uncommon for cataract surgery
- Indications
  - Cannot communicate or cooperate: neurocognitive dysfunction, severe anxiety, claustrophobia
  - Cannot keep still: e.g. tremor disorders such as Parkinson disease
  - Cannot lie flat comfortably: severe symptomatic heart failure, COPD, back pain
  - Most children
- Keep a deep plane to prevent coughing or movement (eye injury, raised IOP)
- ETT or LMA; watch for LMA displacement, as in any facial surgery

## Flashcards
- Q: Typical duration of phacoemulsification?
  - A: 15-30min
- Q: Propofol dose for sedation during cataract surgery?
  - A: Small doses, e.g. 10 to 20 mg
- Q: Should anticoagulants or DAPT be stopped for cataract surgery?
  - A: No, usually continued
- Q: What should be done if the patient has eye pain under sedation?
  - A: Ask the surgeon for more LA; do not deepen sedation
- Q: Why use "airplane" nasal prongs?
  - A: Oxygen, ETCO2 monitoring and less CO2 rebreathing under drapes
