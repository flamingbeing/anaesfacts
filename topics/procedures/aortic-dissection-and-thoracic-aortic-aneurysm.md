# Aortic Dissection and Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm Repair

Two main thoracic aortic pathologies, aneurysm and dissection: anti-impulse control before surgery, multiple arterial lines, brain and spinal cord protection, and heavy bleeding.

## Thoracic aortic aneurysm
- Definition: >50% increase in diameter
  - Normal size varies with aortic segment, age and sex
  - Usually about 5cm
- True aneurysm involves all 3 layers of the wall
- Causes
  - Mainly degenerative (hypertension, smoking, COPD)
  - Bicuspid aortic valve
  - Connective tissue disease
    - Marfan syndrome: fibrillin-1 mutation; high risk of root dilatation
    - Vascular Ehlers-Danlos: type III procollagen mutation; high risk of spontaneous rupture
    - Loeys-Dietz: TGF-β signalling mutation; aggressive arterial disease

## Aortic dissection
- Ascending dissection said to be x2-3 more common than AAA
- Untreated mortality 1-2%/hr over the first 48hrs
- Anatomy
  - Ascending: LVOT to innominate artery
  - Arch: in between
  - Descending: after the left subclavian artery

## Classification
- Timing
  - Acute: diagnosed within 2 weeks of symptom onset
  - Chronic: >2 weeks since onset
- Related syndromes: intramural haemorrhage, intramural haematoma, aortic ulcer
- DeBakey
  - Type I: ascending, arch and descending (most common; tear usually <10cm from the aortic valve)
  - Type II: ascending only
  - Type III: descending, distal to the left subclavian
    - IIIa: down to the diaphragm
    - IIIb: below the diaphragm
- Stanford
  - Type A: ascending (may extend to arch and descending), i.e. DeBakey I and II
  - Type B: descending only, i.e. DeBakey III

## Causes and risk factors
- 2 origins
  - Intimal tear (most common)
  - Haemorrhage within the media that then breaks through the intima
- Risk factors: hypertension, aortic dilatation, connective tissue disease (e.g. Marfan)

## Clinical features
- Type A: sudden severe sharp chest pain
- Type B: similar, but back and abdominal pain more common
- Examination
  - At the tear: tachycardia, hypertension
  - Later with rupture: tachycardia, hypotension
  - Unequal arm pulses (poor sign); arm BP higher than leg BP
  - New aortic regurgitation murmur
  - Syncope, stroke or neurological deficit

## Investigations
- ECG: 20% of dissections show ischaemic changes
- Imaging
  - CXR: wide mediastinum, prominent aortic knuckle, haemothorax, apical cap
  - CT angiography
  - MRI
  - TTE sees only the aortic root; TOE gives better views of arch and descending aorta
  - Aortography: gold standard

## Initial management
- Anti-impulse therapy: control BP and HR to reduce pulsatile load on the wall (lower dP/dt)
  - SBP target 110-120: lowers absolute wall stress (Laplace's law)
  - HR target 60–80 bpm: fewer pulses hitting the tear each minute
  - Labetalol ideal (alpha and beta blocker)
- Analgesia
- Bleeding: massive transfusion protocol; little room for permissive hypotension because the false lumen may already limit visceral flow
- Surgery
  - Type A: urgent surgery as soon as possible; aortic valve and root replacement with coronary reimplantation is common
  - Type B: only for persistent pain, aneurysm expansion, peripheral ischaemia or rupture; otherwise surgery has not beaten medical treatment

## Type A repairs
- Simple ascending procedures
  - Routine cannulation (right atrium or femoral artery, and ascending aorta); no circulatory arrest
  - Wheat procedure: aortic valve and ascending aorta replaced, root spared
  - Ross procedure: diseased aortic valve replaced by the patient's own pulmonary valve (autograft); a cadaveric pulmonary homograft replaces the pulmonary valve
- Root procedures
  - Coronary reimplantation: risk of kinking or air emboli; the anastomoses can bleed and are hard to reach
  - Bentall: aortic valve replacement plus reimplantation of coronary buttons
  - Valve-sparing root replacement, e.g. David procedure (native valve reimplanted into the new root)
- Hemiarch replacement (proximal arch)
  - Needs deep hypothermic circulatory arrest and NIRS
  - Bilateral radial arterial lines: right radial reflects brain perfusion pressure; left radial shows whether the left carotid or subclavian is perfused or clamped
  - Axillary artery cannulation for antegrade cerebral perfusion while the arch is open
  - Coagulopathy
- Frozen elephant trunk (total arch plus distal support)
  - Hybrid: total arch replacement with a stented endovascular graft
  - Arch part: deep hypothermic circulatory arrest; even longer bypass than hemiarch; expect coagulopathy
  - Stent part: spinal cord protection with a spinal drain; femoral arterial line to confirm lower body perfusion during partial bypass

## Frozen elephant trunk: anaesthetic management
### Preoperative
- Emergency dissection
  - Anti-impulse: SBP <120, HR <60
  - Bleeding: massive transfusion protocol
  - Consider a PA introducer sheath or rapid infusion catheter
- Previous cardiac surgery: more blood loss, external defibrillator pads needed
- Document baseline neurology

### Monitoring and access
- 2x SpO2 probes
- 2-3 arterial lines (male-to-male Luer connector to share a transducer)
  - Right radial: pressure proximal to clamp or stent (brachiocephalic origin); reflects brain perfusion via axillary cannulation
  - Left radial: in case the innominate is dissected, or to see if the left carotid or subclavian is pinched by clamp or stent
  - Femoral: distal perfusion during clamping
  - Dissection or clamps can distort arterial readings
- 5 transducers
  - 2-3 arterial (can share)
  - CVP
  - Spinal drain
  - Antegrade perfusion pressure: can share with CVP, as CVP is irrelevant once on full bypass or arrest
- NIRS
- TEE
- Large-bore IV
- Large-bore dialysis-type catheter (Mahurkar)
- TEG in theatre; fibrinogen and PCC available

### Induction
- Keep SBP <120 or rupture may follow
- Tranexamic acid

### TEE goals
- Aortic valve: is a Bentall needed
- Confirm true rather than false lumen cannulation
- LV function and regional wall motion during coronary reimplantation
- Distal stent position

### Bypass
- Bilateral axillary artery cannulation, with partial heparinisation (5000IU)
- Bypass may be long
- Circulatory arrest at 25 degrees
  - Selective antegrade cerebral perfusion
  - Cold packs around the head during arrest; remove on rewarming
- Steep head-down tilt to let air out of the aortic graft

### After bypass
- Bleeding control guided by TEG
- Aim for normotension: hypertension stresses the grafts, hypotension risks spinal cord ischaemia
- Dissection may involve renal and mesenteric vessels: watch kidney and gut perfusion

## Open thoracic aneurysm repair
- Left thoracotomy: needs one-lung ventilation for access

### Preoperative
- CT angiography: gold standard for anatomy and distal extent
- Usually elderly with many comorbidities
  - Cardiac risk: ECG, TTE; avoid invasive coronary angiography in the acute phase unless essential
  - Lung function tests to stratify thoracotomy and one-lung ventilation risk
  - CT thorax or CXR: large aneurysms can compress the trachea or distort the left main bronchus, making intubation or a left DLT difficult

### Intraoperative management
- GA with DLT for one-lung ventilation
- Keep anti-impulse control: SBP <120, HR<60
- 2 arterial lines
  - Right radial: pressure proximal to clamp or stent
  - Femoral: distal perfusion during clamping
- TEE, NIRS, with or without SSEP and MEP
- CSF drainage to improve spinal cord perfusion
- Cross-clamp effects are bigger (suprarenal, supracoeliac): expect haemodynamic and metabolic changes
- Consider distal perfusion: passive (Gott) shunt, left heart bypass (left atrium to femoral), or circulatory arrest if the arch is involved

### Postoperative care
- ICU
- Watch for renal impairment, spinal cord injury, mesenteric ischaemia, peripheral VTE

## Distal perfusion techniques
1. Simple cross-clamping
   - Proximal and distal clamps, no circuit
   - Pros: simplest and fastest; no anticoagulation or cannulation problems
   - Cons: severe renal, visceral and spinal ischaemia; risk of permanent organ damage rises sharply beyond 30 minutes of clamp time
   - Large rise in LV afterload and proximal hypertension
2. Passive shunt (e.g. Gott)
   - Large heparin-bonded tube from proximal aorta (or left subclavian) to distal aorta or femoral artery
   - Driven by the patient's own BP; lowers LV afterload
   - Must carry >60% of baseline aortic flow
   - Hard to secure; bleeding, dislodgement, air embolism, stroke, vessel injury; now rarely used
3. Atriofemoral (left heart) bypass
   - Most common: oxygenated blood drained from the left atrium, returned by centrifugal pump to the left common femoral artery
   - Offloads the LV (less preload and afterload, lower myocardial O2 demand)
   - Keeps kidney, gut and spinal cord perfused during clamping
   - Moderate heparinisation (ACT 150–200s)
   - Less need for vasodilators such as SNP
   - Flow usually 2–3 L/min, titrated to distal MAP 60–70 mmHg
   - Preferred with poor LV function, renal disease or expected long clamp time
4. Partial cardiopulmonary bypass
   - Femoral vein or right atrium to oxygenator, back to the femoral artery
   - Full heparinisation (ACT >400s)
   - Distal mean pressure 50–60 mmHg
   - Risk of pulmonary oedema if the LV is not vented or is volume-overloaded
5. Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest
   - Cooling then complete stop of circulation; used when a proximal clamp is unsafe (e.g. arch involvement)
   - Protection by lower metabolic rate
   - Safe window usually 30–40 minutes
   - Bloodless field; no clamp injury to the aortic wall

## Flashcards
- Q: Anti-impulse targets in acute dissection?
  - A: SBP 110-120 and HR 60–80 bpm (frozen elephant trunk section: SBP <120, HR <60); labetalol ideal
- Q: Untreated mortality of acute aortic dissection?
  - A: 1-2%/hr over the first 48hrs
- Q: Why bilateral radial arterial lines in arch surgery?
  - A: Right radial reflects brain perfusion; left radial shows if the left carotid or subclavian is clamped or pinched
- Q: Left heart bypass flow and target distal MAP?
  - A: 2–3 L/min, distal MAP 60–70 mmHg, ACT 150–200s
- Q: Usual safe window for deep hypothermic circulatory arrest?
  - A: 30–40 minutes
