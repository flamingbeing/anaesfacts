# Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery

Shared, often bleeding airway with nasal intubation and throat pack; major cases add airway oedema, blood loss and jaw wiring.

## Summary
- Simple OMS: shared airway; nasal intubation and throat pack
- Major OMS: airway oedema; bleeding risk
- Intraoral abscess: trismus and airway obstruction, which can worsen after surgery

## General principles
- Issues: shared airway, throat pack, blood in the airway
- Intubation:
  - Nasal intubation often needed (check nostril patency, epistaxis risk, anticoagulation; co-phenylcaine)
  - North-facing nasal tubes, prewarmed
  - Throat pack
  - May be a difficult airway needing awake fibreoptic intubation (AFOI)
- Extubation: remove throat pack; consider inspecting the airway under direct vision
- Bradyarrhythmias from traction on the maxilla (trigeminovagal reflex)
  - Worse with light anaesthesia, hypercarbia or hypoxia
  - Prevention: deepen anaesthesia; local anaesthetic blocks the afferent limb; pretreat with atropine or glycopyrrolate

## Orthognathic surgery (maxillary and mandibular osteotomy)
### Surgery
- Cutting and repositioning the mandible, maxilla or both to correct lower facial skeletal disproportion
  - Le Fort I: named after the fracture pattern; corrects maxillary deformity
  - Bilateral sagittal split osteotomy (BSSO): corrects mandibular deformity
  - May need bone grafting
- Indications:
  - Cosmetic correction of malocclusion
  - Obstructive sleep apnoea (bimaxillary advancement)
  - Congenital head and neck conditions

### Key considerations
- Airway: nasal intubation, throat pack, good suction; dexamethasone; high dependency care for airway monitoring
- Bleeding: group and crossmatch, controlled hypotension, tranexamic acid
- Postoperative intermaxillary fixation (IMF): TIVA, 2 antiemetics

### Preoperative assessment
- May have major craniofacial abnormality: assess the airway carefully
- Valid group and crossmatch: bleeding can be heavy

### Intraoperative management
- Supine, head up, head tilted
- Airway:
  - Nasal ETT with IPPV
    - Alternatives: oral tube brought out behind the molars or through the floor of the mouth; tracheostomy
  - Severe malocclusion: consider AFOI
  - Surgery can damage the ETT, which may need replacing
- TIVA with remifentanil
  - Less PONV, which is vital to avoid
  - Remifentanil gives quickly titratable analgesia
- Can bleed a lot:
  - Large-bore IV access
  - Tranexamic acid
  - Permissive hypotension
  - Arterial line
- High-dose dexamethasone
- Large volumes of local anaesthetic with adrenaline may be injected for pain and bleeding:
  - Dental mix contains adrenaline 1:80,000
  - Give no more than 300 mcg adrenaline per hour
  - Tachycardia and rising MAP may follow injections
  - Surgeon may also do mandibular or maxillary nerve blocks
- Long operation
- Graft site: iliac crest or rib
  - Rib graft can cause pneumothorax
- Risk of dural tear

### Postoperative care
- Airway oedema: usually extubated at the end unless surgery was long or involved extensive pharyngeal mucosal handling
  - High-dose dexamethasone
  - Consider a cuff leak test
  - Extubate fully awake, able to protect the airway
  - High-risk patients stay intubated in ICU for 24-48 hours; may need a nasogastric tube
- Intermaxillary fixation (jaws wired together) may be applied
  - Rigid wiring means the patient cannot open the mouth
  - Prevent PONV and bleeding aggressively to avoid airway obstruction
  - If the airway obstructs, release quickly: note wire positions before extubation, keep wire cutters at the bedside
  - Elastic band fixation carries less risk
- Nasopharyngeal airway helps maintain the airway and gives a suction route
- Definitely high dependency care afterwards; consider surgical ICU for the airway

## Facial trauma
### Preoperative assessment
- Assess the airway carefully together with the surgeon

### Intraoperative management
- Pain should fall once fractures are fixed
- Airway:
  - Manage emergent airway compromise as a trauma airway
  - Nasal intubation is standard for access to the occlusion, but contraindicated with suspected base of skull fracture or CSF leak (risk of intracranial passage)
  - Mask seal and bag-mask ventilation hard due to pain and deformity: RSI usually needed
  - Trismus often due to pain and resolves with neuromuscular blockade
- Dexamethasone to limit swelling
- Antibiotics: Augmentin
- Reflex bradycardia when the zygomatic fracture is elevated

### Mandibular fractures
- Jaw may be wired shut (intermaxillary fixation): manage as above

### Extubation
- Risk of oedema and haematoma
- Cuff leak test versus staying intubated

### Postoperative care
- Airway swelling can worsen for 48 hours
- Watch for haematoma; sutures or clips may need removing at once

## Intraoral abscess surgery
### Preoperative assessment
- Possible difficult airway or airway obstruction
  - Clinical signs:
    - Trismus
    - Obstruction: stridor (may be absent at rest), orthopnoea (cannot lie flat), dysphagia (pooled secretions)
  - Airway examination:
    - Mouth opening
    - Oropharyngeal swelling
    - Tongue protrusion: sensitive sign of sublingual involvement (e.g. Ludwig angina)
  - Investigations:
    - CT neck: extent into deep spaces (submandibular, sublingual, parapharyngeal)
    - Flexible nasendoscopy: epiglottic and supraglottic narrowing or deviation
- Urgent, especially with airway obstruction
- Often septic

### Intraoperative management
- AFOI if difficult airway
  - Trismus from pain may improve with relaxants, but not if due to structural limitation such as inflammation: low threshold for AFOI if unsure
- Yankauer suction ready: abscess may rupture during airway manipulation
- High-dose dexamethasone for airway swelling (not immediate)

### Extubation and postoperative care
- Awake extubation
- Airway oedema may worsen after surgery, especially Ludwig angina or parapharyngeal spread
  - Any worrying sign: keep intubated and go to ICU
- NSAIDs for pain

### Ludwig angina
- Life-threatening, fast-spreading cellulitis of the floor of the mouth involving both submandibular and sublingual spaces
- Airway problems:
  - Obstruction: oedema lifts the floor of mouth and pushes the tongue back, rapidly closing the pharynx
  - Difficult airway; inability to protrude the tongue predicts sublingual involvement
  - Difficult front-of-neck access due to hard, swollen pretracheal tissue
    - Risk of spreading infection to the mediastinum

## Flashcards
- Q: Maximum adrenaline dose with dental local anaesthetic mix in orthognathic surgery?
  - A: 300 mcg per hour (mix is 1:80,000)
- Q: When is nasal intubation contraindicated in facial trauma?
  - A: Suspected base of skull fracture or CSF leak
- Q: Mechanism of bradycardia during maxillary traction, and prevention?
  - A: Trigeminovagal reflex; deepen anaesthesia, local anaesthetic, atropine or glycopyrrolate
- Q: How long may high-risk orthognathic patients stay intubated in ICU?
  - A: 24-48 hours
- Q: Which trismus does not improve with neuromuscular blockade?
  - A: Structural trismus such as from inflammation
