# Upper Limb Vascular Surgery and Revascularisation

Upper limb ischaemia is rarer than lower limb disease; its special concern is cerebral perfusion.

## Chronic upper limb ischaemia
- Commonest cause: atherosclerosis
- Collateral circulation is good, so it may be silent

## Subclavian steal syndrome
- Cause: proximal subclavian stenosis
- Pathophysiology: exercising the arm draws blood backwards down the vertebral artery to supply the limb, taking it from the posterior cerebral circulation
- Symptoms
  - Neurological: vertigo, double vision, loss of consciousness
  - Come on after exercising that arm
- Management
  - Endovascular balloon angioplasty
  - Extrathoracic carotid-subclavian bypass
    - Needs a short period of carotid cross-clamping, generally well tolerated

## Acute limb ischaemia
- Usually secondary to emboli
  - AF or mural thrombus
  - Paradoxical venous thromboembolism
  - Iatrogenic
- Management: emergency brachial embolectomy to prevent compartment syndrome

## Anaesthetic technique
- Usually under local anaesthesia
  - Regional anaesthesia may be contraindicated if the surgeon needs to assess for compartment syndrome
- General anaesthesia rarely needed

## Flashcards
- Q: What causes subclavian steal syndrome?
  - A: Proximal subclavian stenosis, with retrograde vertebral flow on arm exercise.
- Q: What symptoms does subclavian steal cause?
  - A: Vertigo, double vision and loss of consciousness after exercising that arm.
- Q: What is the usual cause of acute upper limb ischaemia?
  - A: Emboli (AF or mural thrombus, paradoxical VTE, iatrogenic).
- Q: What is the usual anaesthetic for brachial embolectomy?
  - A: Local anaesthesia; regional may be contraindicated if compartments need assessing.
