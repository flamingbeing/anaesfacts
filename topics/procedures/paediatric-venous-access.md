# Paediatric venous access

Peripheral, external jugular, central (internal jugular, femoral) and intraosseous access in children.

## Peripheral cannulation: why it is hard
- Can defeat even experienced paediatric anaesthetists
    - Veins hard to find (chubby 1-year-old; neonates, where scalp veins may be the best option)
    - Uncooperative child
    - Few attempts possible before child, parents and staff become very distressed
    - Vasoconstriction, e.g. hypovolaemia
- Critically unwell child needing immediate access: maximum of two peripheral attempts, then go to intraosseous

## Peripheral cannulation: general points
- Every child having anaesthesia gets at least one peripheral cannula, as in adults
- After inhalational induction, site it as soon as practical
- Sites
    - Dorsum of hand, antecubital fossa, radial side of wrist
    - Dorsolateral foot, long saphenous vein
    - Cephalic vein in the forearm
    - External jugular: useful in chubby toddlers
    - Small palmar wrist veins may be the most prominent in young children
    - Scalp veins in neonates and young infants: less secure and reliable
    - Avoid flexor wrist veins if possible: extravasation there exposes nearby nerves and tendons
- Size: depends on child size, expected fluid needs and venous anatomy
    - Smallest plastic cannula available: 26 G
    - Neonates: 24 G
    - Infants and older children: 24 or 22 G (22G for children unless a wider bore is clinically needed)
- Technique as in adults
    - Flashback can be slow with small gauge needles
    - Fine cannulae take practice to handle
- Flow
    - Maximum flow is proportional to radius to the fourth power, so paediatric cannulae flow far less than adult ones
    - Standard flow test conditions: distilled water, 22° C, 10 kPa, 110 cm tubing of 4 mm internal diameter
    - Maximum flow rarely needed; ease of placement usually matters more
- Child-friendly language: some avoid "needle" and say "plastic tube" or "straw"

## Elective or emergency anaesthesia: cannulation steps
- Cannulation after inhalational induction may be appropriate
- At the preoperative visit: explain the need for a cannula to child and parents, and look for the best veins
- Topical anaesthesia over the best veins: EMLA 1-2 hours before, or Ametop 30-40 min before
    - Cover two or more sites in case the first attempt fails
    - With distraction, the child is often unaware of cannulation
- Keep the needle out of the child's sight; distract the child (parents, play specialists help)
- Remove the cream; assistant holds the limb still and congests the vein
- Cannulate as in adults; secure quickly before the child moves
- Flush with saline to confirm patency (avoids subcutaneous induction agent)
- Keep anaesthetic drugs at hand to give as soon as the cannula is in
- Keep access to the head and airway despite parents and play specialist: the child desaturates fast once anaesthetised

## External jugular vein
- Anatomy
    - Formed by the posterior auricular vein joining the posterior division of the posterior facial vein, within the parotid at the level of the mandibular angle
    - Runs down the neck from the angle of the mandible towards the mid-clavicle, crossing sternocleidomastoid obliquely
    - Pierces deep fascia in the subclavian triangle and drains into the subclavian vein, lateral to or in front of scalenus anterior
    - Lies under skin, superficial fascia and platysma; deep cervical fascia separates it from sternocleidomastoid
    - Crosses the cutaneous cervical nerve; upper half runs alongside the great auricular nerve
    - Size varies
- Constant, easily found landmark; very useful after failed attempts elsewhere
- Counts as a central vein: aseptic technique
- Technique
    - Slight head down, head turned away, arms by sides, roll under shoulders to extend neck
    - Assistant's finger across the vein just above the clavicle makes it stand out
    - Cannula over needle along the line of the vein, aiming caudal and posterior
    - Flashback: advance cannula and secure
    - More often the vein is transfixed: withdraw slowly until blood appears, then advance
- Can be used to pass a guidewire and central catheter into the SVC, but passage often sticks where the vein pierces deep fascia

## Internal jugular vein
- Used less in children than in adults; hard under 1 year of age (short neck, difficult vein)
- Needs general anaesthesia; muscle relaxation helps by preventing movement and injury to nearby structures
- Same steps as in adults, with shorter needles and catheters
- Anatomy: deep to sternocleidomastoid, from just anterior to the mastoid process to behind the sternoclavicular joint
- Catheters: 4 FG up to adult size; single, double or triple lumen; 6 cm to 30 cm long
- Technique
    - Right side preferred: IJV, SVC and right atrium are in line, so tip position is nearly always right
    - Left side: tip often malpositioned; thoracic duct at risk
    - Low approach near the clavicle: high success, but risk of pneumothorax and haemothorax
    - High approach at the apex of the sternocleidomastoid triangle (cricoid level): less pneumothorax, may increase carotid puncture risk
    - Head down, head turned away, roll under shoulders
    - Feel the carotid; insert just lateral at 30° to the skin, aiming at the ipsilateral nipple, aspirating gently
    - End point: venous blood in the syringe; do not advance beyond 2.5 cm
    - The vein is often kinked and transfixed, with blood only on withdrawal
    - If blood flows freely after removing the syringe: cannulate or Seldinger guidewire for the final catheter
    - Avoid air entry: tiny volumes can cause dysrhythmias, cardiac arrest or cerebral embolism
    - Flush lumens with saline or heparinised saline before use
- Ultrasound
    - Now very common: scan before puncture or guide the needle in real time
    - NICE: preferred method for elective IJV cannulation; consider in emergencies too
    - Keep landmark skills up
    - Benefits: shows anatomy, confirms a patent vein, shows the artery and its relation to the vein, guides the needle and shows it entering the vein
    - In adults: higher success and fewer complications than landmark; likely to hold for children too
- Confirming placement
    - Venous not arterial: venous trace and pressure from the distal lumen, free venous aspiration from every lumen
    - Tip at or near the SVC-right atrial junction on CXR, which also excludes pneumothorax
- Complications
    - Any central line: air embolism, haematoma, thrombosis, sepsis, long-term vein damage
    - IJV-specific: carotid puncture, pneumothorax, haemothorax, nerve injury (brachial plexus, phrenic nerve, Horner's syndrome, usually transient), thoracic duct injury on the left

## Femoral vein
- Popular in children, especially neonates and infants
    - Often easier than internal jugular, subclavian or external jugular at these ages
    - Fewer serious complications and less risk to vital structures
- Does not give accurate cardiac filling pressures; limited use in cardiac surgery
- Less favoured long term than neck lines: infection, kinking with leg movement, displacement during nappy changes and perineal care
- Anatomy: with the femoral artery in the femoral triangle, medial to it at the inguinal ligament, where it becomes the external iliac vein
- Technique
    - General anaesthesia to stop movement
    - Supine, small roll under hips, slight hip abduction and external rotation
    - Feel the femoral artery 1-2 cm below the midpoint of the inguinal ligament (halfway between ASIS and pubic symphysis)
    - Insert just medial to the artery at 45° to the coronal plane, aspirating gently
    - End point: venous blood; do not advance beyond 2.5 cm (risk of perforating a viscus or entering the hip joint)
    - Vein often transfixed, blood only on withdrawal
    - Free flow after removing syringe: cannulate or Seldinger
    - Avoid air entry (dysrhythmias, cardiac arrest, cerebral embolism); flush lumens with saline or heparinised saline
- Confirming placement
    - Tip should lie in the IVC
    - Tip can stray into a small tributary such as an epidural vein, with erosion risk: check venous trace and pressure from the distal lumen and free aspiration from all lumens (also excludes arterial placement)
    - Ultrasound confirms anatomy and spots variants, e.g. vein lying almost behind rather than medial to the artery
- Complications: general central line risks, plus femoral artery puncture (common), hip joint penetration (rare), bowel perforation (rare)

## Intraosseous access
- For rapid access when peripheral or central attempts fail
- Uses: drugs, fluid resuscitation, blood samples (label as bone marrow)
- Physiology
    - Long bone marrow holds non-collapsible venous sinusoids draining to a central venous canal, then out via nutrient or emissary veins: effectively a route into the venous system
    - Infant marrow is highly vascular red marrow; replaced by less vascular yellow marrow after 5 years of age
    - Recommended under 6 years of age, but usable throughout childhood and in adults
- Purpose-made IO needles (18 G and 20 G) should be in every area where children are anaesthetised and on resuscitation trolleys
- Indications
    - Resuscitation of babies or children under 6 years
    - Any emergency where venous access fails after two attempts
    - Bridge during induction in a child with difficult access, until IV or central access is obtained
    - Temporary: replace with IV access within 24 hours if possible
- Advantages: clear landmarks, fast, no special skill needed, high success, few complications, takes many drugs and fluids
- Sites
    - Most common: anterior proximal tibia, 2 cm below the tibial tuberosity
    - Iliac crest in older children
    - Sternum no longer recommended (mediastinal penetration)
- Technique
    - Aseptic; landmarks as above
    - 90° to the bone surface, steady axial pressure, rotating screwing motion
    - Stop at loss of resistance; unscrew the cap
    - Aspirate to confirm and take marrow samples
    - Inject 10 mL 0.9% saline to confirm position
- Signs of correct placement
    - Sudden loss of resistance (not with screw-tipped needles)
    - Marrow aspirated (not always, even when correct)
    - Needle stands upright unsupported
    - Fluid injects easily with no extravasation (no increase in limb circumference, local swelling or firmness)
- Complications: tibia transfixed, calf compartment syndrome, osteomyelitis, haemorrhage, growth plate injury

## Flashcards
- Q: Critically ill child needing immediate access: how many peripheral attempts before intraosseous?
  - A: Two at most
- Q: How long before cannulation should EMLA and Ametop be applied?
  - A: EMLA 1-2 hours; Ametop 30-40 min
- Q: Maximum needle advance for paediatric IJV or femoral cannulation?
  - A: 2.5 cm
- Q: Most common IO site and its landmark?
  - A: Anterior proximal tibia, 2 cm below the tibial tuberosity
- Q: How soon should an IO line be replaced?
  - A: Within 24 hours if possible
