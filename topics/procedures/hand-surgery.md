# Hand and Forearm Surgery

Mostly day cases suited to local or regional anaesthesia; the tourniquet often decides what the patient tolerates.

## Preoperative assessment
- Full assessment as for GA: the patient may ask for GA and blocks can fail
- If awake: can they lie flat for the length of the operation?
- Check movement of the operative arm: can they reach the position needed for the block and surgery?

## Intraoperative management
- Duration
  - Repair of cut structures can be long, depending on what is repaired
  - Long cases: intubate, temperature probe and forced-air warmer, urinary catheter
- Pain: usually well controlled with a wrist or digital block
- Blood loss: minimal
- Position
  - Supine, arm out on a hand table
  - Table is turned with the arm out, so long extensions may not be needed
- Drip and plug on the non-operative side with an anti-reflux valve; BP cuff on the other arm too

## Anaesthetic technique
- Most hand procedures can be done under local or regional anaesthesia as a day case, with sedation or GA added if needed
- Local infiltration
  - Some cases (carpal tunnel release, trigger finger release) need infiltration alone
  - Mainly for short cases because of tourniquet pain; longer cases possible as WALANT (wide-awake local anaesthesia, no tourniquet)
- Nerve block
  - Use a suitable block you know well for the site, with or without tourniquet
  - Top up a plexus block with elbow or wrist blocks to improve success
- Sedation or GA
  - Choose by safety and patient preference; have drugs and kit ready to convert during surgery
  - GA may be needed for bilateral surgery or harvesting from a distant site, where several blocks could exceed the maximum local anaesthetic dose
- IV regional anaesthesia (IVRA): suits below-elbow surgery of 30 min or less; now rarely used

## Tourniquet and surgical needs
- An upper-arm tourniquet is used for almost all hand surgery; its site and duration largely decide whether regional or local anaesthesia alone is tolerated
- A good brachial plexus block usually allows 60–90 min of arm ischaemia
  - Axillary approach gives good hand, forearm and arm anaesthesia, but tourniquet pain can be a problem
  - Other approaches: infraclavicular, supraclavicular
- Reduce tourniquet pain by blocking the intercostobrachial nerve subcutaneously on the medial upper arm above the tourniquet
- Avoid adrenaline-containing solutions near digits

## Emergence
- A splint may go on after skin closure: keep the patient anaesthetised during splinting

## Postoperative care
- Skin and soft tissue surgery hurts less than bone and joint surgery; simple analgesic combinations usually suffice
- More painful cases may need opioids or a regional catheter
- Surgeons can give a digital block at the end

## Specific procedures
- Carpal tunnel release, de Quervain release, excision biopsy of a lump: usually local or wrist block by surgeons
- Digit replantation: usually GA because it is long; a regional block for sympathectomy helps
- MCP joint replacement: often frailer patients with systemic disease, including rheumatoid arthritis
- Dupuytren's contracture
  - GA with wrist block or infiltration
  - Brachial plexus block with upper-arm tourniquet, with or without GA
  - Quick cases: wrist block with a wrist tourniquet
  - Severe or redo disease may need skin grafting; longer tourniquet time usually needs a brachial plexus block or GA with a local block
- Tenolysis: the patient may need to move the hand to test the result, so any block must be distal

## Flashcards
- Q: How long does a good brachial plexus block usually allow an arm tourniquet?
  - A: 60–90 min of ischaemia.
- Q: Which nerve is blocked to reduce upper-arm tourniquet pain?
  - A: Intercostobrachial nerve, subcutaneously on the medial upper arm above the tourniquet.
- Q: What procedures suit IVRA?
  - A: Below-elbow surgery of 30 min or less.
- Q: Why must blocks be distal for tenolysis?
  - A: The patient may need to move the hand to test the repair.
