# Neonatal surgery

Common neonatal operations: pyloric stenosis is a medical emergency first; NEC and CDH babies arrive critically ill and ventilated; abdominal wall defects raise intra-abdominal pressure on closure. Apnoea risk persists to postconceptual age 60 weeks.

## General
- Postconceptual age under 60 weeks: risk of apnoea

## Pyloric stenosis (pyloromyotomy)
- Congenital hypertrophic pyloric stenosis
  - 1 in 300–400; 80% male; usually 2–8 weeks old (rare after 12 weeks)
- Hypertrophied pyloric circular muscle causes gastric outlet obstruction: non-bilious projectile vomiting
- Diagnosis: history and a palpable pyloric "olive"; ultrasound shows muscle thickness >4 mm or pyloric length >16 mm
- Complications of vomiting:
  - Aspiration
  - Severe dehydration
  - Hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis with paradoxical aciduria
    - Loss of H+, Cl- and K+
    - Hypovolaemia triggers secondary hyperaldosteronism; kidneys excrete H+, so urine is acidic despite systemic alkalosis
  - Low or high sodium

### Preoperative assessment
- Medical, not surgical, emergency: correct fluid and electrolytes first (especially chloride)
- Ready for theatre once rehydrated with chloride >100 mmol/L, pH <7.5, base excess <6 mmol/L, bicarbonate <30 mmol/L
- NG tube with regular gastric lavage to empty the stomach

### Anaesthetic technique
- Short operation; no blood loss or fluid shifts
  - Open (left upper quadrant or periumbilical incision) or laparoscopic
- Already has IV access and fluids
- Very high aspiration risk:
  - Aspirate the NG tube before induction in 5 positions: left lateral, right lateral, head up, head down, prone
  - RSI
- Surgeon may ask for 40-60 mL of air down the NG tube to check for mucosal perforation; aspirate it before closure
- Extubate awake

### Postoperative care
- Infant likely postconceptual age under 6 weeks: continuous SpO2 and apnoea monitoring in a special care area
- Prone to hypoglycaemia
- Clear fluids first, then milk

## Necrotising enterocolitis (NEC)
- Almost only in premature babies (5-15% of those under 1500 g birth weight)
- Ischaemic injury to bowel mucosa; may progress to perforation and sepsis
- Presentation: rising gastric aspirates, abdominal distension, bloody diarrhoea, temperature instability, apnoeas
- Later sepsis: thrombocytopenia, anaemia, coagulopathy, metabolic acidosis
- Medical treatment: fluid resuscitation, stop enteral feeds, NG decompression, antibiotics
- About 50% need surgery
  - Peritoneal drain(s), or laparotomy with resection of necrotic bowel and stoma
  - Tunnelled central line often placed for sampling and parenteral nutrition

### Preoperative assessment
- Often very sick; usually intubated and ventilated on the neonatal unit before transfer
- Discuss with the neonatologist: ETT details, ventilator settings, rising ventilatory needs
- Note fluid type and volume given
- Assess circulating volume: often hypovolaemic (large third-space losses); may be on inotropes
- Review recent FBC, clotting, electrolytes, blood gas and their trends
- Crossmatch blood; platelets and red cells may be needed before surgery
- Note vascular access; arterial line very useful if it does not delay surgery (acid-base, clotting, electrolytes, Hb, platelets)

### Anaesthetic technique
- Often brought by neonatal staff: full handover
- Unstable: handle carefully, monitor continuously; often deteriorate on transfer from incubator to table
- Continue neonatal unit fluids, analgesia and sedation; extra analgesic bolus and relaxant if needed
- Volatile in oxygen and air; nitrous oxide contraindicated
- IPPV, often better by hand with a T-piece than by ventilator
- Titrate FiO2 to SpO2 92-95%; avoid hyperoxia (retinopathy of prematurity)
- Bowel handling lowers lung compliance; ventilatory needs rise
- Instability increases with ischaemic bowel handling and bleeding
  - Give volume in 10 mL/kg aliquots titrated to response
  - Blood sequestered in ischaemic bowel; transfusion often needed
  - Often a circulating volume or more replaced with colloid, red cells, platelets and FFP during extensive resection
  - Volume versus inotrope balance is hard; CVP rarely available; blood loss hard to measure
- Hypothermia common despite warm theatre, warmed fluids, warming mattress and forced-air blanket
- Monitor urine output if possible
- Serial blood gases, FBC and electrolytes when sampling is possible

### Postoperative care
- Back to NICU, usually for prolonged IPPV and parenteral nutrition

## Abdominal wall defects: gastroschisis and exomphalos
- Exomphalos: herniation into the umbilical cord; gastroschisis: defect lateral to the umbilicus
- Combined incidence 1:3000 live births; usually detected antenatally, obvious at birth

### Gastroschisis
- Defect beside the cord (right side more common); abdominal contents herniate through the wall
- Bowel exposed to amniotic fluid then air: inflamed, oedematous, shortened, dysfunctional
- Fewer associated anomalies than exomphalos
- At delivery: cover with a sterile dressing or plastic bag (heat loss, fluid loss, infection); pass an NG tube; transfer to a neonatal surgical unit
- Reduced urgently: risk of intra-abdominal sepsis and bowel ischaemia if the defect is tight
- Repair: excise any sac, stretch the wall, return bowel, primary closure
- If primary closure impossible: silastic silo over the bowel, gradual reduction over several days, then closure under GA
- Many centres now apply a silo without GA as first treatment
- Feeding line for TPN may be placed

### Exomphalos (omphalocele)
- Failure of gut return from the yolk sac with incomplete rectus closure; midgut herniates into the umbilical cord
- About 50% contain liver, spleen or gonads
- Translucent sac (unless ruptured at birth) with umbilical vessels on its wall; bowel usually normal
- Often low birth weight; about 40% have other malformations: imperforate anus, bowel atresia, Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, congenital heart disease, bladder exstrophy
- Mortality mainly from associated anomalies
- Initial care as for gastroschisis; surgery may wait for investigation of other anomalies; same repair principles

### Preoperative assessment
- Look for associated anomalies; echo if possible (may not be in gastroschisis)
- Assess volume status; resuscitation often needed
- FBC, electrolytes, acid-base
- Regular glucose checks
- Crossmatch
- CXR (lungs may be compromised after reduction)

### Anaesthetic technique
- May arrive intubated from ICU; if not:
  - Aspirate the NG tube
  - IV or inhalational induction; many prefer RSI
  - Intubate (nasal tube, as postoperative IPPV is likely) and ventilate
- Arterial line useful (IVC compression may reduce cardiac output)
- All measures against hypothermia: large area of bowel exposed
- Reduction raises intra-abdominal pressure and lowers compliance: increase rate, pressure and FiO2; slight head-up may help
- IVC compression may reduce cardiac output and organ perfusion
- If staying ventilated: opioid bolus, morphine 50-100 mcg/kg IV or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg
- Small exomphalos: may be extubated; give less opioid or a caudal

### Postoperative care
- Usually ventilated afterwards, except very small exomphalos
- Ventilation may be hard due to high intra-abdominal pressure
- IVC compression lowers cardiac output, shown mainly as oliguria and metabolic acidosis
  - Inotrope (e.g. dopamine 3-5 mcg/kg/min) may help renal and mesenteric perfusion
  - Lower body congestion and cyanosis distort leg NIBP and oximetry
- Gastroschisis usually needs prolonged parenteral nutrition

## Anorectal malformations (imperforate anus)
- Incidence 1:5000 births; more common in males
- Spectrum from a simple distal membrane to complex genitourinary anomalies
- High lesions: rectum ends above levator ani; low lesions: below it
  - Higher lesions: more associated anomalies, more complex, poorer continence
  - High: fistula to urethra (males) or vagina (females)
  - Low: open onto perineum, scrotal median raphe or vaginal vestibule
- Associated anomalies common:
  - VACTERL association; Down syndrome; REAR (renal, ear, anal, radial) syndromes
  - Spinal anomalies frequent; tethered cord in about 25%: beware if a caudal is planned
- Types:
  - Type 1: anal stenosis at or 1-4 cm above the anus (incomplete rupture of the anal membrane); may have a fistula to genitourinary tract or perineum
  - Type 2: persistent membrane
  - Type 3 (commonest): rectum ends blindly well above the imperforate anus; may fistulate to urethra, bladder, penile base, scrotum or vagina
  - Type 4: normal anus and anal pouch, rectum ends blindly
- Presentation: no stool in the first 24 hours, distended abdomen, stool from an abnormal site
- Surgery:
  - High: colostomy first, later pull-through (e.g. posterior sagittal anorectoplasty, PSARP)
  - Low: perineal anoplasty (close fistula, create opening, reposition rectal pouch)

### Preoperative assessment
- Screen for associated anomalies, especially cardiac and tracheo-oesophageal
  - Renal ultrasound, CXR, ECG, echo, lumbar and sacral spine X-rays
- Abdominal distension may impair breathing, cause apnoeas or risk bowel ischaemia
  - Note SpO2 in air, oxygen need, apnoeas
- IV access and maintenance fluids in place
- Crossmatch for high lesions needing colostomy

### Anaesthetic technique
- Intubation and IPPV
  - Distended abdomen: consider RSI or awake intubation
- Muscle relaxant for better conditions unless the anal sphincter must be identified
- Avoid nitrous oxide (bowel distension)
- Adequate fluids: large insensible and third-space losses, more if perforated
  - Balanced salt solution or colloid at 10 mL/kg/h or more
  - Consider sepsis if bowel perforated
- Invasive monitoring not needed in a healthy baby

### Postoperative care
- Usually extubated
- Maintenance fluids until feeding

### Examination under anaesthesia and anoplasty
- Lithotomy (as near as possible in a neonate), hips and knees flexed and taped; baby at the far end of the table
- Caudal single shot for sacral dermatomes: 0.3-0.5 mL/kg 0.25% levobupivacaine or similar
  - Sacrum sometimes abnormal; caudal may not be possible
- Afterwards oral analgesia: paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 6-hourly (max 60 mg/kg/day) and codeine phosphate 0.5 mg/kg oral 4-hourly

### Colostomy
- Supine
- Needs thoracic dermatome cover: single caudal 1.25 mL/kg 0.125% levobupivacaine or similar, or caudal catheter (usable for postoperative infusion)
- Occasionally needs volume replacement
- Afterwards: IV morphine infusion or epidural LA infusion, usually for 24 hours

### Posterior sagittal anorectoplasty (PSARP)
- Standard operation for high and intermediate malformations since introduced by deVries and Peña in 1982
- GA with ETT
- Caudal helps postoperative analgesia and may relax anal muscles
  - Some anaesthetists avoid a caudal so close to the surgical site
- Prone position
- Valid group and crossmatch
- Antibiotics: Rocephin (ceftriaxone) and Flagyl (metronidazole)
- Consider postoperative high dependency care

### Endorectal pull-through
- Laparoscopically assisted (LERPT) or total transanal (TERPT)
- Avoids open surgery risks
- GA with ETT
- Laparoscopic assisted: supine; transanal: supine or prone

## Hirschsprung's disease
- Absent ganglion cells in the distal colon cause functional obstruction
- Diagnosis: contrast enema helps; full-thickness rectal biopsy is the gold standard
- Medical: rectal irrigation, prophylactic antibiotics
- Surgical:
  - Traditionally colostomy at diagnosis, definitive repair delayed until 10 kg
  - Repair usually by pull-through

## Congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH)
- Presents early (if not found antenatally) with cyanosis, respiratory distress and a scaphoid abdomen
- Pleuroperitoneal membrane fails to close before bowel returns to the abdomen in the 10th week of gestation; bowel enters the chest
- Incidence 1:4000 live births
- Left posterolateral (Bochdalek) hernia most common (85%): small and large bowel plus solid organs in the chest
- Right-sided: usually liver and large bowel only; bilateral rare and usually fatal
- Pulmonary hypoplasia (often bilateral), surfactant dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, right-to-left shunt
  - These, not the hernia itself, are the core problem; optimise medically in a specialist unit before surgery
- Repair via abdominal, thoraco-abdominal or transthoracic approach; sometimes a synthetic patch
- Prognosis depends on pulmonary hypoplasia, pulmonary hypertension and other anomalies; mortality 30-50%
- Late presentation (infancy or adulthood) with respiratory distress or obstruction usually does well

### Preoperative management
- Usually needs intubation and IPPV
  - Bag-mask with low pressures and volumes to avoid distending intrathoracic bowel
  - Limited inflation pressures with permissive hypercapnia to reduce barotrauma
- NG tube to decompress intrathoracic bowel
- Pulmonary hypertension treatment often needed: nitric oxide, high-frequency or oscillatory ventilation, ECMO
  - Vasodilators (prostaglandin E1, glyceryl trinitrate, nitroprusside) cause systemic hypotension; may need an inotrope
- Preductal arterial line (right radial)
- Central line: femoral, or neck on the hernia side (pneumothorax on the good-lung side may be fatal)
- Look for associated anomalies, especially congenital heart disease
- FBC, U&E, crossmatch, CXR if not done

### Anaesthetic technique
- Usually arrives intubated from ICU
- Continuation of intensive care plus volatile and opioid: morphine 100-200 mcg/kg, fentanyl 5-10 mcg/kg, fentanyl infusion 2-4 mcg/kg/hour, or remifentanil 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min
- Supine; usually subcostal incision
- Pressure-limited ventilation preferred (avoid high pressures and pneumothorax)
- Frequent arterial sampling

### Postoperative care
- Back to ICU; respiratory function usually worse after surgery
- Continue treatment of pulmonary hypertension and ductal right-to-left shunt

## Myelomeningocele
- Neural tube fails to fuse in the 4th week of gestation; neurological deficit below the lesion; may or may not be skin-covered
- 75% lumbosacral
- Myelomeningocele contains neural tissue; meningocele contains only CSF
- Associated with Arnold-Chiari type II malformation; hydrocephalus in 70-85%
  - Chiari II: posterior fossa and upper cervical bony abnormality with cerebellar vermis, fourth ventricle and lower brainstem below the foramen magnum
  - Cord compression may blunt ventilatory responses to hypoxia and hypercapnia; occasional lower cranial nerve palsies affect swallowing and airway reflexes
- Usually diagnosed by antenatal ultrasound
- High infection risk; ventriculitis rate depends on speed of repair; ideally closed within 24 hours
- VP shunt usually at primary repair or soon after

### Preoperative assessment
- Level and size of lesion affect positioning at induction, duration and blood loss
- Record neurological examination for postoperative comparison
- Cover the lesion with a damp dressing to stop drying
- Other anomalies, especially congenital heart disease, may alter management
- Crossmatch blood

### Anaesthetic technique
- IV cannula and maintenance fluids usually in place
- Optional atropine 20 mcg/kg IV before induction
- IV or inhalational induction; support on a cushion or jelly ring to protect the lesion, or lateral/semi-lateral depending on anatomy
- Armoured ETT and IPPV
- Volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
- Consider arterial and central lines for large lesions
- Prone, with bolsters under shoulders and pelvis so the abdomen moves freely; pad the limbs
- Surgeon may stimulate nerves: discuss before giving a long-acting relaxant
- Blood loss usually small; large lesions needing skin flaps can bleed
- Surgeon infiltrates LA with adrenaline; extra opioid if needed (morphine 25-50 mcg/kg or fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg); sensory level unclear so needs vary
- IV antibiotics as requested by the surgeon or per protocol
- Extubate at the end if stable

### Postoperative care
- Tight closure of a large lesion may impair ventilation; Chiari malformation may also affect breathing
- Neonatal surgical unit with ECG and pulse oximetry
- Maintenance fluids until feeding
- Paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 6-hourly (max 60 mg/kg/day) and codeine phosphate 0.5 mg/kg oral 4-hourly; IV morphine infusion for extensive lesions

## Flashcards
- Q: When is a baby with pyloric stenosis ready for theatre?
  - A: Rehydrated with chloride >100 mmol/L, pH <7.5, base excess <6 mmol/L and bicarbonate <30 mmol/L
- Q: Target SpO2 during NEC laparotomy in a premature baby?
  - A: 92-95%, avoiding hyperoxia (retinopathy of prematurity)
- Q: Where should the arterial line go in CDH and why?
  - A: Preductal, right radial
- Q: Caudal dose for colostomy (thoracic dermatomes) in a neonate?
  - A: 1.25 mL/kg 0.125% levobupivacaine
- Q: Why beware a caudal in anorectal malformation?
  - A: Spinal anomalies; tethered cord in about 25%; sacrum may be abnormal
