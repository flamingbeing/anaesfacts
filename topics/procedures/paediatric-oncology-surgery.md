# Paediatric Oncology Anaesthesia

Children with cancer need repeated anaesthetics for diagnosis, access and treatment; assess tumour effects, treatment toxicity and immunosuppression every time.

## Epidemiology
- Risk of cancer before age 15: about 1:500 (1:1600 leukaemia, 1:2200 CNS tumours, 1:1100 all others combined)
- 30% leukaemias (ALL and AML), 20% brain and CNS tumours, 10% lymphomas
- Embryonal tumours about 15%: arise from tissue normally only present in the embryo
  - Medulloblastoma, neuroblastoma, retinoblastoma, nephroblastoma (Wilms), hepatoblastoma
  - Neuroblastoma 7%, Wilms tumour 6%
- Less common: bone cancers, soft tissue sarcomas, germ cell and gonadal tumours

## General anaesthetic considerations
- Common procedures: tumour biopsy or resection, vascular access devices, lumbar puncture, intrathecal chemotherapy, bone marrow aspiration, imaging
- Preoperative assessment
  - Local and systemic tumour effects, e.g. anterior mediastinal mass
  - Treatment toxicity: combination chemotherapy can affect almost every organ; radiotherapy complications
  - Immunosuppression: infections may present subtly or atypically
  - Many previous procedures: find old anaesthetic charts, but a previous uneventful anaesthetic does not guarantee safety, as organ dysfunction can develop at any time
- Perioperative
  - Sedative premedication may help; very stressful for child and parent
  - Immunosuppressed: avoid rectal drugs (bacteraemia risk)
  - Thrombocytopenia common: avoid IM injections; think carefully about epidural and caudal blocks
  - Hickman line or portacath: strict asepsis when accessed
  - Long-term opioids may raise analgesic needs
  - Dexamethasone may need to be avoided as an antiemetic
- Postoperative: anaesthetists contribute a lot to acute, chronic and palliative pain management

## Chemotherapy toxicity
- All chemotherapy agents have adverse effects

### Haematological
- Myelosuppression is the commonest dose-limiting effect: anaemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia
- Cancer itself often causes myelosuppression, even before treatment; causes are often mixed
- Anaemia: may need packed cell transfusion
- Thrombocytopenia: may need platelet transfusion
- Neutropenia: strict asepsis for procedures, no rectal drugs, perioperative isolation

### Gastrointestinal
- Common and often treatment limiting: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, mucositis, enterocolitis, stomatitis
- Lead to poor intake and malnutrition, dehydration, and disturbed fluids, electrolytes and serum proteins
- Risk of delayed gastric emptying and aspiration
- Oral mucositis (redness, oedema, atrophy, ulceration)
  - Affects 40% of children on treatment; up to 75% of those being prepared for stem cell transplant
  - Often the most distressing part of treatment; can involve the whole GI tract
  - Treatment: gentle oral debridement; decontamination (nystatin, clotrimazole, chlorhexidine); prevent trauma and bleeding (keep platelets > 20000/mm3)
  - Pain: oral care and rinses; many need parenteral opioid infusion, NCA or PCA; cautious adjuncts (ketamine, gabapentin, clonidine)
  - NSAIDs relatively contraindicated (GI erosion, platelet effects)

### Cardiac
- Cardiotoxic agents: anthracyclines (doxorubicin), fluorouracil and cyclophosphamide
  - Common in leukaemia and lymphoma
  - Effects: myocardial depression, ischaemia, hypotension, hypertension, myocarditis, endomyocardial fibrosis, conduction defects with dysrhythmias and heart block
  - Early or late onset: surveillance echocardiography during and after treatment
  - Risk factors: cumulative dose, daily dose, rate and route, schedule, drug combinations

### Pulmonary
- Early
  - Interstitial pneumonitis: methotrexate, bleomycin, paclitaxel
  - Non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema: bleomycin, interleukin-2
  - Bronchospasm: vinblastine, methotrexate
  - Pleural effusion: methotrexate
- Late effects are commoner, mainly pulmonary fibrosis
- Bleomycin: toxic in up to 10% of recipients
  - Bleomycin-induced pneumonitis can progress to fibrosis; treat by stopping the drug and giving steroids
  - Lung injury is oxidant mediated, so high FiO2 during or after therapy may worsen it: keep intraoperative FiO2 as low as safely possible

### Renal
- Cisplatin, carboplatin and ifosfamide most often cause acute renal failure in children
- Cisplatin nephrotoxicity is dose dependent and cumulative
- High-dose methotrexate causes severe renal impairment in 2% of children
- Amphotericin B, ciclosporin, aciclovir, immunoglobulin and some antibiotics can contribute

### Neurological
- Platinum agents (cisplatin, carboplatin): more neurotoxicity
- Also methotrexate, vincristine, ciclosporin
- Serious neurotoxicity especially with ifosfamide and systemic or intrathecal methotrexate

### Tumour lysis syndrome
- During early induction chemotherapy (especially the first 5 days)
- Cell lysis releases potassium, phosphate and uric acid, which all rise
- Uric acid and calcium phosphate precipitate in renal tubules: renal impairment
- Calcium falls (binds phosphate): tetany
- Hyperkalaemia: dysrhythmias
- Treatment: hydration, allopurinol, diuretics

## Radiotherapy
- Indications: ALL and AML; Wilms, neuroblastoma, retinoblastoma; CNS tumours; others
- Usually daily for several weeks (6 weeks is not unusual)
  - Condition can change during the course: hair loss, skin damage, nausea, fatigue, intercurrent chest infections, distress from daily fasting and anaesthesia
- Anaesthesia needed for planning, simulation and treatment
  - Each field exposure is short: 30-90 sec
  - Planning: a custom plastic shell is made from a plaster cast and marked to guide the beam; reused for each session and for simulation
  - Simulation: imaging builds a 3D model to find the best approach with least harm to nearby tissue
- Complications
  - Airway, after high-dose neck or mouth radiotherapy: soft tissue fibrosis limiting mouth opening and neck extension; mucosal fibrosis; supraglottic and subglottic oedema, narrowing or stenosis; mucositis
  - Radiation heart disease: pericarditis, pericardial effusion, endocardial and valve fibrosis, conduction defects, coronary disease
  - Radiation pneumonitis: common; chemotherapy plus chest radiation usually gives a restrictive pattern on PFTs
    - Severity relates to lung volume irradiated, total dose and fraction size
    - Risk factors: previous chemotherapy or radiotherapy, steroid withdrawal

## Radiotherapy: anaesthetic technique
- Children over five may manage without GA with good preparation; younger children (<5-6 years) usually need anaesthesia
- First case of the morning if possible, to limit fasting and disruption
- Once a routine is set, may come from home; otherwise from the oncology ward
- Remote site, often another hospital, with limited facilities
  - Machine often runs on cylinders; limited drugs and equipment; check everything
- Child is alone in the room during treatment: watch the patient and monitor by camera
- Face mould for head or cervical spine fields
- Usually has a Hickman line or portacath with a Gripper needle
- IV induction usually; inhalational if preferred
- Almost always LMA with spontaneous breathing and volatile in oxygen; 100% oxygen preferred because the anaesthetist is remote if a problem arises; TIVA is an alternative
- Analgesia usually not needed: radiotherapy is painless
- Child eats once recovered and often goes straight home

## Short procedures (lumbar puncture, intrathecal drugs, bone marrow aspirate and trephine)
- Many procedures over years
- Lumbar puncture: diagnostic or therapeutic, during induction and consolidation chemotherapy
- Many children manage, and some prefer, conscious sedation (midazolam, ketamine or propofol; given by non-anaesthetists in some centres)
- Preoperative assessment
  - Look for toxicity
    - Cardiac: cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin (Adriamycin) (dysrhythmias, heart failure); radiation (cardiomyopathy)
    - Respiratory: common chest infections (fever may be absent); bleomycin, cyclophosphamide, methotrexate (pulmonary toxicity)
  - Use the child's and parents' preferred induction unless contraindicated
  - FBC with or without U&E; in leukaemia a normal platelet count may not mean normal function
  - No agreed safe platelet level for lumbar puncture; if <50000/mm3 a platelet transfusion may be needed first
- Anaesthetic technique
  - Inhalational or IV induction as the child prefers
  - Indwelling lines (Hickman, portacath, PICC): aseptic non-touch technique; for longer procedures insert a peripheral cannula to limit line handling
  - Flush accessed central lines with the agreed heparin solution
  - Face mask or LMA; volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
  - Oncology staff do the lumbar puncture; the anaesthetist should not be operator and anaesthetist at once
  - Anaesthetists do not give intrathecal chemotherapy; only registered staff prescribe, check and give it, because errors (e.g. intrathecal vincristine) have been fatal
  - Intrathecal drugs: cytarabine, methotrexate, thiotepa, gentamicin, vancomycin, hydrocortisone
  - Ondansetron 100 mcg/kg (max 4 mg) IV if intrathecal chemotherapy given
  - Bone marrow aspirate and trephine: usually posterior superior iliac region, lateral or semi-prone; moderate pain; infiltrate local anaesthetic
- Postoperative
  - Little effect usually; new, unwell patients managed by clinical state; maintenance-phase patients are day cases
  - Post-dural puncture headache occurs, especially in older children: rest and regular oral analgesics

## Portacath or Hickman line insertion
- For severe chronic illness needing repeated IV drugs, fluids or support
- Largest groups: cancer chemotherapy and intestinal failure needing parenteral nutrition; haemophilia patients often get a portacath for daily home factor
- Hickman line: cuff at the exit site secures the line and resists infection
- Portacath: subcutaneous port (chamber with metal base and silicone dome) accessed with a non-coring Gripper or Huber needle; lower infection rate
- Usual site: internal jugular, directly or via the external jugular; subclavian occasionally; others rare
- Done in theatre with fluoroscopic guidance
- Usually open in children with two incisions (over the vein, and in the axilla or on the chest wall); catheter tunnelled between them and either buried (portacath) or brought out (Hickman)
- Percutaneous ultrasound-guided approach: risks pneumothorax (1-2%) and arterial puncture (6%)
- Repeat procedures are common: clinical change, catheter infection (25%), thrombosis, wound infection (15%), systemic bacterial or yeast colonisation
- During percutaneous insertion
  - Ventricular arrhythmias can occur once the guidewire is threaded: ask the operator to withdraw it slightly
  - Fluoroscopy confirms the tip at the right atrium and SVC junction
  - Tip position landmarks: 2 vertebral bodies below the carina; 6th thoracic vertebra posteriorly; between the 2nd and 3rd ribs anteriorly
  - Tunnelling and pocket dissection are more stimulating than the initial puncture
  - Arterial puncture can cause major unrecognised bleeding: have blood available
  - Chemotherapy patients often have low platelets and may need platelet cover

## Portacath or Hickman line: anaesthetic management
- Preoperative
  - From very fit (e.g. haemophilia boy) to near moribund with multiorgan disease
  - Check clotting if coagulopathy risk; adequate platelets on chemotherapy
  - Haemophilia: recombinant factor VIII cover as advised by haematology
- Anaesthetic technique
  - IV or inhalational induction by clinical state and access
  - Intubation with IPPV commonest; LMA with spontaneous breathing for well older children
  - Volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide; avoid nitrous oxide for percutaneous insertion (pneumothorax risk)
  - Trendelenburg (lower FRC and compliance), head turned away from the access side; infants may need IPPV
  - Main complication: bleeding from the venotomy, which can be hard to control
- Analgesia
  - Wound infiltration with 0.25% or 0.5% bupivacaine by weight and maximum dose
  - Opioid often needed (morphine 100-150 mcg/kg or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg), especially for a portacath where dissection reaches the rib cage
  - NSAIDs usually relatively contraindicated by the underlying condition
- Postoperative: care set by the underlying condition; paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly and oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly

## Bone marrow harvest
- Stem cells for transplant to treat haematological cancers (leukaemia, Hodgkin lymphoma), solid tumours (Wilms, retinoblastoma, neuroblastoma) and Fanconi syndrome (marrow failure)
- Marrow (autologous or allogeneic) is harvested, frozen, stored and reinfused after high-dose chemotherapy
- Preoperative
  - Autologous: patient must be fit enough; acceptable FBC and U&E, adequate heart and lung function
  - Allogeneic from a child donor (often a sibling): ethical issue of risk with no personal benefit
- Anaesthetic technique
  - Prone; 60-90 min
  - ETT and IPPV
  - Volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
  - Large harvest (up to 10% of marrow) can cause hypovolaemia and anaemia: replace fluid; delay any transfusion until harvest is complete to avoid contaminating the marrow
  - Many punctures: morphine 100-150 mcg/kg IV or fentanyl 2-4 mcg/kg IV; ondansetron 100 mcg/kg IV
- Postoperative: IV fluids until drinking; paracetamol 15 mg/kg 6-hourly, oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly, NSAID if no contraindication

## Leukaemia
- ALL: 80% of leukaemias and 25% of all childhood cancer; peak at 2-3 years; 30% more common in boys
- AML 15%; chronic myeloid leukaemia <4% of paediatric leukaemias
- Features: lethargy, fever, malaise, marrow failure (petechiae, pallor, bruising), bone pain; anaemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia
- Treatment phases
  - Induction of remission: about 4 weeks; high tumour cell death (tumour lysis)
  - Consolidation: further treatment with or without intrathecal chemotherapy and cranial radiotherapy
  - Maintenance: pulsed chemotherapy over two to three years
- Survival: ALL 5-year survival about 80%; acute non-lymphocytic leukaemia about 55%, rising to 67% with bone marrow transplant

## Lymphoma
- Third commonest childhood cancer
- Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: 60% of paediatric lymphoma; twice as common in boys; steady incidence at 3-14 years
  - 30% T-cell lymphoblastic (similar to ALL); 50% B-cell; 20% large cell (T, B or indeterminate)
  - T-cell NHL: anterior mediastinum, with airway compression, pleural effusions and SVC syndrome
  - B-cell NHL: abdomen, with pain, distension, ascites, bowel obstruction
  - Treated like ALL; 90% remission; 5-year survival about 80%
- Hodgkin lymphoma: 5% of childhood cancer
  - Incidence rises from 2 years, peaking at 15-30 years; firm cervical or supraclavicular nodes (up to 60% also have mediastinal nodes)
  - Stages 1 to 4: 1 = single region, 2 = two regions on one side of the diaphragm, 3 = both sides, 4 = disseminated; plus A (no symptoms) or B (night sweats, fever, weight loss)
  - Chemotherapy and radiotherapy
  - 5-year survival: grade 1A 90-95%; grade 4 65%

## CNS tumours
- Commonest solid tumours in children (20%)
- About 40% astrocytomas (usually low grade) and medulloblastomas; ependymomas and gliomas make up most of the rest
- Infratentorial: raised ICP (headache, nausea and vomiting, nystagmus, ataxia, cranial nerve palsies)
- Supratentorial: seizures, hemiparesis, raised ICP
- Treatment: surgery (biopsy, excision, debulking, shunts), chemotherapy, radiotherapy, sometimes molecular or immunotherapy

## Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma)
- Commonest malignant abdominal tumour in children
- Incidence 0.8:100000; 6-8% of paediatric solid tumours
- Mean age 3.5 years; 90% present before 8 years
- Causes: sporadic mutations 90%; familial 1.5%; 10% syndromic (Beckwith-Wiedemann, isolated hemihypertrophy, trisomy 18, Denys-Drash, WAGR: Wilms, aniridia, genitourinary anomalies, intellectual disability)
- Presentation: usually painless abdominal or flank mass; also pain, haematuria (30%) with anaemia, fever, anorexia, nausea and vomiting
- Hypertension in 50%: renin from trapped or compressed renal cortex drives angiotensin II, causing vasoconstriction and polydipsia
- Investigations: abdominal ultrasound; CT or MRI of abdomen and chest for lung metastases, IVC or atrial thrombus, vascular extension
- Treatment
  - Resection and staging at laparotomy, then chemotherapy with or without radiotherapy
  - Preoperative chemotherapy only for bilateral, unresectable or vascular-extension tumours
  - Stages I and II, favourable histology: 18 weeks vincristine and actinomycin D
  - Stage II anaplastic and stage III: 24 weeks vincristine, actinomycin D and doxorubicin (cardiotoxic)
  - Stages II to IV, worst histology: 24 weeks vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide and etoposide plus radiotherapy
  - Cure in stage I >85%
  - Bilateral in 5% (each side staged): 6-8 weeks of chemotherapy, then function-sparing surgery; 5-year survival 70%, with some renal failure

## Wilms tumour: anaesthetic management
- Preoperative
  - Chemotherapy history, old access sites, renal function, FBC, clotting, crossmatch
  - Up to 10% have acquired von Willebrand disease (platelet dysfunction, coagulopathy): cryoprecipitate and/or FFP on haematology advice
  - Occasional polycythaemia from raised erythropoietin
  - Hypertension treated with ACE inhibitors (captopril) or the angiotensin II antagonist saralasin
  - Review CT; if IVC or atrial extension, also angiography and echocardiography
  - Intravascular extension (renal vein, IVC, right atrium) does not affect survival but may need cardiopulmonary bypass; preoperative chemotherapy shrinks it and lowers complications
- Intraoperative
  - IV induction usually; some use rapid sequence induction for the large upper abdominal mass
  - Intubation and IPPV; nasogastric tube
  - Volatile in oxygen and air; remifentanil 0.1-0.25 mcg/kg/min reduces volatile needs and steadies the circulation
  - Two large-bore peripheral cannulae, ideally in the arms
  - Usually arterial and central lines
  - Labile BP from contracted blood volume, LVH and IVC compression (by tumour or surgical handling); acute hypertension on tumour handling is uncommon
  - Labile or severe hypertension (as with phaeochromocytoma and neuroblastoma): phenoxybenzamine, phentolamine or sodium nitroprusside
  - Replace blood loss with colloid; transfuse if needed
  - Long retroperitoneal surgery: careful warming (mattress, blanket, fluid warmer) and fluid balance; central temperature
  - Thoracic epidural is best if no contraindication (transverse upper abdominal incision to inspect the other kidney); epidural veins may be engorged from IVC compression, but benefits usually outweigh risks
  - Otherwise morphine 100-150 mcg/kg before the end of surgery, topped up in recovery
  - No NSAIDs (significant blood loss, possible renal impairment)
- Postoperative
  - HDU; oxygen for diaphragmatic splinting; FBC and U&E
  - Maintenance fluids plus colloid or blood to keep fluid balance and urine output
  - Epidural (local anaesthetic plus opioid) or IV morphine infusion usually for 48 hours
  - Regular paracetamol; avoid NSAIDs
  - BP may take several weeks to normalise

## Neuroblastoma
- Malignancy of primitive neural crest cells of adrenal medulla and sympathetic ganglia
- Commonest malignancy under 1 year
- 7% of childhood cancers, 15% of childhood cancer deaths
- Over >70% abdominal, 20% thoracic, 5% cervical; of abdominal tumours one-third arise from the retroperitoneal sympathetic chain and two-thirds from the adrenal medulla
- 50% diagnosed <2 years and 75% <4 years
- Usually sporadic; 1 to 2% familial
- Presentation
  - Abdominal mass with calcification and necrosis on CT in 85%; abdominal pain
  - Hypertension (renal vessel compression or catecholamine secretion)
  - Diarrhoea (vasoactive intestinal peptide)
  - Respiratory distress (5% of posterior mediastinal masses compress or deviate the trachea)
  - Epidural invasion: back pain or cord compression
  - Metastases in >50% at diagnosis; tends to invade nearby structures (unlike Wilms and hepatoblastoma, which compress)
  - Associated with neurofibromatosis, Hirschsprung disease and Ondine's curse (central hypoventilation)
- Investigations
  - MRI
  - Catecholamines produced in 85%: urinary VMA and HVA (24-hour collection)
  - mIBG scan
  - Catecholamine excess may cause cardiomyopathy, LVH and heart failure: full cardiac assessment with echocardiography
- Treatment
  - Surgery for localised tumours without metastases; chemotherapy afterwards if unfavourable histology
  - Spread disease: chemotherapy first to shrink, then resection; agents include doxorubicin (dysrhythmias, acute cardiomyopathy), cisplatin, cyclophosphamide, etoposide
  - Radiotherapy for disseminated disease
  - Cure >80-90% for fully resected localised tumours; much lower for metastatic disease unless age <1 year

## Neuroblastoma: anaesthetic management
- Preoperative
  - Chemotherapy history, old access sites, renal function, FBC, U&E, crossmatch
  - Possible airway compression with posterior mediastinal masses
  - Control BP and expand plasma volume as needed: restores intravascular volume, can reverse catecholamine myocardial dysfunction, and reduces intraoperative hypertensive events
  - Hypertensive crises on tumour handling are rare compared with phaeochromocytoma (fewer storage granules, mixed cholinergic and adrenergic cells, lack of enzymes converting dopamine to noradrenaline and noradrenaline to adrenaline), though BP surges can occur
- Intraoperative
  - As for nephroblastoma and phaeochromocytoma
  - Intubation and IPPV; nasogastric tube
  - Volatile in oxygen and air; remifentanil 0.1-0.25 mcg/kg/min
  - Two large-bore peripheral cannulae; usually arterial and central lines
  - BP sometimes labile (contracted volume, LVH); phenoxybenzamine, phentolamine or sodium nitroprusside may be needed
  - Careful warming and fluid balance; central temperature
  - Abdominal tumours: thoracic epidural unless contraindicated; otherwise morphine 100-150 mcg/kg before the end, topped up in recovery; NSAIDs if no contraindication
- Postoperative
  - HDU; oxygen for diaphragmatic splinting; FBC and U&E
  - Maintenance fluids plus colloid or blood as needed
  - Epidural or IV morphine usually for 48 hours; regular paracetamol; NSAIDs if no contraindication

## Phaeochromocytoma in children
- From chromaffin tissue of the adrenal medulla and sympathetic ganglia (around the aortic bifurcation, GI tract, bladder, chest)
- Functional tumours secrete noradrenaline, adrenaline and dopamine
- Rare: 2-8 per million per year; 5-10% in children, mostly teenagers, very rare under 8 years
- Male to female ratio 2:1
- Compared with adults
  - Malignancy less common
  - Bilateral, extra-adrenal or multiple tumours more common
  - Noradrenaline usually predominates
  - More MEN (hyperparathyroidism, medullary thyroid carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma)
  - Associated with neurofibromatosis and tuberous sclerosis
- Presentation: sympathetic overactivity (nausea, vomiting, fatigue, abdominal pain, sweating, headache, palpitations); hypertensive crisis with possible cerebral haemorrhage, pulmonary oedema or heart failure
- Investigations
  - 24 hour urine VMA and HVA (strict protocols; false negatives occur)
  - CT (95% accuracy) and/or MRI (99% accuracy) to locate and plan
  - mIBG scan (80% sensitivity)
- Treatment: surgical removal; laparoscopic excision increasingly common, with less stimulation and quicker recovery than open surgery

## Phaeochromocytoma: preoperative management
- Assess end-organ damage
  - Cardiac: LVH, dilated or obstructive cardiomyopathy, dysrhythmias, ischaemia, heart failure; ECG and echocardiogram
  - Cardiac dysfunction rules out perioperative beta-blockers (negative inotropy: pulmonary oedema, hypotension); it is usually reversible, and treating it lowers morbidity and mortality
  - Also pulmonary oedema, renal impairment, high calcium (hyperparathyroidism), low potassium (high renin, secondary hyperaldosteronism), hyperglycaemia (alpha stimulation in the pancreas reduces insulin)
- Alpha blockade
  - Phenoxybenzamine: alpha1 antagonist with some alpha2 blockade; binds the receptor non-competitively by alkylation
  - Long acting: resistant postoperative hypotension until new receptors are made
  - Children: start 0.25-1.0 mg/kg/day orally and increase until controlled (may take weeks)
  - Adequate blockade: normal BP, orthostatic hypotension, haematocrit falls about 5% from plasma expansion
  - Beta-blockers for reflex tachycardia rarely needed in children
  - Preoperative alpha blockade cut mortality from 50% to 3%

## Phaeochromocytoma: anaesthetic technique
- Minimise adrenergic responses and catecholamine release
- Sedative premedication, e.g. midazolam 0.5 mg/kg oral (maximum 20 mg)
- IV induction in older children
- Intubation and IPPV; spray larynx with lidocaine 1%; remifentanil bolus (0.1-1 mcg/kg) or fentanyl (1-2 mcg/kg) to blunt the laryngoscopy pressor response
- Two large-bore cannulae
- Volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide; ensure adequate depth before positioning and incision; remifentanil 0.1-0.25 mcg/kg/min for stability
- Standard monitoring plus arterial line, central line, urine output, temperature
- Open surgery: thoracic epidural gives good analgesia but does not stop catecholamine release on handling
- Laparoscopic: opioid bolus near the end if on remifentanil, or at the start if not
- Unpredictable BP swings; volatiles give titratable vasodilation; have short-acting vasodilators and pressors ready (BP can fall steeply once the adrenal vein is tied)
  - Sodium nitroprusside 0.5-4 mcg/kg/minute: direct vasodilator, fast on and off; tachyphylaxis; cyanide toxicity only if rate >10 mcg/kg/min
  - Phentolamine 5-50 mcg/kg/min: short-acting alpha antagonist; tachycardia, tachyphylaxis
  - Esmolol 0.5 mg/kg over 1 min or 25-200 mcg/kg/min: short-acting beta-blocker
  - Magnesium sulphate up to 50 mg/kg: reduces catecholamine release, blocks adrenergic receptors, vasodilates, antiarrhythmic
- Hypotension after removal: fluid boluses first, then
  - Phenylephrine 2-10 mcg/kg (alpha agonist)
  - Noradrenaline 0.02-0.1 mcg/kg/min (20-100 nanograms/kg/min)
- Glucose hourly until the adrenal vein is tied, then more often

## Phaeochromocytoma: postoperative care
- HDU; FBC and U&E; maintenance fluids plus colloid as needed
- Hypotension: bleeding or residual alpha blockade
- Transient hypoglycaemia possible: monitor glucose; 10% dextrose if needed
- Open surgery: epidural (local anaesthetic plus opioid) or IV morphine for 48 hours; laparoscopic: morphine infusion or PCA overnight
- Regular paracetamol; NSAID if no contraindication
- Hypertension may persist for several weeks
- Laparoscopic surgery: faster return to feeding and discharge

## Flashcards
- Q: Why keep FiO2 low in a child who has had bleomycin?
  - A: Bleomycin lung injury is oxidant mediated; high FiO2 can worsen it (toxicity in up to 10%).
- Q: Platelet level below which platelets may be given before lumbar puncture?
  - A: <50000/mm3 (no agreed safe level).
- Q: Phenoxybenzamine starting dose in children, and signs of adequate blockade?
  - A: 0.25-1.0 mg/kg/day orally; normal BP, orthostatic hypotension, haematocrit falls about 5%.
- Q: Sodium nitroprusside dose and cyanide threshold in paediatric phaeochromocytoma?
  - A: 0.5-4 mcg/kg/minute; cyanide toxicity if >10 mcg/kg/min.
- Q: Why is nitrous oxide avoided for percutaneous central line insertion?
  - A: Risk of pneumothorax.
