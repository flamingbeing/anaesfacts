# Liver resection

Hepatectomy for tumour: the anaesthetic centres on low-CVP technique to limit bleeding, liver-sparing drugs and watching for postoperative liver failure.

## Indications
- Usually hepatocellular carcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma or metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma

## Extent of resection
- Major resection: 30-75% of the liver removed
- Anatomic resections (6 standard types in the Brisbane classification)
  - Right hemihepatectomy: segments V-VIII
  - Left hepatectomy: segments II-IV, sometimes segment I
  - Extended right hepatectomy (right trisectionectomy): segments IV-VIII
  - Extended left hepatectomy (left trisectionectomy): segments II-IV, V and VIII
  - Left lateral sectorectomy: segments II-III
  - Right posterior sectionectomy: segments VI-VII
  - Segmentectomy: any single segment
- Non-anatomic
  - Wedge resection of superficial lesions
  - Debulking, or resections aimed at preserving remnant parenchyma

## Surgical approach
- Traditionally open
- Laparoscopic increasingly common
  - Fully laparoscopic or hand-assisted
  - Less blood loss, shorter stay
  - Patient selection is key; right posterior lesions near the dome are difficult
  - French position: legs up and apart
- Robotic-assisted
- Staged resection, e.g. ALPPS (associating liver partition and portal vein ligation for staged hepatectomy)

## Relevant anatomy
- Very vascular: about 1.5L/min
  - 80% via portal vein
  - 20% via hepatic artery
- Regenerates by hyperplasia of the remnant
- 9 Couinaud segments, each with its own inflow, outflow and bile duct
- The portal triad sits in a fibrous capsule, so it can be controlled during dissection

## Preoperative assessment
- Eligibility
  - General health
  - Tumour factors, e.g. extent
  - Reserve of the liver remnant (degree of liver dysfunction and remnant size)
  - Laparoscopic: must tolerate pneumoperitoneum and reverse Trendelenburg
- Investigations
  - Child-Pugh score: guides how much can be resected and predicts mortality
    - Ascites, encephalopathy, albumin, bilirubin, PT/INR
  - MELD score
  - Indocyanine green (ICG) retention: measures liver perfusion and biliary excretion; ICG may also be used to locate tumours
- Avoid sedatives such as benzodiazepines that may worsen postoperative encephalopathy
- No IV fluid drip on the ward, to help keep CVP low

## Intraoperative management
- Monitoring
  - Arterial line, with or without CVP
  - Baseline ABG; trend lactate
  - Close glucose monitoring
  - Surgeons may want a nasogastric tube
- Position (laparoscopic)
  - Left arm out
  - Patient pulled down, legs up and apart
  - Head up once pneumoperitoneum is established
- Maintenance
  - Remifentanil
  - Preserve hepatic blood flow: isoflurane or desflurane; avoid N2O
  - Short-acting drugs with minimal hepatic metabolism
  - Acetate-based maintenance fluid (e.g. Plasmalyte) rather than Hartmann's, as acetate metabolism depends less on the liver
- Analgesia
  - Spinal morphine
  - Surgical wound infiltration or wound catheter pump
  - Fentanyl PCA
  - Thoracic epidural works, but RCTs show wound catheters are as good with shorter stay
  - Dexmedetomidine infusion
  - Avoid hepatotoxic drugs; 500mg paracetamol is often still acceptable (confirm with surgeons)
- Other drugs
  - Antibiotic: ceftriaxone
  - Some give esomeprazole

## Preparing for major blood loss
- Rapid infuser/fluid warmer available; can be set up when needed rather than primed
- Large-bore peripheral access, possibly a PA sheath or rapid infusion catheter
- Consider crossmatching up to 10U, with spare units kept ready

## Minimising blood loss
- Why: transfusion is immunosuppressive, and transfusion after major bleeding raises the risk of postoperative liver failure
- Low CVP: aim for CVP 5 mmHg or less (alternatives: pulse pressure variation or stroke volume variation)
  - Restrict fluids; consider vasopressors
    - No more than 1 bag of fluid; clamp the drip after induction
    - Vasopressors as needed to keep SBP over 90 mm Hg and urine output over 25 mL/h
  - Avoid excessive PEEP
  - Head up: may lower CVP but not necessarily hepatic venous pressure
  - Remifentanil, furosemide, GTN infusion, milrinone
  - Thoracic epidural
- Laparoscopic: very low CVP increases the pressure gradient and the risk of gas embolism
- Permissive hypotension: SBP 70-80mmHg (BP usually drifts down anyway with fluid restriction)
- Tranexamic acid
- Keep warm
- Surgical measures
  - CUSA (Cavitron ultrasonic surgical aspirator): about 25 kHz ultrasound destroys liver parenchyma but spares vessels
  - Topical haemostatic agents on the cut surface: gelatine matrix granules that help convert fibrinogen to fibrin
  - Pringle manoeuvre: limits bleeding but risks ischaemic injury
- Once the parenchymal resection is nearly done, the surgeon will say when fluids can be given

## Stages of surgery
- Perihepatic dissection and mobilisation
- Vascular anatomy identified; portal triad controlled with sutures or clips
- Lesion localisation
  - ICG: dissolve 25mg powder in 10ml water (do not prepare early, it degrades in light); give about 2ml (5mg), then flush with saline
  - Intraoperative ultrasound
- Parenchymal transection
  - Clamp-crush, electrocautery, ultrasonic dissection
  - Haemostasis with clips, sutures, argon beam coagulator, haemostatic agents
  - Pringle manoeuvre
    - Intermittent clamping of inflow (portal vein and hepatic artery)
    - Leads to a 10% fall in CO and a 20-30% rise in afterload
    - May cause ischaemic injury
    - Does not stop back-bleeding from the hepatic veins
  - Total vascular exclusion (clamping IVC above and below the liver)
    - Major cardiovascular compromise; CO falls by 60%
- Drains placed and closure

## Postoperative care
- High dependency care to monitor the liver
- Early enteral feeding
- Trend lactate
- Postoperative liver failure
  - Incidence 3%, peaks at 72hrs
  - Signs: coagulopathy and encephalopathy
  - Prevention
    - Preoperative radiological ligation or embolisation of the lobe to be resected, causing hypertrophy of the rest (preconditioning)
    - Limit ischaemia-reperfusion injury
      - Ischaemic preconditioning: short ischaemia, reperfusion, then long ischaemia
      - Intermittent clamping: 10-15min clamped, then 5min perfusion
      - Antioxidants: NAC infusion
- Coagulopathy peaks on day 3 (INR 1.2-1.8) while on LMWH
  - Coincides with epidural removal: consider FFP cover
- Complications in 30%
  - Major bleeding
  - Liver or renal dysfunction (check ammonia if encephalopathy suspected)
  - Respiratory failure
  - Sepsis
  - Intra-abdominal infection
- Self-limiting ascites expected in the first 48hrs

## PROSPECT 2019 analgesia recommendations
- Paracetamol plus an NSAID unless contraindicated, started before or during surgery and continued after
  - Paracetamol hepatotoxicity risk factors: liver disease, age, malnutrition, intraoperative liver ischaemia
  - Kinetics may change with liver disease or major resection, which may affect dose
  - Overall toxicity risk after resection is low, so it remains a basic analgesic
- Regional
  - Thoracic epidural: good analgesia, especially for coughing and deep breathing
  - Bilateral oblique subcostal TAP blocks, single shot and/or continuous infusion
- Systemic opioids only as postoperative rescue

## Flashcards
- Q: What CVP target is used to limit bleeding during liver resection?
  - A: 5 mmHg or less (or use PPV or SVV).
- Q: What are the haemodynamic effects of the Pringle manoeuvre?
  - A: CO falls by 10% and afterload rises by 20-30%.
- Q: What proportion of hepatic blood flow comes from the portal vein?
  - A: 80% (20% from the hepatic artery), total about 1.5L/min.
- Q: When does postoperative liver failure peak and how common is it?
  - A: Peaks at 72hrs; incidence 3%.
- Q: When does coagulopathy peak after hepatectomy and why does it matter?
  - A: Day 3 (INR 1.2-1.8), which coincides with epidural removal.
