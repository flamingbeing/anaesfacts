# Acute Limb Ischaemia and Compartment Syndrome

Embolectomy and fasciotomy: heparinise early, prepare for reperfusion hyperkalaemia and acidosis, and avoid blocks that could mask compartment syndrome.

## Acute limb ischaemia: presentation (6Ps)
- Pain, out of proportion to clinical findings
- Paraesthesia
- Poikilothermia (cool limb)
- Pallor
- Pulselessness (late)
- Paralysis (advanced ischaemia)

## Pathophysiology
- Embolic (sudden onset)
  - Source usually AF, recent MI with mural thrombus, or a proximal aneurysm
  - Little collateral circulation
- Thrombotic (subacute)
  - Pre-existing peripheral vascular disease
  - Some collateral flow present

## Management
- Systemic heparinisation at once
- Surgical embolectomy
- Risks: reperfusion injury and compartment syndrome
- Consider amputation if the limb is not viable, since reperfusion can be fatal

## Intraoperative management
- Choice of technique
  - Local anaesthesia with sedation: preferred in high-risk patients for haemodynamic stability
  - GA when:
    - The patient cannot cooperate
    - Bypass or fasciotomy is likely
  - Neuraxial is contraindicated because of heparinisation
- Reperfusion injury
  - Expect hyperkalaemia, acidosis and hypothermia
  - Have ready: calcium, sodium bicarbonate, insulin, furosemide
  - Consider CRRT
- Fasciotomy for compartment syndrome may also be needed
  - Especially if ischaemia time is >4-6 hours

## Compartment syndrome: presentation
- Pain is the cardinal and early symptom, especially pain out of proportion
- Clinical signs of acute compartment syndrome alone justify fasciotomy
- Palpable distal pulses and normal capillary refill do not rule it out
- Pulse oximetry is insensitive; do not use it to detect compartment syndrome

## Compartment pressure measurement
- Not essential when the clinical picture is convincing
- Indications for fasciotomy: absolute pressure >30 mmHg, or delta pressure <30 mmHg
  - Delta pressure = diastolic blood pressure minus intracompartment pressure

## Anaesthetic considerations for fasciotomy
- GA needed for extensive debridement
- Avoid regional blocks and epidurals in patients at risk of compartment syndrome; analgesia masks early signs
  - Risk is highest with tibial and forearm fractures; avoid blocks here
- Expect large fluid shifts and blood loss
- Myoglobinuria peaks after reperfusion: keep the patient well hydrated with a good urine output

## Flashcards
- Q: List the 6Ps of acute limb ischaemia.
  - A: Pain, paraesthesia, poikilothermia, pallor, pulselessness (late), paralysis (advanced).
- Q: Why is neuraxial anaesthesia contraindicated for embolectomy?
  - A: The patient is systemically heparinised.
- Q: What drugs should be ready for reperfusion injury?
  - A: Calcium, sodium bicarbonate, insulin and furosemide (consider CRRT).
- Q: Which compartment pressures indicate fasciotomy?
  - A: Absolute pressure >30 mmHg or delta pressure (DBP minus compartment pressure) <30 mmHg.
- Q: After how long an ischaemia time is fasciotomy especially likely?
  - A: More than 4-6 hours.
