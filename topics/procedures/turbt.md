# Transurethral Resection of Bladder Tumour (TURBT)

Cystoscopic diathermy resection of a bladder tumour; key risk is the obturator jerk.

## Preoperative assessment
- Often smokers with comorbidities such as COPD and IHD
- Review previous anaesthetic charts: many have had multiple procedures

## Intraoperative management
- General anaesthesia (LMA) or spinal with or without sedation (block must reach above T10)
- Lithotomy position
- Variable blood loss
- Antibiotic prophylaxis, may be guided by urine cultures
- Obturator spasm: surgeon may want muscle relaxation or an obturator nerve block
- Surgeon may ask for a diuretic to flush the bladder; make sure the patient is not hypovolaemic

## Postoperative management
- Marked bladder spasm
- NSAIDs

## Obturator nerve spasm
- The obturator nerve runs next to the lateral bladder wall
- Irrigation distends the lateral wall and brings it close to the nerve
- Diathermy resection of lateral wall tumours can stimulate the nerve, causing a strong leg adductor spasm (the "obturator jerk")
- An obturator jerk greatly raises the risk of bladder perforation

## Prevention and treatment
- Anaesthetic options (often requested when the surgeon works near the nerve)
  - Muscle relaxation: small dose of atracurium; suxamethonium if the procedure is very short
    - An ETT may then be needed to secure the airway
  - Obturator nerve block
  - Spinal or epidural does not prevent obturator spasm
- Surgical options
  - Lower resectoscope current
  - Laser resection
  - Physically restrain the leg

## Flashcards
- Q: What spinal block height is needed for TURBT?
  - A: Above T10.
- Q: Does spinal anaesthesia prevent the obturator jerk?
  - A: No.
- Q: What is the main danger of an obturator jerk during TURBT?
  - A: Bladder perforation.
- Q: What anaesthetic measures reduce obturator spasm?
  - A: Muscle relaxation (small dose atracurium, or suxamethonium if very short) or obturator nerve block.
