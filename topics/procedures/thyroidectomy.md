# Thyroidectomy

Hemi- or total thyroidectomy: shared airway, possible goitre airway compromise, and postoperative risks of haematoma, nerve palsy and hypocalcaemia.

## Surgery
- Hemithyroidectomy or total thyroidectomy, with or without frozen section
- Most retrosternal goitres come out through the neck; a few need a sternotomy
- Duration: 3-4 hrs for a total; can take the whole day if a neck dissection is added
- Blood loss: <100 ml
- Pain: moderate

## Summary
- Preoperative: euthyroid? Airway compromise? Retrosternal extension?
- Position: supine, shoulder roll to extend the neck, slight head up for venous drainage, arms tucked (long extensions)
- Airway: armoured or north-facing RAE tube with straight extension, head wrap and bite block; intubation may be difficult; smooth extubation to reduce bleeding
- Drugs: often TIVA
  - Antiemetics: single or dual (PONV causes bleeding; less airway oedema)
  - Antibiotics: cefazolin
- Monitoring and lines: forced-air warmer, temperature probe
- Special: shared airway (secure airway, tight connections); with or without recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) monitoring (no paralysis, remifentanil, electrode wrapped around the ETT); if not euthyroid, see thyroid considerations
- Complications: tube dislodgement or circuit disconnection, thyroid storm
- Postoperative: airway monitoring in PACU, calcium levels

## Major considerations
- Disease-related
  - Risk of perioperative thyroid disturbance (hyper- or hypothyroidism)
  - Potential airway compromise
    - Goitre with retrosternal extension: mass effect on trachea, SVC obstruction (anterior mediastinal mass)
    - Vocal cord palsy
- Head and neck surgery: shared airway
- Postoperative airway obstruction: haematoma, oedema, vocal cord palsy from RLN injury, tracheomalacia
  - RLN monitoring
- Accidental removal of parathyroids leads to hypocalcaemia

## Preoperative assessment
- Clinical
  - Symptoms and signs of hyper- or hypothyroidism
  - Goitre size and relation to airway; retrosternal extension?
    - Symptoms: positional breathlessness, dysphagia, stridor, voice change
    - Signs of SVC obstruction: face and neck oedema, distended neck veins, Pemberton's sign
- Thyroid function tests
  - Postpone elective surgery until euthyroid (lowers the risk of perioperative thyroid storm or myxoedema coma)
- Corrected calcium
- Airway imaging and assessment
  - CXR: goitre size, tracheal compression or deviation
    - Lateral thoracic inlet views: retrosternal extension and tracheal anteroposterior diameter
    - Any airway concern: CT neck and thorax for the extent and level of tracheal narrowing or invasion
  - Nasendoscopy (usually by ENT): documents vocal cord function; very useful to the anaesthetist for seeing the laryngeal inlet and distorted anatomy
  - Flow-volume loops may show fixed upper airway obstruction but are seldom useful as a routine test

## Preoperative orders
- Give usual antithyroid drugs on the day of surgery
  - Some stop carbimazole on the morning of surgery, as it makes the gland more vascular and may increase bleeding

## Intraoperative management
### Technique
- General anaesthesia is now preferred
- Regional anaesthesia is an option (see below)
- Historically done under local anaesthesia

### Position
- Head fully extended on a padded ring, sandbag or roll between the scapulae
- Head-up tilt helps venous drainage; watch arterial pressure
- Pad the eyes well, especially with exophthalmos
- Avoid neck ties

### Monitoring and access
- Arms by the sides, so long drip extensions help
- Hang the bed controller on the machine

### Airway
- Intubate
  - Consider alternatives if there is any doubt about airway patency or distorted anatomy
  - An LMA can rescue ventilation, but is of doubtful use here: tracheal compression or deviation, retrosternal goitre, abnormal cord movement and possible malignancy all make the airway hard to secure
- Armoured ETT or north-facing RAE tube preferred: least likely to kink
  - The surgical team needs space at the head, so a plain PVC tube can kink under the drapes
  - Pass the tube beyond any point of external compression
  - Tape north, head wrap, tube extension, straight board to hold the tube, tourniquet to hold the circuit
- Electrode tube may be needed if the RLN is monitored
- Little access to the airway during surgery: tape the tube securely
  - A fall in airway pressure suggests disconnection, usually at the surgical field

### Drugs
- TIVA
- Good muscle relaxation is essential unless nerve monitoring is used
- Remifentanil infusion is common
  - Less need for relaxant, allowing intraoperative RLN testing in complex cases
  - Can be titrated to blood pressure for a dry field during dissection, then allowed back to normal or above-normal pressure before closure to check haemostasis (may need phenylephrine boluses)
  - Helps reduce coughing at emergence
- If neuromuscular function is monitored: avoid paralysis, use remifentanil

### End of procedure
- Surgeon may ask for a Valsalva to check haemostasis
  - Switch to bag mode, set the APL valve to 30 and squeeze the bag continuously
- If RLN integrity is in doubt, look at the cords with a laryngoscope or a fibreoptic scope through an LMA (if in place or placed after deep extubation)
- Extubation
  - Fully reverse neuromuscular block, sit the patient up, deflate the cuff to confirm a leak
  - Usually extubated awake; minimise airway handling and head and neck movement to avoid coughing and straining
  - Topical lidocaine to the cords at intubation helps a smooth emergence
  - Alternatives: deep extubation, or IV lidocaine 1.5 mg/kg
- Steroids (e.g. dexamethasone 8 mg) may reduce airway oedema after a long or difficult case

## Regional anaesthesia
- Uncommon; used alone (with or without sedation) or with GA for analgesia
  - Benefits: avoids GA, allows voice monitoring during surgery, excellent postoperative analgesia
  - Suits medically unfit patients (including thyrotoxicosis) or those with obstructive symptoms from large goitres
  - Risks: local anaesthetic toxicity, haematoma, pneumothorax; needs a very cooperative patient
  - Rarely used in the UK; used as the sole technique in low-resource settings
- Bilateral C2-C4 superficial cervical plexus block, fully monitored, with or without sedation
  - Sedation: midazolam increments or propofol TCI
  - Bilateral deep cervical plexus blocks have more complications: vertebral artery or subdural injection, bilateral phrenic palsy (poorly tolerated by some)
  - Blocks must be bilateral for thyroidectomy
  - Midline field block: subcutaneous line from thyroid cartilage to suprasternal notch, covers retractor pain medially
- Cervical epidural is also possible

## Recurrent laryngeal nerve monitoring
- ETT electrode
  - EMG tracheal tubes have four surface electrodes that detect cord movement
  - Or surface electrodes wrapped around a normal ETT (use a slightly smaller tube)
  - Electrodes must touch the true cords: correct depth, not rotated; a videolaryngoscope lets the surgeon confirm placement
- Ground electrode: subcutaneous over the sternum or on the skin of the shoulder
- Evidence: nerve monitoring does not significantly reduce RLN palsy rates

## Postoperative management
- Analgesia
  - Surgeon's subcutaneous local anaesthetic with adrenaline before incision gives some postoperative cover
  - Regular paracetamol, NSAIDs and weak opioids usually suffice; morphine sometimes needed
  - Bilateral superficial cervical plexus blocks markedly reduce pain and morphine use
- PONV: high risk; ondansetron and/or cyclizine plus dexamethasone (which may also reduce airway oedema)
- Thyrotoxic patients: removing the gland does not end thyrotoxicosis at once; T4 half-life is 7–8 days, so beta-blockers may need to continue

## Complications
### Immediate
- Airway obstruction (surgical)
  - Haematoma: 0.4%
    - Staple removers or stitch cutters must stay with the patient; treatment is to drain the haematoma
    - Airway compression and venous and lymphatic obstruction cause laryngeal oedema needing early reintubation
  - RLN injury
    - Unilateral: hoarseness
    - Bilateral: stridor and severe respiratory distress, needing reintubation
  - Laryngeal oedema: steroids, humidified oxygen, nebulised adrenaline; may need reintubation
  - Tracheomalacia: immediate reintubation, possible tracheostomy; some evidence it is rare with modern surgery
- Hypocalcaemia: 1/3 transient, 3% permanent
  - Perioral tingling, twitching or tetany, carpopedal spasm on BP cuff inflation (Trousseau's sign)
  - Facial spasm on tapping over the parotid (Chvostek's sign)
  - Prolonged QT
  - Calcium < 2 mmol/L needs urgent IV calcium gluconate 10%
- Pneumothorax, haemothorax or chylothorax, especially with retrosternal goitre
- Thyroid storm
- PONV

### Late
- Hypothyroidism
- Wound infection
- Neuropraxia at the operative site (problems shaving for men)

## Respiratory distress in recovery after total thyroidectomy
- Call for help, inform the surgeon
- Differentials
  - Surgical: haematoma, RLN palsy, laryngeal oedema, tracheomalacia (especially malignant goitre), hypocalcaemia (unlikely early), pneumothorax from mediastinal dissection
  - Anaesthetic: opioid excess, inadequate reversal, anaphylaxis
  - Patient: heart failure, asthma, anxiety
- Assess and support ABC
  - Review history, anaesthetic record and recovery staff concerns
  - Check SpO2, pulse, BP; airway patency, stridor, breathing
  - Suction, exclude supraglottic obstruction; examine neck, trachea and drains
  - High-flow oxygen by non-rebreather, or bag-mask with self-inflating bag or Mapleson circuit; intubate at once if breathing cannot be supported
- Targeted management
  - Airway swelling: nebulised adrenaline
  - Neck swelling with suspected venous or lymphatic oedema: sit up at 45 degrees; reopen wound and deep cervical fascia to evacuate haematoma (at bedside if in extremis, otherwise urgent return to theatre)
    - Expect a distorted, oedematous airway; have difficult airway backup; consider spontaneous breathing induction and laryngoscopy before relaxant
  - Bilateral RLN palsy: cords paramedian, difficulty speaking, stridor on agitation or obstruction (hoarseness suggests unilateral); nasendoscopy if unclear; urgent intubation then tracheostomy may be needed
  - Suspected tracheomalacia: bronchoscopy under spontaneous ventilation; tracheostomy may be needed
  - Reverse neuromuscular block if indicated
  - Treat pre-existing conditions after investigation (bronchodilators, steroid nebuliser, diuretics, chest drain)

## Intraoperative thyroid storm
- Can occur during surgery but more often 6 to 18 hours after
- Thought to follow handling of the gland in hyperthyroid patients
  - Other triggers
    - Thyroid: stopping antithyroid drugs, radioiodine, iodinated contrast
    - Other stressors: other surgery, infection, stroke, heart failure, PE, pregnancy or labour, DKA, trauma
- Mainly in poorly treated patients; unusual if controlled on antithyroid drugs

### Diagnosis
- Burch-Wartofsky Point Scale (BWPS)
  - 25–44: suggests impending storm
  - \>45: highly suggests storm
  - Under anaesthesia parts of the score (e.g. CNS features, heavily weighted) cannot be assessed
- Intraoperative clues: hyperpyrexia and tachycardia
  - Rising heart rate, arrhythmias, heart failure, hypotension
  - Rising temperature, higher O2 consumption and CO2 production, acidosis
- Awake: GI and mental status changes
  - CNS: agitation, tremor, delirium, coma
  - GI: diarrhoea, abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting

### Management
- Emergency (mortality 20%): urgent endocrinology input, ABC
- IV fluid resuscitation
- Active cooling (cooling blankets, cold IV fluid)
- The 5 Bs
  - Block sympathetic effects: beta-blockade treats fever, tachycardia, tremor; caution in heart failure
    - Esmolol 0.25-0.5 mg/kg bolus or 50-200 mcg/kg/min infusion
    - Propranolol 10-40 mg PO or up to 1 mg/min IV
  - Block synthesis: PTU 200-400 mg PO/NG/PR 6-hourly
  - Block release: potassium iodide 5 drops PO/NG 6-hourly or sodium iodide 0.25 g IV 6-hourly (1 hr after PTU); lithium if iodine allergic
  - Block T4 to T3 conversion: hydrocortisone 100-200 mg IV 8-hourly
- Look for and treat complications
  - Stroke, loss of consciousness
  - MI, AF (avoid amiodarone because of its iodine; use digoxin), heart failure
  - Hypoventilation and hypercarbia
  - Electrolyte disturbance
- Last-resort options: plasmapheresis, dantrolene, lithium, neuraxial block to T4, cholestyramine (blocks enterohepatic circulation)
- Differentials for a hypermetabolic state
  - Malignant hyperthermia: ETCO2 and O2, blood gas, urine colour
  - Phaeochromocytoma
  - Carcinoid crisis

## Flashcards
- Q: What is the risk of haematoma after thyroidectomy and the immediate treatment?
  - A: 0.4%; open the wound and drain it (stitch cutters or staple removers kept with the patient)
- Q: How common is hypocalcaemia after thyroidectomy, and when is IV calcium urgent?
  - A: 1/3 transient, 3% permanent; urgent IV calcium gluconate 10% if calcium < 2 mmol/L
- Q: Esmolol dosing in thyroid storm?
  - A: 0.25-0.5 mg/kg bolus or 50-200 mcg/kg/min infusion
- Q: When should iodide be given relative to PTU in thyroid storm?
  - A: 1 hr after PTU
- Q: Why can beta-blockers need to continue after surgery for thyrotoxicosis?
  - A: T4 half-life is 7–8 days, so thyrotoxicosis does not resolve at once
