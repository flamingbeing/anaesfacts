# Endovascular Aortic Aneurysm Repair (EVAR and TEVAR)

Stent-graft repair of aortic aneurysms: key issues are vasculopathic patients, heparin, radiation, controlled hypotension and breath-hold for deployment, and protection of kidneys, spinal cord and brain.

## Key considerations
- Elective EVAR: vasculopathic patients, heparinisation, radiation, graft deployment, organ protection
- Emergency EVAR: start under LA, aortic balloon occlusion, abdominal compartment syndrome
- TEVAR: graft deployment and organ protection are critical (spinal cord, stroke); right radial arterial line; type 1a endoleak

## Overview
- Anaesthesia:
  - EVAR: GA, regional or LA all possible; start with LA for ruptured AAA
  - TEVAR: GA needed to limit movement
- Position: supine
- Approach: percutaneous or femoral cut-down
- Procedure:
  - P0 emergency if ruptured AAA; arterial line
  - Radiation: image intensifier or hybrid theatre
  - Heparin with ACT monitoring
  - Graft deployment: blood pressure control and breath-hold, especially TEVAR
- Duration: 1-3hrs; 4-12hrs if difficult or with extra procedures
- Blood loss: usually small but can be massive
  - RIGHT radial arterial line for TEVAR
  - Large-bore IV access, group and crossmatch, fluid warmer and rapid infuser (Hotline, Belmont)
  - Ruptured AAA: plan for massive transfusion; ROTEM-guided correction of coagulopathy; avoid the lethal diamond
- Special points:
  - Spinal cord protection, especially TEVAR: consider spinal drain and neuromonitoring
  - Renal protection (no parecoxib), especially TEVAR
  - Induced hypotension with breath-hold during graft placement
  - Blood pressure control with balloon occlusion: remifentanil, vasopressors and vasodilators
- Complications:
  - Arterial injury and massive bleeding, conversion to open
  - Endoleak
  - Postimplantation syndrome
  - Abdominal compartment syndrome after ruptured AAA

## Elective EVAR

### EVAR compared with open repair
- Benefits of EVAR:
  - Minimally invasive; no laparotomy or aortic cross-clamp
  - Less blood loss and fluid shift
  - Fewer haemodynamic swings and a smaller stress response
  - Less pain, earlier mobilisation, shorter stay
- Drawbacks:
  - Higher procedure cost
  - More **long-term surveillance and re-intervention** (endoleak, graft migration)
- Mortality:
  - 30 days mortality with EVAR: 1.7% in patients fit for open repair, up to 9% in those unfit for open repair
  - Elective open AAA repair: 5% mortality

### Preoperative assessment
- Older patients with severe cardiac, respiratory and renal comorbidity

### Setting
- **Hybrid theatre with angiography**, able to **convert to open repair**
- Radiation protection

### Anaesthetic technique
- **Local or MAC**: short, simple infrarenal cases
- **Regional (CSE or epidural)**:
  - Avoids GA and ventilation
  - Blunts stress response
  - Good postoperative analgesia
  - Patients are often on **antiplatelets** and will get **heparin**; plan **catheter removal timing** around residual anticoagulation
- **GA**: the practical standard
  - Precise blood pressure control, breath-holds for angiography
  - Secure airway if rupture occurs
  - Tolerates long supine time and transient leg ischaemia
- Choosing a technique depends on:
  - Comorbidity
  - Length of procedure and ability to lie flat throughout
  - Antiplatelet and anticoagulant use

### Monitoring and access
- **Arterial line**: blood gases and ACT
  - Haemodynamic instability is usually small unless aortic occlusion balloons are used (mainly in rupture)
- Central venous access for complex or long cases
- **Large-bore IV access**: major bleeding possible if converted to open (<2%)
- 5-lead ECG, hourly urine output, temperature
- Neuromonitoring if high risk of spinal cord ischaemia

### Intraoperative management
- **Blood pressure control**
  - Hypertension may cause **rupture**; hypotension may cause **myocardial, cerebral, renal or spinal cord ischaemia**
  - Hypotension after induction is common because surgical stimulus is small; low-dose **phenylephrine** infusion often needed
- **Heparin and ACT**
  - Usually **5000 IU IV heparin after access vessel cannulation**
  - Keep ACT at 2–2.5 times baseline (about **200–250 s**)
  - Protamine reversal usually not needed
- **Device deployment**
  - **Breath-hold and lower MAP** during stent positioning, especially **complex suprarenal repairs** with fenestrated or branched stents
    - **Controlled hypotension** (MAP 50-60) with **propofol** (10 to 30 mg boluses), **esmolol** (10 to 30 mg), **remifentanil or GTN**
    - **Adenosine or rapid ventricular pacing** in TEVAR; rarely in EVAR
  - Low heart rate may be requested
  - **Breath-hold**: stop ventilation under GA; awake patients hold their breath
- **Blood loss** from large-bore sheaths: may go into the drapes and be hard to judge
- **Renal protection** (contrast nephropathy risk):
  - Adequate hydration
  - Maintain MAP and renal perfusion
  - Avoid nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides)
  - Limit contrast
  - No convincing evidence for acetylcysteine or bicarbonate
- **Spinal cord protection**:
  - **Lower risk than TEVAR**
  - Risk rises sharply if the **internal iliac** arteries are covered
- Conversion to open:
  - Rare (<2%), but be ready for **open repair and massive bleeding** (e.g. arterial rupture)
  - Rapid infuser immediately available; crossmatch as for open repair

### Postoperative management
- **Pain is usually minimal**: simple oral analgesia or titrated opioid
- **Limb ischaemia**: regular clinical and Doppler checks of the legs
- **Renal**: continue IV fluids to prevent AKI and contrast nephropathy
- **Coagulopathy**: consumption and fibrinolysis from thrombus in the excluded sac
- **Postimplantation syndrome**

## Emergency EVAR for ruptured AAA
- EVAR is increasingly first-line for rupture

### Preoperative management
- As for open repair: rapid focused assessment, large-bore cannulae, crossmatch, **restrictive resuscitation (hypotensive haemostasis)**
- **CT** (if stable) lets surgeon and radiologist decide if anatomy is **suitable for EVAR**

### Intraoperative management
- Technique: **LA or GA**
  - LA keeps sympathetic tone; GA induction in shocked patients often causes immediate cardiovascular collapse
  - Option: **start under LA or MAC** for femoral access and balloon placement; convert to GA once the balloon is up or if the patient cannot cope
  - Avoid regional anaesthesia: risk of **coagulopathy** from bleeding and massive transfusion
  - Reasons to convert to GA:
    - Pain from expanding haematoma and endovascular manipulation
    - Ventilatory compromise from diaphragmatic splinting by haematoma
    - Ischaemic buttock or leg pain when internal iliac or femoral arteries are occluded
    - Delirium and restlessness from metabolic and cardiovascular instability
    - Secondary procedures such as embolectomy or femoral-femoral crossover graft
- As for open ruptured AAA:
  - **Arterial line before induction; CVC after**
  - **Large-bore IV access**
  - Rapid infuser, cell salvage, inotropes, vasopressors and vasodilators ready; **blood** available
  - **Permissive hypotension**
  - Coagulopathy: unlike elective EVAR, rupture causes **consumptive and dilutional coagulopathy**; early massive transfusion protocol and **ROTEM**
- **Balloon occlusion**
  - Supracoeliac aortic balloon (**REBOA**) for bleeding control if unstable; effects like a cross-clamp
  - Have **noradrenaline** and **GTN** ready

### Postoperative management
- **Abdominal compartment syndrome** risk from retroperitoneal haematoma

## TEVAR for thoracic aortic aneurysm

### Differences from EVAR
- Higher **stroke** risk: wires in an atheromatous arch; left common carotid coverage if the graft migrates
- Higher **spinal cord injury** risk
- Harder proximal landing zone deployment

### Intraoperative management
- **GA** for precise placement (breath-hold, haemodynamic control); TEE
- Monitoring:
  - **RIGHT radial arterial line**: left subclavian may be covered on purpose or by accident
    - **Consider a femoral arterial line** to track lower body perfusion
  - **NIRS for cerebral perfusion** (stroke risk)
  - **Consider TEE** (retrograde dissection risk)
- **Breath-hold and controlled hypotension** during deployment matter more here: high flow, pulsatility and nearness to the carotids
  - May need **adenosine**
  - May need **rapid ventricular pacing**:
    - Needs temporary pacing wires
    - Usually paced at 180bpm
    - Faster on and off than drugs
    - Risk of myocardial ischaemia and arrhythmias
- Spinal cord protection:
  - **Keep MAP >80-90 after graft deployment**, with noradrenaline if needed
  - **Lumbar CSF drain** if long coverage
  - **Neuromonitoring** possible: TIVA without paralysis
  - Early neurological exam after extubation
- Left subclavian bypass may be done (open, before TEVAR) if subclavian coverage is planned

### Postoperative management
- **Type 1a endoleak** is common and may need urgent reintervention

## TEVAR for thoracoabdominal aneurysm
- Needs **fenestrated (fTEVAR)** or **branched (bTEVAR)** grafts to keep gut and renal flow
- Technically hard to align; higher endoleak risk

## Complications of endovascular repair
- Early:
  - **Failed deployment, conversion to open** (<2%)
  - **Arterial rupture, dissection**
  - **Ischaemia or embolism**: spinal cord, renal, bowel, lower limb and buttock
  - **Endoleak**
  - Medical: ACS, heart failure, pulmonary complications, stroke, AKI, coagulopathy
  - Postimplantation syndrome
- Late:
  - Endoleak
  - Graft infection, thrombosis or migration
- **Acute kidney injury (especially AAA)**:
  - **Contrast nephropathy** from IV contrast
  - **Renal ischaemia** from para-renal stents or emboli
  - Also: age, comorbidity, complex repair, dehydration, ACE inhibitors, aminoglycosides, diuretics
- **Spinal cord ischaemia** (especially thoracic and thoracoabdominal)
- Thoracic repairs:
  - **Stroke (the main risk in thoracic aneurysm repair)**
  - **Retrograde type A dissection**
  - **Left arm ischaemia**

## Endoleak
- Type I: **poor seal at graft ends**
  - Ia: **proximal (commonest after TEVAR)**
    - Poor proximal seal from "bird-beak" effect with high-velocity flow, steep arch angle or short landing zone
    - Treatment: **urgent stenting**
  - Ib: distal; treatment: stent
- Type II: **retrograde filling from a branch** (usually lumbar or inferior mesenteric artery)
  - Commonest leak after EVAR
  - Also called a "retroleak"
  - Most **resolve spontaneously**; can coil or glue
- Type III: **graft fabric failure** (**mechanical**)
  - IIIa: separation at modular junctions
  - IIIb: fracture or hole in the graft
  - Treatment: new stent
- Type IV: **porous graft material**; rare with modern grafts
- Type V: **endotension**: sac keeps growing from transmitted pulsation without a visible leak
  - May need relining or open repair
- Types 1,3,5 may need re-intervention

## Postimplantation syndrome
- **Common and usually benign** after EVAR or TEVAR
- **Sterile inflammatory response**: **fever, raised white cells and inflammatory markers**; looks like sepsis without infection
- Prevention: IV **dexamethasone**; NSAIDs if renal function allows
- **Diagnosis of exclusion**: rule out infection; treat with antipyretics and fluids
- Usually self-limiting, settling within 2 weeks

## Renal failure and renal protection

### Causes
- Pre-renal:
  - Aortic **clamping** in open repair
    - Worse if **suprarenal** (stops renal blood flow)
    - Infrarenal clamps can also cause **turbulent flow and renal vasoconstriction** (sympathetic surge, renin-angiotensin activation)
  - Renal artery trauma
  - **Plaque embolism** during endovascular work
  - Ischaemic rhabdomyolysis, especially after rupture
  - **Hypotension, low cardiac output**, low renal perfusion pressure
  - **Hypovolaemia**, bleeding
- Renal:
  - **Contrast nephropathy**
  - Nephrotoxins before or during surgery (ACE inhibitors, aminoglycosides)

### Prevention
- Preoperative:
  - Good **hydration**: avoid dehydration, stop diuretics
  - **Stop nephrotoxins** (ACE inhibitors, diuretics)
  - Stop smoking, control glucose
- Intraoperative:
  - Clamp infrarenally where possible; minimise cross-clamp time
  - Limit contrast; acetylcysteine sometimes used, weak evidence
  - **Maintain MAP** (know the baseline) and **oxygen delivery**
  - **Good IV hydration, avoid hypovolaemia**
  - **Good urine output**
  - Drugs:
    - **Mannitol** before cross-clamp: osmotic diuresis and antioxidant effect
    - Methylprednisolone (rarely used)
    - Dopamine (obsolete)
- Postoperative:
  - Monitor urine output
  - Careful ICU care; treat sepsis, rhabdomyolysis, bleeding

## Spinal cord ischaemia and protection
- Spinal cord is at risk once grafts are deployed

### Anatomy and blood supply
- Artery of Adamkiewicz: largest collateral; supplies most of the anterior spinal artery and so the anterior third of the cord
  - Arises anywhere from T8 to L2, most often **T9 to T12**; at risk from grafts extending **suprarenally**
- **Long grafts** cover more thoracic and lumbar collaterals
- Intrinsic supply: anterior median spinal artery and paired posterior spinal arteries
- Feeding vessels:
  - Aortic segmental arteries and the **artery of Adamkiewicz**
  - **Subclavian branches (vertebral arteries)**
  - **Internal iliac branches**

### Risk factors
- Physiological:
  - Perioperative **hypotension** lowers **spinal cord perfusion pressure (SCPP)**
  - Diabetes, end-stage renal failure
- Loss of feeding arteries:
  - **Segmental artery sacrifice**, including the **artery of Adamkiewicz** (typically **T8–L1**); risk adds up with each intercostal pair covered
    - Crawford II thoracoabdominal aneurysm has the highest risk (up to 15-20%): whole descending thoracic and abdominal aorta
  - **Subclavian coverage** without revascularisation: vertebral arteries are key collaterals; risk rises markedly without a bypass (e.g. carotid-subclavian)
  - **Internal iliac injury or coverage**: they supply retrograde flow via lumbar and sacral arteries
- Procedural: cross-clamp time (open repair); emboli from graft or clamp
- **Emergency cases**: rupture or symptomatic dissection carry more risk (**instability and no time for protection** such as a CSF drain)

### Prevention
- **Lumbar drain** keeping **CSF pressure <10mmHg**
  - SCPP = MAP - CSF pressure
  - Draining CSF lowers CSF pressure, so SCPP rises at the same MAP
- **Induced hypertension: MAP >80-90 after deployment** (to keep perfusion pressure at 70mmHg)
- Others:
  - **Distal perfusion techniques**
  - **Systemic or regional hypothermia** (cooling the cord via an epidural catheter)
  - Drugs: calcium channel blockers, steroids, naloxone
  - Staged stenting
  - Short procedure time

### Monitoring
- **SSEP and MEP** for cord monitoring; use **TIVA**
  - SSEPs may be unreliable during cross-clamping: they test only posterior columns, while the anterior motor columns are most at risk
- Early neurological exam after extubation

## Lumbar CSF drain
- Placement:
  - Day before surgery or morning of surgery, often by **neurosurgery**
  - May be placed after surgery if neurology deteriorates
- Intraoperative:
  - Drain max 10mL/hr
  - Target CSF pressure <10mmHg
  - Drain 10ml if SSEP signals fall
- Postoperative:
  - Clamp after confirming both legs move normally
  - Remove after 24 hrs clamped
  - Reopen and drain if delayed paraparesis or paraplegia
  - If CSF becomes bloody, stop draining and consider CT or MRI

## Flashcards
- Q: Which arm should carry the arterial line for TEVAR, and why?
  - A: Right radial, as the left subclavian may be covered
- Q: What ACT target is used during EVAR, and what heparin dose is typical?
  - A: 2–2.5 times baseline (about 200–250 s); 5000 IU IV heparin
- Q: What MAP target protects the spinal cord after TEVAR deployment?
  - A: MAP >80-90 (perfusion pressure about 70mmHg)
- Q: What are the CSF drain limits in aortic repair?
  - A: CSF pressure <10mmHg; drain max 10mL/hr; remove after 24 hrs clamped
- Q: Which endoleak is commonest after EVAR, and which after TEVAR?
  - A: Type II after EVAR; type Ia after TEVAR
