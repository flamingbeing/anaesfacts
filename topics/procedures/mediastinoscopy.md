# Mediastinoscopy

Inspection and biopsy of tumours and nodes in the superior and anterior mediastinum; key risks are great vessel bleeding and compression of the brachiocephalic artery.

## Procedure
- Access via a small suprasternal or anterior intercostal incision
- 2 types
  - Cervical (more common)
    - Reaches pretracheal, paratracheal and anterior subcarinal nodes
    - Scope passes beneath the manubrium
  - Transthoracic (Chamberlain procedure, anterior mediastinotomy)
    - More extensive; allows dissection of aortopulmonary nodes
    - Small incision at the left second interspace
- Supine; patient must be still, as movement can cause scope injury to nearby organs
- Safe in experienced hands: mortality under 0.1%, morbidity about 1.5%

## Main considerations
- Brachiocephalic (innominate) artery compression
  - In cervical mediastinoscopy it lies directly in front of the scope
  - Risk of stroke: monitor the right arm pulse (SpO2 or arterial line)
  - BP from the right arm is unreliable
- Major haemorrhage from great vessel injury: 0.4%
  - Vessels most often injured: azygos vein, innominate artery, pulmonary artery
- Anterior mediastinal mass (AMM) issues
  - Mass effect: compression of airway or great vessels
  - Cancer: metastases, medications, metabolic effects
- Immobility essential
- Shared airway in cervical mediastinoscopy: neck extended and turned, so the tube can dislodge

## Preoperative assessment
- Assess any anterior mediastinal mass
- Agree extent of dissection with the surgeon
- Valid group and crossmatch

## Intraoperative management
- Anaesthetic technique
  - Cervical: usually GA with ETT (LMA has been used)
  - Anterior: can be done under local anaesthesia
  - IPPV via a reinforced ETT; TIVA
  - IV fentanyl
- Position
  - Supine if tolerated, otherwise head up
  - Shoulder roll, head ring, arms by the sides
- Monitoring
  - Arterial line often needed for beat-to-beat BP, to detect vessel compression or massive bleeding
  - Right arm pulse monitoring for brachiocephalic compression
    - Pulse oximeter on the right
    - Right radial arterial line on the scope side, with non-invasive BP on the left arm for comparison
  - Watch tidal volume for surgical compression of the airway
- Induction: high-risk AMM needs a specific plan
- Blood loss
  - Large-bore IV before biopsies; consider a leg cannula
  - Blood immediately available
  - Catastrophic bleeding can need immediate sternotomy
- Immobility
  - Avoid coughing
  - Once the tube is secured, keep deep paralysis
- If thoracotomy may follow straight away, choose drugs that suit a procedure cut short by the mediastinoscopy findings

## Anterior mediastinal mass and SVC obstruction
- Tracheobronchial compression
  - Ventilation can become difficult and worsen with induction or IPPV, especially in children
  - Mechanism: loss of chest wall tone and cephalad shift of the diaphragm remove the transmural distending pressure, leading to complete obstruction
  - Keep spontaneous breathing (very important)
  - Consider awake fibreoptic intubation
  - Rigid bronchoscope ready for rescue
- SVC syndrome
  - Poor venous drainage from the head: large tongue, laryngeal oedema, hoarseness, more bleeding
  - Treat with head elevation, steroids, diuretics

## Complications
- Brachiocephalic artery compression leading to stroke (reduced flow to right arm and right carotid)
- Tracheal compression or injury
- Major haemorrhage from great vessels (most common and important)
  - Pack or use the mediastinoscope to tamponade the bleeding
  - Emergency sternotomy or thoracotomy may be needed
  - One-lung ventilation may be needed; a bronchial blocker may be quicker
  - Activate the massive transfusion protocol
  - Get lower limb venous access quickly
- Pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum
- Oesophageal injury
- Thoracic duct injury leading to chylothorax
- Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury (left more than right); bilateral injury can be life threatening
- Phrenic nerve injury
- Venous air embolism if head up and a vein is opened
- Infection, tumour seeding, haemiparesis, tracheal collapse after the procedure
- Complications may need urgent conversion to sternotomy or lateral thoracotomy, depending on stability

## Postoperative care
- Disposition depends on the severity of the AMM
- Postoperative CXR
- Simple analgesia
- Monitor for pneumothorax, bleeding and cerebral ischaemia

## Flashcards
- Q: Why put the pulse oximeter or arterial line on the right arm in cervical mediastinoscopy?
  - A: The scope can compress the brachiocephalic artery, reducing right arm and right carotid flow (stroke risk).
- Q: What is the risk of major haemorrhage during mediastinoscopy?
  - A: 0.4%; most often azygos vein, innominate or pulmonary artery.
- Q: Quoted mortality and morbidity of mediastinoscopy?
  - A: Mortality under 0.1%, morbidity about 1.5%.
- Q: Where is the incision for a Chamberlain procedure?
  - A: Left second interspace.
- Q: Which recurrent laryngeal nerve is more often injured?
  - A: The left.
