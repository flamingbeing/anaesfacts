# Lower Limb Angioplasty and Stenting

Long endovascular procedure in vasculopaths, often off-site, with contrast, heparin and radiation; regional or MAC usually preferred.

## Major considerations
- Long procedure
  - Risk of hypothermia, DVT, pressure sores and nerve injury
- Remote site or hybrid theatre
  - Unfamiliar help, drugs and equipment
  - Bring drugs, resuscitation drugs and equipment; plan for contingencies
- Limited access to patient and lines: use long extensions
- Radiation
  - Lead gowns for staff
  - No pregnant staff in theatre
- Contrast
  - Renal injury: avoid hypovolaemia and nephrotoxic drugs (e.g. NSAIDs); use iso-osmolar contrast
  - Contrast allergy: cross-reactions between iodinated agents are common (iodixanol (Visipaque), iopamidol, iomeprol, iohexol (Omnipaque), ioversol, ioxitalamate)
  - After a hypersensitivity reaction a safe alternative is sometimes hard, occasionally impossible, to name because cross-reactivity is high
- Heparin
  - May contraindicate regional blocks
  - Increases bleeding
  - Protamine reversal can cause hypotension and pulmonary hypertension
- Reperfusion injury
  - Hyperkalaemia, lactic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis
  - Check ECG and ABG
- Bleeding: large volumes can be hidden in the drapes at the puncture site
- Technique choice: GA, regional or MAC
  - Regional or MAC is ideal, avoiding GA-related instability, but the patient must cooperate and lie still for a long time
- Chronic pain
  - Multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia
  - Consider chronic pain team referral

## Preoperative assessment
- Usually vasculopaths
- Pacemaker: usually no special action needed

## Anaesthetic technique
- Local anaesthesia with sedation (MAC)
- Regional (usually preferred)
  - Unilateral: popliteal plus saphenous block
  - Bilateral: spinal (less cardiovascularly stable)
- GA if the patient cannot tolerate the above

## Intraoperative management
- Balloon inflation is painful
- Revascularisation can release toxic metabolites
- Surgeon may inject IV GTN at the angioplasty site
  - 100mcg bolus (1mg diluted in 10ml)
  - Anticipate hypotension: give phenylephrine at the same time
- Heparin is usually given locally by the surgeons

## End of procedure
- Groin is compressed for about 15 min, so the patient cannot be moved out quickly

## Flashcards
- Q: Typical intra-arterial GTN dose during angioplasty?
  - A: 100mcg bolus (1mg diluted in 10ml), with phenylephrine ready.
- Q: Regional technique for unilateral lower limb angioplasty?
  - A: Popliteal plus saphenous block.
- Q: How to reduce contrast-induced renal injury?
  - A: Avoid hypovolaemia and nephrotoxic drugs; use iso-osmolar contrast.
- Q: Risks of reperfusion after revascularisation?
  - A: Hyperkalaemia, lactic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis.
