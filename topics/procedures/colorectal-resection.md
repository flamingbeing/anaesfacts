# Colorectal Resection

ERAS surgery: treat anaemia, protect the anastomosis with goal-directed fluids, spare opioids to avoid ileus.

## Key issues
- Patient blood management and IV iron
- ERAS
  - Preoperative: fasting, anaemia, nutrition, education, prehabilitation
  - Intraoperative: surgical technique, short-acting anaesthetics, fluids, analgesia, active warming
  - Postoperative: early feeding, early mobilisation, early removal of catheters
  - Highest yield: treating anaemia, carbohydrate loading, goal-directed fluids to protect the anastomosis, opioid-sparing analgesia (regional, adjuncts) to avoid ileus

## Bowel preparation
- Types
  - Osmotic cathartic: more complications but better tolerated
  - Non-absorbed osmotic: large volumes (litres of fluid)
  - Stimulant laxative
  - Combinations
- Complications
  - Electrolyte disturbance, especially high potassium
  - Osmotic cathartics: high sodium, magnesium and phosphate; low calcium
  - Water intoxication or dehydration

## Laparoscopic resection
- Time: 90-180min (duration 2-4hr); open AR is faster
- Pain: severe
- Blood loss: under 500ml (more if converted to laparotomy)
- Position: steep head-down lithotomy (Lloyd-Davies)
- Technique: GA, ETT, IPPV; routine oral intubation
- Lines and monitoring
  - Large-bore IV
  - NG tube and urinary catheter; surgeons may keep the NG tube postoperatively; the urinary catheter can come out postoperatively
  - Temperature monitoring and warming
  - Consider arterial line and BIS; with or without FloTrac
- Drugs
  - Keep paralysed
  - Antibiotics: Rocephin (ceftriaxone) and Flagyl (metronidazole)
  - Emergency drugs ready
- Operation may be prolonged and may eventually need conversion to GA

## Preoperative assessment
- Patients are often elderly with significant comorbidity; risk scores exist
- Indication sets the urgency
  - Elective non-cancer surgery: time for full work-up and optimisation
  - Cancer surgery: limited time to optimise

## Intraoperative management
- Gentle mask ventilation after induction to avoid gastric inflation
- Ask whether an NG or OG tube is wanted: easier to place before surgery starts
- Arterial line useful; secure the transducer so positioning does not dislodge it
- ETT and full relaxation throughout; always monitor with a nerve stimulator
- Remifentanil infusion blunts the stimulation of pneumoperitoneum
- Multimodal analgesia: rectus sheath catheters, TAP block or wound catheters, with PCA
- Monitor temperature and warm actively
- Tighten all table fixings; pad the shoulders
- Antibiotic prophylaxis
- High-risk patients: central line or minimally invasive cardiac output monitoring
- Do not let the surgeon stay in steep head-down for long if there is no progress

## Postoperative management (laparoscopic)
- Conjunctival oedema is common; restlessness reported after long head-down
- PCA morphine or fentanyl, plus other analgesics as needed

## Open resection
- Technique: GA
  - Regional alone (e.g. combined spinal-epidural) is possible for low anterior resection
- Surgeon may allow arms out
- Higher analgesic needs intraoperatively and postoperatively
  - Paracetamol; prescribe for 1 day postoperatively
  - With or without parecoxib (discuss with surgeon)
  - Avoid diclofenac: linked to anastomotic leak
  - Lidocaine infusion: controversial
  - Regional: thoracic epidural, TAP, rectus sheath catheter
    - Thoracic epidural: ropivacaine 0.12% with fentanyl 2mcg/ml; programmed intermittent bolus 4-6ml every 45-60min; PCEA 3-5ml, lockout 15-20min, max 12-20ml/hr; refer to acute pain service
  - PCA fentanyl 20mcg/ml: 1ml bolus, lockout 5min, 6-10ml demand
    - Especially important for open AR
    - Prescribe ondansetron and naloxone with it; refer to acute pain service
  - Surgeons may place a PainBuster continuous wound infusion (ropivacaine 2mg/ml)

## ERAS for major colorectal surgery
- Bundle of care; multidisciplinary planning of surgery, anaesthesia and recovery
- Clear fluids until 2.5hrs before surgery, with or without carbohydrate loading
- No sedative premedication
- PONV prophylaxis: dual antiemetics; consider TIVA if high risk
- Preoperative
  - Detailed patient education, information and risk assessment
  - Prehabilitation, including nutrition
  - Iron for anaemia
  - Avoid prolonged fasting and dehydration: carbohydrate loading
    - Clear fluids up to 2hrs before surgery
    - Carbohydrate drink 2hr before surgery reduces postoperative problems (nausea and vomiting, pain, diarrhoea, dizziness, wound problems) by 44%
  - No bowel preparation (it causes dehydration and makes fluid balance difficult)
- Intraoperative
  - No routine NG tubes or drains
  - Antibiotics within 30min before incision
  - Minimal surgical incisions
  - Avoid hypothermia
  - Zero-balance or goal-directed fluid therapy
    - Avoiding overload is vital: each extra litre increases complications by 32% and adds 24hrs to length of stay
    - Restrict to 2-3ml/kg/hr; avoid normal saline
    - Consider FloTrac to guide fluids
- Postoperative
  - Multimodal analgesia: regional (single-shot spinal versus epidural), IV opioid PCA, adjuncts (ketamine, lidocaine, gabapentinoids, clonidine)
  - Treat nausea and vomiting aggressively
  - Remove urinary catheter early
  - Early enteral feeding: less muscle loss, shorter stay, possibly fewer infections
  - Early mobilisation

## Acute pain service follow-up
- All patients with IV PCA or regional analgesia
- 1. Review pain and analgesic use
  - Aim to stop IV PCA at about 24-48h
  - Epidural for 48-72h
  - Multimodal analgesia where possible
- 2. Check surgical plan
  - Once on oral intake (clear feeds through to full diet), change IV paracetamol to oral
  - Add oral etoricoxib (Arcoxia) 1-1.5mg/kg daily if surgeons agree; maximum 5 days
- Goals: avoid side effects, early mobilisation and bowel function, intact anastomosis

## Flashcards
- Q: Effect of each extra litre of fluid in colorectal surgery?
  - A: Complications rise by 32% and length of stay by 24 hours
- Q: Fluid restriction target in colorectal ERAS?
  - A: 2-3ml/kg/hr, avoiding normal saline
- Q: Which NSAID to avoid after colorectal resection and why?
  - A: Diclofenac: linked to anastomotic leak
- Q: Thoracic epidural regimen for open colorectal surgery?
  - A: Ropivacaine 0.12% with fentanyl 2mcg/ml; bolus 4-6ml every 45-60 min; PCEA 3-5ml, lockout 15-20 min, max 12-20ml/hr
- Q: How long are IV PCA and epidurals usually kept?
  - A: PCA about 24-48h; epidural 48-72h
