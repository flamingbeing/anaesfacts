# Paediatric Ophthalmic Surgery

Children need GA for eye surgery; key issues are repeated anaesthetics, the oculocardiac reflex, intraocular pressure (IOP) and high PONV rates.

## General points
- Most patients are healthy; a few have conditions that change the technique
- Many eye conditions need repeated anaesthetics
- Unlike adults, children need GA rather than local anaesthesia
- Airway
  - Limited access during surgery, so intubation is common, usually a south-facing RAE ETT
  - Reinforced LMA also used, especially in older children, to avoid paralysis and coughing on the tube at the end
- Oculocardiac reflex is particularly strong in children
- Consider effects on IOP
- PONV is very common
- Most cases are suitable for day surgery

## Examination under anaesthesia and IOP measurement
- Children under 5 years rarely tolerate a proper eye examination, especially IOP measurement
- Anaesthetic problems usually stem from associated conditions affecting the airway or other systems
  - Metabolic: diabetes, mucopolysaccharidoses, hypercalcaemia, galactosaemia
  - Infection: herpes simplex, CMV, rubella
  - Syndromes: Apert, Marfan, Sturge-Weber, Lowe, Crouzon
  - Others: congenital myotonic dystrophy, sickle cell disease
- General examination when IOP does not matter
  - IV or inhalational induction
  - Flexible LMA gives the surgeon free access
  - Volatile in oxygen with air or nitrous oxide
- When IOP is measured: give good conditions without changing IOP
  - Commonest case: congenital glaucoma, with repeated EUAs over years for diagnosis and monitoring
  - Use the same GA technique each time so serial readings are comparable
  - Most anaesthetic drugs lower IOP, so readings after induction with anything other than ketamine may be falsely low
  - IOP often measured after ketamine 1-2 mg/kg IV or 5-10 mg/kg IM, before airway stimulation or other drugs
    - Atropine 20 mcg/kg or glycopyrronium 10 mcg/kg given with it to reduce ketamine secretions
    - Many avoid ketamine because IM injection is disliked and emergence reactions are a concern
  - With volatile or IV agents: use the least needed for a still child and measure IOP as early as possible
  - LMA has little effect on IOP; face mask pressure may raise it
- Recovery
  - After ketamine, emergence reactions (hallucinations, bad dreams) can occur; recover in a dim, quiet area without disturbing the child
  - Oral intake resumes quickly; analgesia seldom needed

## Strabismus surgery
- Misaligned visual axis affects 3-5% of people; the commonest paediatric eye operation
- Male to female ratio 1:1
- Causes: usually idiopathic; sometimes trauma, infection or inflammation (muscle palsies)
- Associations: family history, prematurity, CNS disorders (cerebral palsy, meningomyelocele with hydrocephalus)
- Occult myopathy possible
  - Masseter spasm is three times more common in these children
  - Possibly higher, though still very low, malignant hyperthermia risk
  - Suxamethonium usually avoided
- Surgery: recession (lengthening), resection (shortening) or transposition of one or more extraocular muscles (four recti, two obliques)
- Older children: occasional adjustable suture, set under local anaesthetic drops at 24-48 hours to reduce malposition
- Check how many muscles are being operated on: 2 muscles take about 1.5 hours

## Strabismus surgery: anaesthetic technique
- Usually healthy; premedication as needed
- Induction as preferred or requested
- ETT or LMA, spontaneous or controlled ventilation as indicated
- Volatile in oxygen and air; TIVA may lower PONV in older children
- Topical adrenaline used by the surgeon is absorbed systemically: watch the ECG for a while
- Forced duction test: surgeon pulls the eye into normal position to tell a paretic muscle from a restricted one
  - Invalid within 20 min of suxamethonium (it contracts extraocular muscles)
  - Non-depolarising relaxants do not interfere
- Oculocardiac reflex: traction on extraocular muscles causes vagal bradycardia; prevent with atropine 20 mcg/kg or glycopyrronium 10 mcg/kg
- Analgesia: topical tetracaine, NSAID, paracetamol
  - Fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg IV adds analgesia and sedation but increases PONV
- PONV is common (50-75%)
  - Use two antiemetics with different actions, e.g. ondansetron 100 mcg/kg (max 4 mg) and dexamethasone 100-200 mcg/kg
  - Combinations can cut PONV to about 5%

## Strabismus surgery: postoperative care
- The eye patch and poor vision can upset children
- Early oral intake
- Paracetamol, NSAID and an oral opioid
- Rescue antiemetic of a different class, e.g. cyclizine 1 mg/kg IV if ondansetron and dexamethasone already given
- Leave the IV cannula in for IV antiemetics
- Mostly day cases

## Cataract surgery
- Major cause of childhood blindness worldwide: 1 child goes blind every minute, mostly in developing countries; of 1.5 million blind children, 85% live in Africa or Asia
- Types: congenital (at birth), infantile (first 2 years), juvenile (later); many infantile and juvenile cataracts were present from birth
- Causes
  - Hereditary (75%): usually autosomal dominant in otherwise well children; sometimes syndromic, e.g. Lowe oculocerebrorenal syndrome (X-linked recessive), Down (trisomy 21), Edwards, cri du chat
  - Metabolic: G6PD deficiency, galactosaemia, hypoglycaemia, infant hypocalcaemia
  - Trauma: blunt or penetrating, usually one eye
  - Inflammation: uveitis (juvenile chronic arthritis)
  - Tumour: retinoblastoma
  - Intrauterine infection: rubella, CMV, toxoplasmosis, toxocariasis
  - Radiation: total body irradiation for leukaemia
  - Long-term steroids
- Surgery with lens implant done early (as young as 4 weeks in some centres) to allow visual development; takes 30-60 min
- More complications than in adults: glaucoma (lifelong follow-up), uveitis, iris damage or prolapse, endophthalmitis, posterior capsule thickening needing more surgery (up to 90%), retinal detachment
- Preoperative: consider the underlying condition (e.g. mucopolysaccharidosis airway, prematurity, Down syndrome); surgeon gives mydriatics, usually phenylephrine 2.5% and cyclopentolate 1%

## Cataract surgery: anaesthetic technique
- Aims: still eye, no rise in IOP, smooth induction and emergence without coughing
- IV or inhalational induction
- Usually intubation (south-facing RAE ETT) and IPPV to avoid hypercapnia
- Volatile in oxygen and air; nitrous oxide often avoided to reduce PONV
- Remifentanil 0.1-0.25 mcg/kg/minute lowers volatile needs and smooths emergence; TIVA often used in older children
- Avoid high FiO2 in premature neonates
- Two antiemetics to limit vomiting-related IOP rises, e.g. ondansetron 100 mcg/kg and dexamethasone 100-200 mcg/kg
- Analgesia: tetracaine drops, NSAID, paracetamol; opioid for analgesia and mild sedation, e.g. fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg IV
- Postoperative: early oral intake; paracetamol, NSAID and oral opioid, e.g. oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly; rescue cyclizine 1 mg/kg IV; keep the cannula; mostly day cases
- Manage any syndrome needs, e.g. respiratory support in Pompe disease

## Glaucoma
- Raised IOP reduces optic nerve capillary flow, leading to loss of optic nerve tissue and function
- Bilateral in 75%; congenital glaucoma 1:10000 births; female to male ratio 65%:35%
- Primary congenital: trabecular drainage channels fail to develop
- Secondary: existing channels blocked by
  - Infection
  - Inflammation
  - Eye trauma, e.g. uveitis, iritis, or after congenital cataract surgery
  - Rare syndromes: Axenfeld-Rieger (abnormal iris and corneal margin); aniridia (linked with genitourinary anomalies and Wilms tumour (20%) and intellectual disability); Sturge-Weber (intraocular angiomas raise IOP and block the angle)
- Primary glaucoma predominates under 3 years; secondary after that
- Medical treatment
  - Acetazolamide 15-30 mg/kg/day oral in 3 or 4 divided doses: carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, reduces aqueous production; occasional metabolic acidosis
  - Topical beta-blockers sometimes added
- Surgery (after EUA and IOP measurement)
  - Trabeculectomy: new channel from anterior chamber to sub-Tenon space under conjunctiva; may add mitomycin C or 5-fluorouracil to limit scarring
  - Aqueous shunt (drainage tube)
  - Trabeculotomy: probe in Schlemm's canal opens a new channel
  - Goniotomy: incision in the trabecular meshwork under gonioscopic view; sometimes surface cornea removed for a better view
  - Laser to the ciliary body to cut aqueous production

## Glaucoma: anaesthetic technique
- Many anaesthetics over time: children often anxious and need sedative premedication; assess any underlying cause
- Avoid raised IOP; smooth induction and emergence without coughing
- Most agents lower IOP, as does a calm or premedicated child; ketamine probably raises it slightly; suxamethonium clearly raises it and is avoided
- Measure IOP after induction, before laryngoscopy
- Intubation (south-facing RAE ETT) and IPPV to avoid hypercapnia
- Volatile in oxygen and air; avoid nitrous oxide (PONV); remifentanil 0.1-0.25 mcg/kg/min; TIVA in older children
- Two antiemetics, e.g. ondansetron 100 mcg/kg (max 4 mg) and dexamethasone 100-200 mcg/kg
- Analgesia: tetracaine drops, NSAID, paracetamol; fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg IV
- Extubate without coughing or straining
- Postoperative: early oral intake; paracetamol, NSAID and oral opioid (e.g. oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly); more painful than most paediatric eye surgery

## Retinoblastoma
- Treatments: IV, periocular, intravitreal or intra-arterial chemotherapy; radiotherapy; focal therapy; enucleation
- Patient factors
  - Many frequent anaesthetics: build rapport; review old charts for past reactions; premedication may help older children
  - Previous chemotherapy: peripheral IV chemotherapy may make IV access hard; systemic chemotherapy can cause neutropenia
- EUA of eyes
- Focal therapy (cryotherapy, transpupillary thermotherapy, laser)
  - For smaller or shrunken tumours
  - LMA acceptable
  - Laser safety and immobility during laser

## Intra-arterial (ophthalmic artery) chemotherapy
- Done in interventional radiology: remote site considerations
- Child must be completely still: GA with ETT and paralysis
- Sudden severe fall in lung compliance
  - Abrupt, profound, when the microcatheter enters the ICA or ophthalmic artery
  - Can happen before any saline, contrast or drug is injected
  - Happens despite deep anaesthesia and paralysis
  - Not reliably prevented by steroids or salbutamol
  - Always linked in time to first placing the catheter
  - Tidal volume can fall within seconds; wheeze usually absent
  - Management
    - Tell the neuroradiologist; pause catheter work until resolved
    - Adrenaline is very effective, especially early: boluses of 0.5 to 1 mcg/kg work better than smaller doses; response should be rapid
    - Usually does not recur in the same procedure, even with the catheter in the other ophthalmic artery
    - Usually no respiratory problems in recovery
  - Cause unknown; possibly an unusual vagally mediated severe bronchospasm (trigeminocardiac reflex)
- Haemodynamic instability
  - Bradycardia and hypotension
  - Often follows the respiratory change; may be masked if IV adrenaline already given
  - Adrenaline is first-line for both respiratory and haemodynamic instability
- PONV very common afterwards

## Penetrating eye injury
- In developed countries commonest in boys aged 3-9 years
- Usually sharp tools (knives, scissors) poked into the eye by the child, or thrown objects; mostly at home
- Needs wound closure with or without foreign body removal
- Preoperative
  - Urgent, but usually delayed until fasted
  - Avoid crying and struggling, which raise IOP (e.g. cannulation without topical anaesthetic); give analgesia
  - Fear of suxamethonium causing vitreous extrusion is probably overstated; most will use it if indicated; IV induction agents (e.g. thiopental) lower IOP, so net effect is small
- Anaesthetic technique
  - Avoid crying or struggling; if fasted, an elective induction (premedication, smooth gas or IV induction after topical skin anaesthesia)
  - Non-depolarising relaxant for intubation
  - Fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg IV or lidocaine 1.5 mg/kg IV to blunt the IOP rise from laryngoscopy
  - Regurgitation risk: rapid sequence induction with preoxygenation, suxamethonium and cricoid pressure
  - Volatile in oxygen and air; avoid nitrous oxide (expands intraocular gas, adds to emesis)
  - May be long: maintenance fluids, temperature monitoring, warming mattress
  - Surgeon's local anaesthetic drops give good analgesia
  - Prophylactic ondansetron 100 mcg/kg IV (high PONV risk)
  - Smooth emergence and extubation to avoid IOP rises
- Postoperative
  - IV fluids until drinking without PONV; cyclizine 1 mg/kg IV as rescue
  - Paracetamol 15 mg/kg oral 4-hourly, ibuprofen 10 mg/kg oral 6-hourly, oral morphine 200-300 mcg/kg 4-hourly; topical NSAID drops
  - Pain usually not a big problem
  - Overnight stay common after long or out-of-hours surgery, or if more eye drops are needed

## Retinal detachment
- Unusual in children (1-2% of all cases)
- Primary (idiopathic)
  - Retinal defect lets vitreous fluid into the subretinal space; usually peripheral
  - Risk factors: high myopia (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos, Stickler) and trauma; may be familial or have no cause
  - Visual symptoms may go unreported in young children
  - Treated surgically
- Secondary
  - Subretinal transudate from vitreous, retinal or choroidal vessels: hypertension, chronic renal failure, uveitis, tumours including retinoblastoma
  - Or traction by fibrous bands after trauma with vitreous haemorrhage, or retinopathy of prematurity
  - Treat the underlying cause
- Preoperative: obtain records for any syndrome; occasionally further tests (e.g. echocardiography); surgery for primary detachment waits until fasted
- Anaesthetic technique
  - Full stomach concern (especially after trauma): rapid sequence induction
  - ETT, usually south-facing preformed RAE; tape very securely as the anaesthetist is far from the airway under the drapes
  - Surgery about 2-4 hours; ventilate for normocapnia and good conditions
  - Monitor temperature; warming mattress
  - Minimal blood loss; crystalloid for deficit and maintenance
  - Retinal manipulation is briefly very stimulating: short-acting opioid (fentanyl, alfentanil) or remifentanil 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min, allowing low volatile concentrations
  - Prophylactic ondansetron 100 mcg/kg IV or cyclizine 1 mg/kg IV

## Flashcards
- Q: Ketamine dose for IOP measurement at EUA?
  - A: 1-2 mg/kg IV or 5-10 mg/kg IM, with atropine 20 mcg/kg or glycopyrronium 10 mcg/kg.
- Q: How long after suxamethonium is forced duction testing unreliable?
  - A: 20 min.
- Q: PONV rate after strabismus surgery, and with combination antiemetics?
  - A: 50-75%; about 5% with combinations.
- Q: Treatment of sudden loss of compliance during intra-arterial retinoblastoma chemotherapy?
  - A: Pause catheter work; adrenaline boluses 0.5 to 1 mcg/kg.
- Q: Acetazolamide dose for paediatric glaucoma?
  - A: 15-30 mg/kg/day oral in 3 or 4 divided doses.
