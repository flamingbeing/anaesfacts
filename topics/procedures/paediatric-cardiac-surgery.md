# Paediatric Cardiac Surgery and Congenital Heart Disease

Physiology of the main congenital heart lesions and anaesthesia for children with CHD, for cardiac and non-cardiac surgery.

## Key points
- Acyanotic vs cyanotic
- Shunts: think through shunt dynamics (avoid raising pulmonary pressures) and take bubble precautions
- Review the echo (TTE) and involve paediatric cardiology
- Antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures where indicated (see infective endocarditis)

## Epidemiology
- Incidence 5-10:1000 live births
  - Figures usually exclude mitral valve prolapse, bicuspid aortic valve and PDA in premature infants
- More than 80% survive the first year; about 80% of these reach adulthood; complex disease increasingly common among survivors
- Over half of CHD surgery is in children under one year; trend to early complete repair rather than staged repair
- Isolated, or with chromosomal abnormalities and syndromes

## Classification
- Anatomical: segmental, sequential look at each atrium, ventricle, great artery and vein and how they connect; still evolving
- Physiological (most useful for anaesthesia): grouped by shunting, oxygenation, pulmonary blood flow, and volume or pressure loading
  - Groups overlap and physiology may change over time
- For each patient, trace the blood flow path, the shunts, chamber and vessel pressures, and how changing SVR and PVR will act; then predict effects of drugs, ventilation and fluids

## Shunt-based groups
- Left to right shunt: increased pulmonary blood flow, volume overload
  - PDA, ASD, VSD, AVSD
- Right to left shunt: deoxygenated blood enters the systemic circulation, causing cyanosis
  - Tetralogy of Fallot, pulmonary atresia, tricuspid atresia
  - Ebstein's anomaly (tricuspid leaflets displaced towards the RV apex)
- Mixed or complex: cyanosis with variable volume and pressure overload
  - TGA, total anomalous pulmonary venous drainage (TAPVD), hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS), double outlet right ventricle (DORV), truncus arteriosus
- Obstructive: pressure overload
  - Coarctation, interrupted or hypoplastic aortic arch, valve stenosis (AS, MS, TS)
- Regurgitant lesions

## Cyanotic vs acyanotic
- Acyanotic: PDA, ASD, VSD, AVSD; PS, AS, coarctation
- Cyanotic, the "5 Ts": Tetralogy of Fallot, Transposition, Tricuspid valve anomalies, Total anomalous pulmonary venous return, Truncus arteriosus
- Better surgery and palliation mean more survivors with cyanotic and single ventricle disease who carry limitations, e.g. after:
  - Blalock-Taussig (BT) shunt
  - Norwood procedure
  - Fontan with a single ventricle

## Transitional circulation
- First few days to weeks of life
- Pulmonary vasculature very reactive; the duct may reopen
- Very sensitive to hypoxia, hypercarbia, acidosis and prostaglandins
- Shunting or reversion to fetal circulation can occur

## Balanced (parallel) circulation
- Systemic and pulmonary circulations connected and working in parallel
  - Normal heart: flow in series, Qp = Qs (Qp:Qs=1)
  - Non-restrictive shunt: Qp:Qs depends on the balance of SVR and PVR
- Connections: natural (VSD, PDA, ASD) or created (balloon atrial septostomy in uncorrected TGA, modified BT shunt, PDA stent)
- Balancing matters:
  - Too much pulmonary flow: poor systemic perfusion, hypotension, possible pulmonary oedema (with or without desaturation)
  - Too little pulmonary flow: desaturation and acidosis

## Duct-dependent lesions
- Need a patent ductus to supply either the pulmonary or systemic circulation until surgery
- Duct-dependent systemic circulation: presents with collapse or cardiac failure
  - TGA, HLHS, coarctation, critical aortic stenosis
- Duct-dependent pulmonary circulation: worsening cyanosis that does not respond to more oxygen
  - Tricuspid atresia, critical pulmonary stenosis, tetralogy of Fallot
- Keeping the duct open:
  - Avoid high FiO2; allow moderate hypercarbia
  - PGE1 infusion: alprostadil 5-50 nanograms/kg/min
    - Can cause apnoea: monitor breathing, IPPV sometimes needed
    - Potent vasodilator: may need volume or inotropes once started
  - Support cardiac output with fluids and inotropes as needed

## Anaesthetic considerations by physiology
- 1. Cyanosis
  - Complications: chronic hypoxia; compensatory polycythaemia with thromboembolism and coagulopathy; metabolic acidosis
  - Severity shown by room air saturation and degree of polycythaemia
  - Keep hydrated: IV fluids while nil by mouth
  - Some need preoperative oxygen, correction of acidosis or inotropes
- 2. Reduced pulmonary blood flow
  - Lower PVR with oxygen and hyperventilation; avoid falls in SVR
  - Selective pulmonary vasodilators (e.g. nitric oxide) may help, especially with RV hypertrophy
- 3. Increased pulmonary blood flow, cardiac failure, duct-dependent systemic circulation, high pressure or volume shunts
  - Moderate hypercarbia, room air or avoid high FiO2, inotropes in some
  - Avoid marked falls in SVR

## Atrial septal defect (ASD)
- Left to right atrial shunt: RA and RV volume overload, increased pulmonary blood flow
  - No LA dilatation, as excess LA blood passes straight to the RA (unlike VSD)
  - Usually acyanotic unless severe pulmonary hypertension (Eisenmenger)
- Types: ostium secundum, ostium primum
- Signs: wide fixed split S2; pulmonary flow murmur (relative pulmonary stenosis)
- ECG depends on size and type
  - Prolonged PR (first degree block): atrial enlargement and the extra conduction distance
  - Incomplete (sometimes complete) RBBB, reflecting RV overload rather than true conduction delay
  - Notched R waves in inferior leads (crochetage sign)
  - Pulmonary hypertension signs: RVH with or without strain, RA enlargement (P pulmonale)
  - Right axis deviation; left axis deviation in primum defects (involves the His bundle and branches)
  - May be normal with a small uncomplicated shunt
- CXR: enlarged RA, RV and PA; increased pulmonary vascular markings
- Management
  - Transcatheter closure (secundum) or closure on CPB, by size, site and associated defects
  - Usually at 3-5 years of age, earlier if large with cardiac failure
  - Stable patients: conventional anaesthesia for non-cardiac surgery
- Adults needing ASD surgery: atrial flutter or AF in about 20%

## Ventricular septal defect (VSD)
- Left to right shunt from LV to RV in systole: increased pulmonary flow, LA and LV volume overload
  - Shunt happens during RV systole, so blood goes straight into the PA; RV not volume loaded and stays near normal size
- 30-40% close spontaneously, by site and size; muscular defects most likely to close
- CXR: enlarged main PA, LA and LV; increased pulmonary vascular markings
- Restrictive (smaller than the aortic root): usually asymptomatic; standard anaesthesia
- Non-restrictive: cardiac failure and early pulmonary vascular disease more likely; repair may be needed at 3-6 months of age

## Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)
- Left to right shunt through the duct, causing:
  - LV volume overload (heart failure)
  - Increased pulmonary flow (pulmonary oedema, respiratory distress)
  - Reduced systemic flow and oxygen delivery; low diastolic BP risks myocardial ischaemia
- Investigations
  - CXR as for VSD
  - TTE: duct size, peak gradient, shunt direction and size, LV and LA dilatation, pulmonary hypertension
- Risk factors
  - Prematurity (50% in very low birth weight infants)
  - Respiratory distress syndrome
  - Term infants: anatomical closure at 6hrs, complete occlusion usually by 6 weeks
- Symptomatic treatment
  - Fluid restriction, diuretics
  - Falling PVR worsens the shunt: limit FiO2 and allow hypercapnia
- Medical treatment
  - NSAIDs: classically indomethacin, now ibuprofen (lower risk)
    - Complications: GI perforation, NEC, renal impairment, bleeding
  - Paracetamol (not FDA approved): hepatotoxicity
- Surgical ligation; transcatheter closure may suit older patients
- Factors promoting duct closure: oxygen and hyperoxia, hypocarbia; prostaglandins (e.g. PGE infusion); bradykinin and acetylcholine
- PDA ligation
  - Preductal arterial line in the right radial artery
  - Postductal SpO2, usually a lower limb
  - Left thoracotomy
  - May desaturate during lung retraction: higher FiO2 and hand ventilation
  - Nearly 50% of preterm infants may develop post-ligation cardiac syndrome (PLCS)

## Atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD)
- Ranges from primum ASD with cleft mitral valve (little disturbance) to complete AVSD
- Complete AVSD: volume overload of both atria and ventricles, common AV valve regurgitation, early pulmonary vascular disease, possible cardiac failure; repair at 3-6 months of age
- Trisomy 21 in 30% of complete AVSDs; other congenital and cardiac anomalies also linked

## Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)
- Four features: infundibular stenosis, overriding aorta, VSD, RV hypertrophy
- Cyanosis depends on the degree of RV outflow obstruction
- Surgery: primary complete repair in infancy, or (now less often) two stages
  - Neonatal systemic to pulmonary shunt (usually modified BT shunt: tube graft from subclavian to pulmonary artery), then full repair around 1 year of age
- Tet spells: hypercyanosis with fast deep breathing; peak at 2-4 months of age
  - Cause unclear; linked with higher oxygen demand, acidosis and hypercapnia
  - Treatment: abdominal compression, leg flexion, oxygen, morphine (or other opioid), IV fluid, beta-blockers (esmolol, propranolol; relieve infundibular spasm), vasoconstrictors (noradrenaline, phenylephrine)
- Avoid long fasts: hyperviscosity worsens; dehydration risks clotting a surgical shunt
- Expect coagulation defects with significant polycythaemia
- Sevoflurane is a useful induction agent despite reduced pulmonary blood flow
- After repair: conduction defects and pulmonary regurgitation possible

## Transposition of the great arteries (TGA)
- PA arises from the LV and aorta from the RV; about 25% also have a significant VSD
- Life depends on mixing between the circulations; in the fetus the duct and foramen ovale provide this, so the neonate deteriorates as they close
- Initial treatment: IV alprostadil infusion, with or without atrial septostomy
- Arterial switch within the first 14 days of life: great arteries divided and reattached to the correct ventricles; coronaries reimplanted above the neo-aortic valve
  - May be delayed if a VSD is present
  - Uses deep hypothermia with DHCA or low-flow CPB
  - LV needs afterload reduction after CPB; milrinone useful
  - Consider aprotinin (complex neonatal surgery)
- Atrial switch (physiological, not anatomical repair) is now rare; older patients who had it need careful haemodynamic management

## Coarctation of the aorta
- LV pressure overload; weak femoral pulses
- Diagnosed at any age
- Neonates may present with cardiac failure or collapse
  - Resuscitate and start a PGE1 infusion (5-50 nanograms/kg/min) to reopen the duct
  - Ventilation and inotropes may be needed
- Repair via left thoracotomy with excision; residual gradients may need later balloon dilatation

## Single ventricle physiology
- Only one functioning ventricle; the other is rudimentary or hypoplastic
- Complete mixing of systemic and pulmonary venous blood; one ventricle pumps to both circulations
- HLHS is the commonest (25% of single ventricle lesions)
- Others: double inlet ventricle, pulmonary atresia with intact septum, tricuspid atresia, unbalanced AV canal defect

## Unpalliated single ventricle
- Needs two shunts:
  - Mixing, usually intracardiac
    - With complete mixing, PaO2 in the proximal PA and ascending aorta is about the same
    - Poor mixing causes pulmonary venous hypertension and poor systemic oxygenation; urgent balloon atrial septostomy creates a common atrium
  - Systemic to pulmonary connection (usually the duct) to supply both circulations
    - Supplies the lungs if pulmonary flow is obstructed (pulmonary or tricuspid atresia), or the body if systemic outflow is obstructed (aortic atresia, HLHS)

## Staged single ventricle palliation
- Stage 1 (infancy): Norwood, BT shunt or PA band
  - Goals: full intracardiac mixing, controlled pulmonary flow, unobstructed systemic flow
- Stage 2: bidirectional cavopulmonary connection (Glenn or hemi-Fontan: SVC to PA), in the 1st or 2nd year of life
  - PVR keeps falling over the first months, raising pulmonary flow and risking overcirculation
  - By 3–6 months PVR is low enough for passive SVC flow alone to perfuse the lungs
  - SVC joined end-to-side to the right PA; BT or RV-PA shunt taken down; azygos sometimes tied to stop SVC decompressing to the IVC
  - Benefits: lungs perfused at lower PA pressure as the child grows; systemic ventricle volume-unloaded and remodels
- Stage 3: Fontan (both IVC and SVC to the PA)
  - Pulmonary flow is passive, driven by the PA to left atrial gradient
  - Higher PVR and positive intrathoracic pressure reduce pulmonary flow
  - IPPV may still be needed to avoid hypercapnia and hypoxia; use minimal peak pressures and short inspiratory times

## Preoperative assessment
- CHD is diagnosed on prenatal checks, infant presentation with cardiovascular compromise, or routine postnatal checks (including preop) or after another congenital diagnosis
- Define the lesion: echo, with or without cardiac catheterisation; cardiac MRI increasingly used
- Cardiac function and failure
  - Review all cardiac tests to understand anatomy and physiology
  - Limited reserve: poor exercise tolerance, poor growth, recurrent chest infections
  - Infant heart failure: poor feeding, failure to thrive, tachypnoea at rest, pallor, hepatomegaly
  - Drugs: diuretics may cause borderline preload and electrolyte problems
  - ECG for conduction defects and arrhythmias (from lesion or prior surgery)
  - Can function be improved before surgery in the time available?
- Hypoxia and cyanosis
  - Severity; episodic hypercyanotic spells
  - Polycythaemia causes hyperviscosity and abnormal coagulation
  - Persistent metabolic acidosis in severe hypoxia
  - Avoid prolonged fasting if polycythaemic (2 hours for clear fluids); IV fluids may be needed
  - Plan for intraoperative coagulation factor replacement
- Respiratory
  - Airway problems not uncommon: difficult intubation, short trachea, airway compression by vessels, tracheobronchomalacia
  - Increased or reduced pulmonary flow; pulmonary hypertension
  - Active chest infection increases complications and ICU stay; postpone non-urgent surgery (can be hard to separate from CHD signs)
  - Review CXR
- Associated anomalies (non-cardiac in about 25% of infants with CHD); check other organs
  - Trisomy 21: AVSD, TOF, VSD
  - DiGeorge: aortic arch anomalies, truncus arteriosus
  - VATER: VSD
  - CHARGE: TOF, septal defects
  - Turner: coarctation, septal defects
  - Noonan: pulmonary stenosis, septal defects

## Non-cardiac surgery in CHD
- Seen on any list, especially dental lists
- Technique and setting depend on lesion severity and surgery; cardiac techniques can be adapted
- Endocarditis prophylaxis for at-risk patients having dental procedures
- Good analgesia (including regional and peripheral blocks), weighing cardiovascular and respiratory effects

## Types of cardiac surgery
- Corrective (PDA ligation, ASD or VSD closure) or palliative (PA banding, BT shunt, cavopulmonary shunts)
- Open (on CPB) or closed (no CPB):
  - PDA ligation (thoracotomy)
  - Coarctation repair (thoracotomy)
  - PA banding (thoracotomy or sternotomy)
  - BT shunt (thoracotomy or sternotomy)
- Understand the lesion and the planned operation, then predict how anaesthesia will affect physiology
- Many CHD patients for non-cardiac surgery can be managed normally; complex lesions need a paediatric cardiac centre

## Premedication
- Usual indications and contraindications
- Common before major surgery including bypass; avoided in young infants and neonates
- Reducing anxiety helps cardiovascular stability
- Midazolam 0.5 mg/kg (maximum 20 mg) oral 30 min before induction
- Alimemazine (trimeprazine) 2 mg/kg (maximum 60 mg) oral 1 hour before induction

## Monitoring
- Full routine monitoring plus arterial and central lines as needed
- Unlike adults, invasive lines usually go in after induction and intubation
- Line sites depend on anatomy and past or planned surgery
  - Right radial arterial line for coarctation repair; left radial for a right BT shunt
  - Usually 22G arterial cannula (24G in small neonates)
- Central lines usually right internal jugular, ultrasound guided; size by patient
- For CPB:
  - Core and peripheral temperature
  - Urinary catheter
  - Redo surgery: defibrillation pads on; surgeon may want femoral access for partial bypass if the RV or great vessels are injured at sternotomy
  - Surgeon may place lines directly into the atria or PA (alternative to percutaneous central lines)
  - Intraoperative echo (epicardial or TOE) increasingly standard
  - Neuromonitoring in some centres, especially with deep hypothermia and circulatory arrest: processed EEG, transcranial Doppler, NIRS

## Induction and maintenance
- Choose agents by effects on cardiac output, heart rate, SVR and PVR; titrate carefully; onset is slow with cardiac failure (long circulation time)
- Sevoflurane: cardiovascularly stable, well tolerated
- Ketamine: useful in neonates and with cardiac compromise
- Etomidate: alternative in older children
- Propofol: usable, with caution in cardiac impairment
- Opioid at induction, especially with cardiac failure
- Neuromuscular block: pancuronium (100 mcg/kg) for long action and vagolytic effect; vecuronium and rocuronium are stable and shorter acting
- Nasal intubation when postoperative IPPV planned (most CPB cases); suits up to 8-10 years of age; more stable fixation
- Maintenance: volatile (e.g. isoflurane) in oxygen and air, or propofol infusion (may be TCI); opioids as needed
- Nitrous oxide: optional; in CPB cases limit to induction (air embolism risk)
- CPB cases: moderate to high dose opioids (fentanyl, alfentanil, morphine, remifentanil); trend to moderate opioid plus volatile
- On CPB: anaesthesia usually IV; volatile can be added to the oxygenator gas (possible myocardial preconditioning and neuroprotection)
- Endocarditis prophylaxis: high and moderate risk lesions for bacteraemic procedures such as dental extraction or tonsillectomy
  - Highest risk: previous endocarditis, valve replacement, surgical systemic-pulmonary shunts or conduits
  - Follow local or national guidance
- Avoid air bubbles, especially with right to left shunts
- Resuscitation drugs at hand

## Regional anaesthesia
- Thoracic epidural catheter for thoracotomy, especially with planned early extubation
- Neuraxial block for CPB remains controversial (anticoagulation; rare but catastrophic epidural haematoma)

## Cardiac catheterisation
- Diagnostic catheters now less common; includes finding residual or missed defects after repair in possibly very sick patients
- Interventional work increasing: balloon dilatation of vessels and valves, stents, device closure (PDA, ASD), pathway ablation
- In the catheter lab: intubation and ventilation, low-dose opioid, limited volatile
- Can be long: keep warm, protect pressure points
- Be ready for arrhythmias, sudden loss of cardiac output and major bleeding

## Closed cardiac procedures
- No bypass; intubation and ventilation required
- Neonates and infants likely to need postoperative ventilation
- Arterial line usual; central line for major cases
- Thoracotomy or median sternotomy; lung retracted at thoracotomy

## Systemic to pulmonary artery shunt
- Improves pulmonary flow and oxygenation where pulmonary flow is restricted (TOF, pulmonary atresia, tricuspid atresia)
- Usually modified BT shunt: 3.5-5 mm graft from subclavian artery to the same-side PA
- Thoracotomy; one-lung collapse for access can worsen hypoxia
- Aim: enough reliable pulmonary flow for acceptable oxygenation without overloading the LV; postoperative SpO2 usually in the 80s
- Existing shunt: avoid dehydration; give antibiotic prophylaxis

## Pulmonary artery banding
- Restricts pulmonary flow to prevent heart failure and pulmonary hypertension
- Formerly for large left to right shunts awaiting repair (AVSD, large VSD); most now repaired early
- Current indications:
  - Multiple VSDs where early repair is not feasible
  - VSDs with coarctation or interrupted aortic arch
  - Preparing the LV for an arterial switch by raising afterload
- Median sternotomy or left thoracotomy
- Dacron or PTFE band tightened while watching PA pressure, until PA pressure is below 50% of systolic or the lowest PA pressure with acceptable SpO2
- Removed at definitive surgery
- Too loose: heart failure and pulmonary hypertension; too tight: right to left shunt and profound hypoxia

## Epicardial pacemaker insertion
- Pacing in infants and young children, mostly for AV block
- Median sternotomy; lead placement may take time
- Consider an arterial line; expect sudden major bleeding after previous cardiac surgery

## Cardiopulmonary bypass: before bypass
- Team approach: surgeon, anaesthetist, perfusionist, cardiology; communication is key
- Expect instability during cannulation, especially neonates and small infants; accurate cannula placement is vital for systemic perfusion and venous drainage
- Some hypotension and arrhythmia from handling must be tolerated up to a point
- Most stimulating: skin incision and sternotomy
- Baseline ABG and ACT; point of care electrolytes, haematocrit, glucose, lactate
- Heparin 300 units/kg when the surgeon asks (some inject into the heart); ACT over 400 secs before going on bypass
- Antifibrinolytics: aprotinin may be used in redo and complex neonatal cases; test dose after induction; then load, infusion and a pump prime dose
- Pre-bypass time varies with previous surgery and extra-cardiac shunts to ligate

## Cardiopulmonary bypass: on bypass
- Standard circuit: sized cannulae, tubing, venous reservoir, cardiotomy suction, roller pump, membrane oxygenator and gas supply, blood warmer
- Prime is smaller than adult but large relative to blood volume (up to 100% vs 25-30% in adults); blood added to reach haematocrit 24-28% on CPB
- Flow by body surface area; target pressure by age
- Adjust pressure with vasopressors (phenylephrine), vasodilators (phentolamine, GTN) and anaesthetic drugs
- Continuous mixed venous saturation confirms adequate flow; some electrolytes and haematocrit can be monitored continuously
- Stop ventilation at full flow
- Anaesthesia IV with opioids, plus benzodiazepines, propofol or volatile via the oxygenator as wished
- Deeper hypothermia than adults: commonly 25-32°C; 18-20°C with circulatory arrest
- Blood gases during hypothermia:
  - Samples measured at 37°C (uncorrected), then corrected to patient temperature by nomogram
  - Alpha stat (uncorrected values): now more common; keeps electrochemical neutrality and cerebral autoregulation in moderate hypothermia
  - pH stat (corrected values): keeps pH and pCO2 normal for 37°C by adding CO2; renewed interest for circulatory arrest
- Cross-clamp and cardiac arrest for access; myocardium protected by hypothermia and cardioplegia into the aortic root
  - Crystalloid or (now more often) blood cardioplegia; potassium is the key component
- Ultrafiltration during or after bypass reduces CPB and haemodilution effects

## Separation from bypass
- Before weaning: rewarmed, normal electrolytes, neutral acid-base, adequate ventilation; anticipate inotropes
- Mild hypothermia may protect the brain; avoid hyperthermia; core 36-36.5°C at separation
- Filling judged by looking at the heart and by CVP or atrial pressures
- Pacing if slow or AV dissociation; surgeon places atrial and ventricular epicardial wires in all but the simplest cases
- Epicardial echo after bypass checks the repair, residual lesions, filling and function; small TOE probes increasingly used; needs expertise
- Protamine 2-5 mg/kg slowly once off bypass and stable, only when the surgeon asks; stop cardiotomy suction at once
  - Reactions rarer than in adults
- Coagulopathy may need blood components, especially in neonates and cyanosed children; expect low platelet count and function

## Problems after bypass
- Residual defects
  - Found by echo or, if needed, catheterisation
  - Significant ones are fixed, possibly with more CPB
- Ventricular dysfunction (left and/or right)
  - Optimise preload, rate and rhythm, ionised calcium; start inotropes
  - Dopamine 3-10 mcg/kg/min: commonest first line, works across ages
  - Adrenaline 0.02-0.5 mcg/kg/min: persistent hypotension despite adequate preload
  - Milrinone 0.35-0.75 mcg/kg/min: inotrope, lusitrope and vasodilator; attractive after CPB, especially neonates; loading dose 25-50 mcg/kg, often on CPB
  - RV failure: an atrial right to left shunt keeps systemic output at the cost of cyanosis
  - Chest left open for 24-48 hours in some, helping RV recovery especially
  - ECMO or VAD in less than 5% of CPB cases; best outcomes with RV failure, reparative (not palliative) surgery and initial successful weaning
- Pulmonary hypertension
  - More likely after repair of large left to right shunts (AVSD, large VSD), pulmonary venous obstruction, or in neonates
  - Causes RV failure
  - Early management: careful ventilation, more inspired oxygen, inhaled nitric oxide; sometimes mechanical support

## Postoperative care
- Team approach in paediatric ICU
- Analgesia: IV opioid infusion (morphine common; fentanyl or alfentanil if unstable)
- Sedation: benzodiazepines, triclofos, alimemazine; IV or oral clonidine useful
- Propofol infusion is contraindicated for sedation in children (adverse effects)
- Some suit rapid weaning and extubation, even in theatre; passive pulmonary circulations benefit from early spontaneous breathing; others need longer ventilation
- Inotropes as needed; restrict fluids and use diuretics to avoid overload
- Extubation goals: stable on minimal inotropes, little bleeding, adequate respiratory function and oxygenation, adequate conscious level
- Renal and neurological dysfunction can complicate recovery

## Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA)
- When access needs the bypass cannulae removed; mainly complex neonatal repairs (TGA, interrupted aortic arch, TAPVC)
- Cool on bypass for at least 20 mins to 18-20°C, drain blood into the circuit, remove cannulae
- Neurological injury reported as minimal if arrest lasts less than 40 mins
- Brain protection from fast, even cooling; vasodilators help (phenoxybenzamine, phentolamine)
- Blood gas strategy debated: pH stat may protect better at extreme hypothermia (higher pCO2, cerebral vasodilatation, faster brain cooling) but evidence is not firm
- Steroids and barbiturates used for neuroprotection; none proven to improve outcome
- Alternative: regional low-flow perfusion via the innominate artery; no clear benefit over DHCA yet

## Flashcards
- Q: Alprostadil (PGE1) infusion dose to keep the duct open?
  - A: 5-50 nanograms/kg/min; watch for apnoea and vasodilatation.
- Q: Heparin dose and target ACT before paediatric CPB?
  - A: 300 units/kg; ACT over 400 secs.
- Q: Milrinone dosing after paediatric CPB?
  - A: Loading 25-50 mcg/kg (often on CPB), then 0.35-0.75 mcg/kg/min.
- Q: Safe duration of DHCA and the temperature used?
  - A: Less than 40 mins at 18-20°C, after at least 20 mins of cooling.
- Q: Ventilation goals after a Fontan?
  - A: Pulmonary flow is passive: avoid raised PVR and intrathoracic pressure; minimal peak pressures and short inspiratory times.
