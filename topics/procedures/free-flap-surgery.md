# Free Flap Surgery

Keep the flap perfused: a warm, well-filled, vasodilated, pain-free patient with good perfusion pressure, haematocrit around 30% and no sympathetic surges.

## Overview
- Reconstruction for **large wounds that cannot be closed primarily**
- Free tissue (skin, muscle, bone, bowel or a mix) is moved to the defect and its circulation restored by microvascular anastomosis
- Defects from trauma, infection or major surgery (e.g. mastectomy, head and neck cancer)
- Two types of autologous reconstruction
  - Pedicled skin or muscle flaps with intact blood supply (latissimus dorsi, TRAM)
  - Free flaps: deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP), superficial inferior epigastric artery (SIEA), gluteal artery perforator (GAP), profunda artery perforator
- Site and size of defect decide the flap, e.g.
  - Gracilis for lower leg trauma
  - Latissimus dorsi or rectus abdominis for breast reconstruction
  - Pectoralis major or radial forearm for head and neck
- In multiple trauma, treat life-threatening injuries and stabilise haemodynamics before reconstruction

## Flap physiology
- The flap moves with its own artery and vein, which are joined to vessels at the recipient site by microsurgery
- Stages
  - Flap raised and vessels clamped
  - Primary ischaemia: no flow, anaerobic metabolism; depends on surgical time, lasts 60–90 minutes
  - Reperfusion: anastomoses completed and clamps released
  - Secondary ischaemia: from later hypoperfusion of the flap (reduced by good anaesthetic care)

### Primary ischaemia
- Flap becomes anoxic: lactate builds, intracellular pH falls, ATP falls, calcium rises, inflammatory mediators accumulate
- Damage is proportional to **ischaemic time**
- High **metabolic rate** tissue suffers most: muscle is more sensitive than skin
- By the end:
  - Capillaries narrowed by endothelial swelling, vasoconstriction and oedema
  - Leucocytes trapped, ready to release proteolytic enzymes and reactive oxygen species
  - Endothelium less able to release vasodilators or clear vasoconstrictors
  - Cell membrane dysfunction; toxins accumulate inside and outside cells
  - Inflammatory enzyme systems up-regulated

### Reperfusion
- Starts when **clamps are released**
- Usually reverses the changes with little injury
- Unfavourable conditions cause ischaemia-reperfusion injury: inflow of inflammatory substrates can destroy the flap
- More likely with **long ischaemic time** or **poor perfusion pressure**

### Secondary ischaemia
- Occurs after transfer and reperfusion
- **More damaging than primary ischaemia**
- **Widespread intravascular thrombosis** and marked **interstitial oedema**; venous blood from the flap has raised fibrinogen and platelets
- Skin tolerates 10–12 hours of ischaemia, but **muscle shows irreversible changes after 4 hours**

### Causes of flap failure
- Poor flap perfusion: arterial, venous or oedema
  - **Arterial** anastomosis **inadequate**, in **spasm** or **thrombosed**
  - **Venous** anastomosis the same, or **compressed** (tight dressings, poor positioning)
  - **Oedema** reduces flow: **excess crystalloid**, extreme **haemodilution**, handling **trauma**, **long ischaemic time**
    - **Flaps have no lymphatic drainage**, so oedema forms easily

### Microcirculation
- **Repeated branching of arterioles and venules** from the main vessels; **flow here decides flap survival**
- Three functional parts
  - **Muscular arterioles** = **resistance vessels**, **control regional flow**; 20 to 50 micrometres across, rich in smooth muscle; **smooth muscle tone** sets resistance
  - **Capillaries** = **exchange vessels**; **precapillary sphincters** at the arterial end control capillary flow
  - **Venules** = **capacitance vessels**, a blood reservoir
- Control of **vessel diameter**
  - **Muscle vascular beds have dense adrenergic supply**, so **constrict strongly to sympathetic stimulation**, mainly via resistance vessels
  - **Precapillary sphincters constrict with sympathetic** input, but relax with **local factors**: hypoxia, hypercapnia, raised potassium, osmolality and magnesium
  - Other **vasoactive hormones** (renin, vasopressin, prostaglandins, kinins) also act
  - Flap vessels have **no sympathetic supply** but still respond to **local and humoral factors**, including circulating catecholamines
  - The recipient **feeding artery and draining vein** still respond to cold, catecholamines and drugs
- **Blood viscosity and rheology**
  - Set by **haematocrit**, plasma **viscosity** and **red cell properties** (**aggregation**, **deformability**)
  - After any surgery under GA:
    - More platelet aggregation and adhesion
    - Less red cell deformability
    - Higher whole blood viscosity
    - More clotting factors
    - Higher fibrinogen and red cell aggregation
    - Disturbed fibrinolysis
  - Red cell deformability needs normal 2,3-DPG; stored blood has almost none, so transfusion impairs it

## Main principles
- Core aim: **maintain flap perfusion and oxygen delivery**
- **Hagen–Poiseuille equation**: Q = ΔPπr⁴ / 8ηL
  - Flow rises with **pressure difference** and **radius to the fourth power**; falls with vessel length and **blood viscosity**; length is fixed
  - So aims are **good perfusion pressure**, **vasodilatation** and **low viscosity**
- The patient's **physiological state strongly affects flap survival**; anaesthetic and postoperative care directly affect outcome
- Long surgery (often 6–8 hours) with several wound sites: **large blood, fluid and heat losses**; resulting **hypovolaemic vasoconstriction** and **hypothermia** cause flap failure if not corrected
- Other: issues of **long surgery**

### Vasodilatation
- **Vessel radius is the strongest determinant of flow**, both feeding and flap vessels; avoid sympathetic stimulation
- **Prevent cold-induced vasoconstriction**: keep warm in theatre, recovery and the ward for the first 24–48 hours
  - **Temperature monitoring**
  - **Active warming** with a forced-air blanket
    - Consider **prewarming**: patients cool fast after induction from **redistribution**
  - Raise theatre temperature
  - Cold also **raises viscosity** (higher **haematocrit and plasma viscosity**, red cell **rouleaux**, **platelet aggregation**), reducing flap microcirculation
- **Avoid hypovolaemia, but also flap oedema**
  - Two wound sites (donor and recipient), both with insensible and possibly blood losses
  - **Mild hypervolaemia lowers sympathetic tone and dilates the flap's supply vessels**
  - Fluid guide
    - **Crystalloid**
      - **10–20 ml/kg** for the **preoperative deficit**
      - **4–8 ml/kg/hour** for **insensible losses**
    - Colloid
      - 10–15 ml/kg for haemodilution
      - To replace blood loss
    - Blood: keep haematocrit at 30%
  - Some aim for **urine output 1–2 ml/kg/hour**, balanced against flap oedema
  - Consider **trending lactate**
- Choice of anaesthetic
  - Isoflurane preserves microcirculatory flow; sevoflurane may lessen ischaemia-reperfusion injury; desflurane effects unclear
  - **Propofol TIVA: less PONV, smoother recovery**; also **inhibits platelet aggregation** (possibly via the lipid emulsion on platelet-red cell interaction and leucocyte nitric oxide), which may lower thrombosis risk
- **Good analgesia and sympathetic blockade**
  - Analgesia lowers circulating catecholamines and prevents pain-induced vasoconstriction
  - Epidural, brachial plexus or interpleural local anaesthetic infusions during and after surgery add sympathetic block and further vasodilatation
    - Worry: denervated flap vessels cannot dilate after a lumbar epidural, so flow may be **"stolen"** from the flap
    - In practice, if block-related hypotension is treated, flap flow rises because recipient artery flow increases
    - Other benefits of regional: **less blood loss and vessel spasm** during and after surgery, less DVT, better diaphragm function, faster recovery
- **Avoid other sympathetic triggers: hypoxia, hypercapnia, hypoglycaemia, nausea and vomiting**
- **Avoid vasopressors**
- **Treat vasospasm**: follows **surgical handling or intimal damage**, during or after surgery
  - Surgeons may apply **topical vasodilators**: papaverine, lidocaine or verapamil

### Low blood viscosity
- **Isovolaemic haemodilution** to **haematocrit 30% improves flow**: lower viscosity, less muscle reperfusion injury, more open capillaries, less necrosis
- **Going lower adds little**, as the viscosity-haematocrit curve flattens, and **oxygen delivery falls**
- Low haematocrit raises myocardial work: care with poor cardiac reserve

### Adequate perfusion pressure
- Pressure gradient depends mainly on **systolic pressure**; a **wide pulse pressure** is essential
- Aim **systolic BP >100mmHg**
- **Appropriate depth** and **aggressive fluid management** usually suffice
  - **Most inotropes are contraindicated** because they vasoconstrict; if needed, dobutamine or low-dose dopamine
- Avoid **pressure on the flap**, which raises venous pressure
- Avoid **coughing**, e.g. at extubation

### Adequate oxygen delivery
- DO2 = CO x [(Hb x sat x 1.34) + (PaO2 x 0.003)]
- Besides cardiac output, **keep oxygenation** and **haematocrit** up

## Preoperative assessment
- Check comorbidities and fitness for long, extensive surgery
- Smokers should stop at least 4 weeks before: nicotine vasoconstricts; carbon monoxide causes tissue hypoxia; platelet aggregation increases, making blood hypercoagulable
- **IV fluids to maintain hydration while fasting**
  - Hand surgeons often prescribe **3L Hartmann's**

## Intraoperative management
### Technique
- GA
- Consider **regional block or catheter infusion**, ideally covering the **recipient** site (not the donor site) for full sympathetic benefit
  - Benefits: vasodilatation, reduced stress response, less DVT, less blood loss
  - Possibly less flap vasospasm
  - But: the flap is denervated, so concern that sympathetic block lowers MAP and causes ischaemia by steal
    - Animal model: 10% hypovolaemia reduced flap microcirculatory flow by 40%

### Monitoring, lines and equipment
- Large-bore peripheral cannula (18G)
- **Arterial line** for **safe control of perfusion pressure**, and for **frequent blood gases and haematocrit**
- Consider CVP: reflects filling and can be used to raise cardiac output
- **Hourly urine output** for volume status
- **Core temperature monitoring** and **active warming** are essential
  - Keep core-peripheral gradient <2deg
  - Nasopharyngeal or rectal probe in theatre; intermittent tympanic or axillary in recovery and ward
    - **Peripheral temperature**: falling skin temperature suggests hypovolaemia and vasoconstriction; core-peripheral difference under 2ºC means warm and well filled
    - **Skin flap temperature** is sometimes monitored after surgery, but it is insensitive: by the time it falls, the flap is usually unsalvageable
  - **Forced-air warmer and circulating H2O mattress**
  - Consider fluid warmer
  - Consider prewarming
  - Consider raising theatre temperature
- Calf compression for DVT prophylaxis in all patients

### Positioning (as for any long operation)
- **Position and pad limbs carefully** to avoid nerve injury or **vascular compression**
- **Tape eyes** to prevent corneal abrasion and drying
- **VTE** prophylaxis

### Airway and ventilation
- **Intubate** (long surgery)
- Ventilate to **normocapnia**: hypocapnia raises peripheral resistance and lowers cardiac output; hypercapnia stimulates the sympathetic system
- **Avoid hypoxia**

### Maintenance
- Goal: warm, well-filled, sympathetically blocked patient with high cardiac output
- Low SVR, wide pulse pressure, hyperdynamic circulation
  - **Avoid vasopressors**: small phenylephrine boluses may be needed, but **no phenylephrine infusion**
  - **Adequate analgesia**
  - **Appropriate depth**
  - Avoid other sympathetic triggers, e.g. hypoglycaemia
- Fluids
  - **Crystalloid** replaces the **fasting deficit**, provides **maintenance** and covers **insensible losses**; **too much causes flap oedema**
  - **Hypervolaemic haemodilution** with **colloid**: albumin, Gelafundin
  - Aim **urine output >1ml/kg/hr**
  - Ideally fill to CVP 12mmHg (trends only are meaningful)
- **Haematocrit 30%**: blood gas for haematocrit at the start and every 2 hours
  - Best balance of lower viscosity, oxygen flux and bleeding
  - Transfuse if Hb <8
- **Systolic BP >100mmHg**
- Propofol: some evidence of better microvascular flow, avoiding the volatile effect on red cell membrane stiffness
- Remifentanil
- Others
  - Avoid beta blockers: cause peripheral vasoconstriction
  - Vasodilators usually not needed

### Emergence
- **Smooth, cough-free emergence**: low-dose remifentanil, LMA exchange
- **Good analgesia** before waking

## Postoperative care
- Avoid vasoconstriction in recovery: cold, pain, low volume, hypotension, hypocapnia
  - **Prevent PONV**: it drives sympathetic tone, and vomiting raises venous pressure, lowering flap perfusion pressure
    - Consider **TIVA**
  - **Good analgesia**: **perineural catheter infusion** or IV **PCA**
  - **Treat shivering**: IV pethidine 25mg, clonidine, chlorpromazine
- Continue regional: epidural for lower limb, axillary catheter for upper limb
- Hourly flap observations; surgeons arrange **Doppler** monitoring of flap flow
  - Arterial problem: pale, pulseless, sluggish capillary refill
  - Venous problem: swollen, dusky, blanches easily, brisk capillary refill
- Some plastic surgeons ask to keep Hb >9
- Disposition: may need surgical high dependency

## Flashcards
- Q: What are the three anaesthetic goals derived from Hagen–Poiseuille for free flaps?
  - A: Good perfusion pressure, vasodilatation, low blood viscosity
- Q: Target haematocrit and systolic BP in free flap surgery?
  - A: Haematocrit 30%; systolic BP >100mmHg
- Q: How long can skin and muscle tolerate ischaemia?
  - A: Skin 10–12 hours; muscle irreversible changes after 4 hours
- Q: Crystalloid guide for free flap surgery?
  - A: 10–20 ml/kg for preoperative deficit plus 4–8 ml/kg/hour for insensible losses
- Q: How do arterial and venous flap compromise differ clinically?
  - A: Arterial: pale, pulseless, sluggish refill; venous: swollen, dusky, brisk refill
