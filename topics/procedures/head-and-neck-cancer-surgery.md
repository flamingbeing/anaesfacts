# Head and Neck Cancer Surgery

Long operations with a difficult, shared airway, blood loss and often a free flap; extubation timing is critical.

## Main considerations
- Difficult airway
- Shared airway; tracheostomy
- Long surgery
- Blood loss and fluid shifts
- May need a flap

## Airway management
- Why the airway is difficult
  - Tumour bulk blocks the view of the glottis
  - Hidden spread to the tongue base fixes the tongue and hampers laryngoscopy
  - Previous surgery: distortion, oedema, fibrosis
  - Previous radiotherapy: stiff neck, poor extension, limited mouth opening
  - Tumours are friable: they bleed, and pieces can break off and fall distally with laryngoscopy
  - Staged reconstruction without a tracheostomy: awake fibreoptic intubation, as laryngoscopy forces can damage the earlier repair
- Shared airway
  - High risk of tube kinking, disconnection or puncture by surgical instruments: stay alert
  - Secure the tube well (e.g. strong tape); pad eyes with sponge and ears with gauze; surgeon can suture the tube to the nostril
  - Nasal intubation with a preformed tube is usually preferred for oral surgery, unless an early tracheostomy is planned
    - Avoid pressure on the nostril
  - Armoured tube or north-facing RAE
  - Throat pack may be needed
  - Tape eyes well; place BIS away from the surgical field
- Tracheostomy may be done: a normal ETT first is then fine
- Possible airway fire risk

## Monitoring and access
- Arterial line for major resections and flaps
- Large-bore IV access; consider the saphenous vein
- Long surgery: urinary catheter and temperature monitoring (rectal probe)
- If a central line is needed, avoid the neck: femoral or subclavian

## Positioning
- Head-up (reverse Trendelenburg) to improve venous drainage and reduce ooze
- Arms tucked
- Long surgery: careful padding of pressure points (back, heels)

## Intraoperative issues
- Nerve monitoring (spinal accessory, facial) may be used to protect them: neuromuscular blockers are then contraindicated
- Venous air embolism: uncommon but a concern in radical neck surgery
- Carotid sinus and stellate ganglion handling in radical neck dissection: wide BP swings, arrhythmias, sinus arrest, prolonged QT
- Traction on the maxilla: bradycardia via the trigeminovagal reflex

## Emergence
- Without a tracheostomy, extubation timing is crucial; depends on oedema and airway distortion from surgery
- After long cases (including free flaps) the patient may stay intubated and sedated overnight in ICU
- Others can be extubated fully awake in theatre or recovery
- Have the same airway equipment ready at extubation as at induction: oedema and altered anatomy may make mask ventilation impossible if obstruction follows
- Aim for a smooth emergence to limit bleeding

## Postoperative care
- Neck haematoma can compress the trachea
  - Intubate at once if breathing is compromised; may be difficult due to tracheal deviation and compression
  - Opening the wound to evacuate the haematoma may relieve obstruction and ease intubation

## Radical neck dissection
- Removes sternomastoid, internal and external jugular veins and associated lymph nodes
- Preoperative: careful airway assessment; often smokers with comorbidities
- Intraoperative
  - Prevent hypothermia
  - Blood available
  - Invasive monitoring: never in the neck (femoral central line)
  - Remifentanil
  - Restrictive fluids
  - Watch for air embolism and carotid sinus handling
  - Dexamethasone for swelling
  - Long case with higher air embolism risk
  - Usually GA with an ETT and controlled ventilation; spontaneous breathing is relatively contraindicated because opened neck veins can entrain air
  - Brachial plexus monitoring may rule out paralysis, yet muscle relaxation helps dissection around big neck muscles: agree timing and dose of blockade with the surgeon
  - More circuit disconnections and significant bleeding
  - Frequent head moves cause disconnection or tube malposition (endobronchial with flexion, cuff herniation with extension): suspect these first with ventilator alarms or hypoxaemia
- Postoperative
  - Head and neck oedema for several days from reduced venous return: head up, limit IV fluids
  - Avoid rebound hypertension after extubation (risk of wound haematoma)
    - Continue low-dose remifentanil
    - Adequate morphine before the end
    - Treat hypertension early and aggressively
  - Analgesic needs are usually surprisingly low
  - Clonidine to keep BP down

## Mandibulectomy
- Usually for malignant oral tumours
- Types
  - Marginal (rim): removes the upper alveolar part and leaves the lower border intact; used when tumour abuts bone; usually preferred
  - Segmental: full-thickness segment removed, leaving a gap; needed if the cortex is invaded
  - Hemiglossectomy
  - Composite: part of the mandible removed together with involved soft tissue
- Often combined with neck dissection
- Needs reconstruction
  - Marginal: intraoral advancement flaps (edentulous), pectoralis major myocutaneous flap or radial free flap
  - Segmental: bone (fibula free flap) or a titanium bar under a pectoralis major flap
- Usually needs a tracheostomy; an intraoral marginal resection closed locally may allow 2–3 days of intubation instead
- Surgical time: 6-8 h plus 3-6 h for the flap (very long)

## Maxillectomy
- Total (with or without orbital exenteration), subtotal or limited; mostly for malignant tumours
- Bleeding can be heavy; it increases as dissection moves front to back; the pterygoid plexus is the usual source
  - Mean blood loss: maxillectomy 1422.7 ml; major mandibulectomy 846.6 ml; minor mandibulectomy 426.0 ml
  - Surgeons may ask for hypotensive anaesthesia
- Airway: usually oral intubation; rarely a problem

## Glossectomy
- Partial or total, for tongue tumours
- Exposure with a side-biting or Dingman gag; resected with diathermy; usually closed primarily
- Total glossectomy is often combined with laryngectomy because of aspiration
- May be combined with neck dissection or mandibulectomy
- Anaesthesia
  - Nasal intubation helps but is not essential
  - Full muscle relaxation needed
  - Antisialagogue (hyoscine or glycopyrrolate) reduces secretions
  - Tracheostomy may be needed depending on extent and site; otherwise oral intubation for 24–48 h may suffice
  - If neither, a short steroid course reduces tongue swelling
  - NG tube for postoperative feeding

## Pharyngectomy
- Removes the pharynx (glossectomy plus radical tonsillectomy); may need a mandibular split and tissue transfer
- Preoperative: agree access with the surgeon (forearm free flap); careful airway assessment; often smokers with comorbidities
- Intraoperative
  - Supine, shoulder roll, head ring, head up
  - ETT, then tracheostomy
  - Invasive monitoring
  - Keep well filled to minimise vasopressors
  - Fine-bore NG tube, well secured
  - Remifentanil works well
- Postoperative: ICU, flap observations, humidification, analgesia

## Laryngectomy
- Removes the larynx and creates an end tracheostomy
- Preoperative: thorough airway assessment; usually smokers with comorbidities; speech and language therapy prepares for life with a stoma
- Intraoperative
  - Supine, shoulder roll, head ring, head up
  - ETT swapped for a tracheostomy tube during surgery
    - Long tracheostomy tube helps access and stoma suturing
    - Change to a standard tracheostomy tube at the end
  - Invasive monitoring; if CVP needed, femoral or subclavian
  - Fine-bore NG tube for feeding, sutured to the nasal septum
  - Prevent hypothermia
  - Remifentanil works very well
  - Watch for air embolism
- Postoperative
  - HDU, humidification
  - Drugs for persistent coughing: morphine, benzodiazepine, propofol
  - Future anaesthetics via the stoma: upside-down paediatric face mask, LMA over the neck stoma, or intubate after spraying local anaesthetic on the stoma

## Flap reconstruction
- Often needed after radical maxillectomy or mandibulectomy
- Flap type chosen by defect: skin graft, local, pedicled fasciocutaneous, musculocutaneous, osteocutaneous
- Blood loss can be fast and large: slight head-up tilt, controlled hypotension, adrenaline infiltration
- Plastic surgeons join; crossing the midline makes the defect much larger
- The raised flap is kept perfused under warm packs until needed; frozen section results come first
- Free flap principles
  - Haematocrit of 27-30% is desirable for flow
  - Avoid diuresis to keep the flap perfused after surgery
  - Hypertension causes flap oedema and bleeding; hypotension causes poor flap perfusion
  - Treat hypotension by lightening volatile and giving fluid, not vasoconstrictors; use inotropes if still inadequate
  - No cannulas or monitoring on donor sites

## Flashcards
- Q: Why is awake fibreoptic intubation advised for staged reconstruction without a tracheostomy?
  - A: Laryngoscopy forces can damage the earlier reconstruction.
- Q: Where should central access go for neck dissection?
  - A: Femoral or subclavian; avoid the neck.
- Q: Mean blood loss in maxillectomy versus major and minor mandibulectomy?
  - A: 1422.7 ml versus 846.6 ml and 426.0 ml.
- Q: Target haematocrit for microvascular free flaps?
  - A: 27-30%.
- Q: Why is spontaneous ventilation relatively contraindicated in neck dissection?
  - A: Opened neck veins can entrain air (air embolism).
