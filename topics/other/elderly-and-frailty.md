# Anaesthesia for the Elderly and Frailty

Elderly patients are multisystem, often frail and prone to delirium: reduce doses, monitor depth and pressure closely, and plan aftercare.

## Key themes
- Multisystem disease, frailty, delirium
- Default set-up: BIS, low-dose propofol, consider 5 lead ECG

## Problems by system
- Organ function declines overall
- Cardiovascular: ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, arrhythmias
- Respiratory: reduced lung function
- Neurological: cognitive decline, stroke
- Renal: reduced function
- Airway: limited neck extension, poor dentition
- Drugs:
  - Altered pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics; avoid NSAIDs
  - More sensitive to side effects (e.g. induction agents, opioids)

## Preoperative assessment
- Thorough systems review is essential
- Sustainable exercise level is a good guide to cardiorespiratory fitness, but joint disease often limits it
- Assess mental state: abbreviated mental test or mini mental state examination help separate dementia from acute confusion
- Dentition often poor; may be edentulous
- After a fracture, look for the medical cause of the fall
- Avoid sedative premedication, especially benzodiazepines, centrally acting anticholinergics and pethidine
- Do not stop usual alcohol the day before surgery; nicotine patches may help smokers

## Intraoperative management
- Monitoring:
  - Measure temperature; prevent hypothermia with fluid warmers, active warming and a warmer theatre
  - Consider CVP, arterial line or cardiac output monitoring: poor tolerance of both fluid overload and hypovolaemia
- Airway:
  - Protect fragile teeth
  - In edentulous patients, leaving dentures in may help airway patency and mask seal
- Drugs:
  - MAC and anaesthetic requirements are lower
  - Lower cardiac output shrinks initial volume of distribution, so less induction agent is needed
  - Use BIS to avoid overdose and reduce postoperative delirium
  - Slow arm-brain circulation delays onset of IV induction agents: flush with saline and wait, or overdose follows
  - Greater fall in blood pressure after induction and after spinal or epidural: monitor closely
- Pressure care:
  - Long surgery and hypotension raise pressure sore risk
  - Pad well; in long cases relieve pressure and massage vulnerable areas from time to time
- Analgesia:
  - Multimodal and regional techniques to reduce opioid need
  - NSAIDs with caution: consider a COX-2 selective drug or add gastroprotection

## Postoperative management
- High-dependency care is ideal after major surgery
- Monitor and correct fluid balance, vital signs, electrolytes and haematology
- Patients with cardiovascular disease may need Hb >9–10g/dl
- Postoperative confusion: look for reversible causes (pain, hypoxaemia, full bladder, myocardial or cerebral ischaemia, electrolyte disturbance, drugs)
- Pain is often undertreated in confused or demented patients; they still feel pain, and poor control worsens confusion
- Early enteral feeding (nasogastric if needed) may improve outcome
- Early physiotherapy, mobilisation and thromboprophylaxis are essential

## Frailty
- Definitions:
  - Frailty: multidimensional geriatric syndrome of reduced physiological reserve and reduced resistance to stressors across many organ systems
  - Sarcopenia: loss of muscle mass with low strength or physical performance
  - Cachexia: complex metabolic syndrome linked to underlying illness or inflammation
- Pathogenesis:
  - Chronic low-grade inflammation ("inflammaging"), immunosenescence
  - Neuroendocrine dysregulation
- Risk factors:
  - Physiological: age, nutrition
  - Comorbidities: strongest link with cardiovascular disease (including silent disease); also COPD, diabetes, malignancy, cognitive impairment
  - Psychosocial: depression (two-way link), lower socioeconomic status, female sex (despite longer life)
  - Disability: difficulty with basic and instrumental activities of daily living
- Implications:
  - Higher risk of postoperative complications
  - May be partly reversible
- Assessment tools:
  - Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS): clinician-rated global scale of function and comorbidity
    - Scored 1–9; ≥4 = frail
    - Quick but subjective
  - Fried's Frailty Phenotype
  - Edmonton Frail Scale
  - Frailty Index
- Optimisation: prehabilitation

## Flashcards
- Q: Which sedative premedications should be avoided in the elderly?
  - A: Benzodiazepines, centrally acting anticholinergics and pethidine.
- Q: Why are IV induction agents slow in onset in the elderly?
  - A: Prolonged arm-brain circulation time; flush and wait to avoid overdose.
- Q: What Hb may elderly patients with cardiovascular disease need postoperatively?
  - A: >9–10g/dl.
- Q: What is the Clinical Frailty Scale range and the frailty cut-off in these notes?
  - A: 1–9; ≥4 = frail.
