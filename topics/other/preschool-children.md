# Preschool and Young Children

Usually healthy but anxious and uncooperative; key issues are anxiety and induction, laryngospasm and fast desaturation, temperature, and emergence delirium.

## Summary
- Anxiety: inhalational induction or EMLA
- Emergence delirium
- Airway: Microcuff tube

## Main considerations
- Usually healthy
- Airway: laryngospasm risk; different anatomy
  - Traditionally uncuffed tubes under 8y (all preschool and younger)
  - Now Microcuff tubes
- Respiratory: URTI and asthma common; desaturate faster; diaphragmatic splinting
- Cardiovascular: good function unless congenital heart disease; usually just need fluids, no need for phenylephrine
  - Beware bradycardia
  - May have a patent foramen ovale: avoid air bubbles
- CNS: uncooperative; may have special needs (e.g. autism); premedication; inhalational induction or EMLA; emergence delirium
- Temperature: prone to hypothermia
- Haematology: blood volume 75ml/kg
- Drugs and fluids: weight-based doses, smaller volumes, fluid requirements, different pharmacokinetics
- Monitoring: different normal ranges; SpO2 alone is enough in recovery
- Equipment: different sizes
- Compare with neonates and infants

## Preoperative assessment
- Drug allergy, especially whether paracetamol is tolerated
- Birth and developmental history
- Past history, especially asthma and URTI
- Airway if the child allows
- Blood tests usually unnecessary: cost without benefit
- Psychological assessment: anxieties and need for sedative premedication
- Vital signs: different normal ranges
- Neonates need extra considerations

## Preparation and orders
- Equipment
  - ETT size; Microcuff or uncuffed
  - Paediatric breathing circuit
  - Paediatric infusion pump and IV set
  - Forced-air warmer and temperature probe
- Warm theatre
- Either IV cannula on the ward or EMLA cream
- Consider paediatric HDU
- May need premedication

## Intraoperative management
### Theatre preparation
- Warm the theatre; switch on the forced-air warmer
- Drugs
  - Calculate doses beforehand, including emergency drugs; draw them up
  - Emergency drugs drawn: atropine, suxamethonium
  - Draw up a flush
- Equipment
  - Set the monitor to paediatric mode
  - Check the machine, paediatric circuits, masks, airways, laryngoscope blades and ETTs
  - Prepare the infusion set

### Before induction
- Review the preoperative assessment
- Decide on premedication
- Consider EMLA
- Build rapport: talk to the child first
- Have a distraction ready, e.g. video

### Induction
- Usually inhalational if no IV access: sevoflurane and nitrous oxide
- Some use thiopentone 5-7mg/kg (painless injection)

### Maintenance
- Watch very closely: laryngospasm and rapid desaturation
- TIVA needs paediatric pharmacokinetic models

### Emergence
- Wrap the hand and tape down the IV cannula; consider disconnecting the IV line
- Deep extubation or deep LMA removal

## Recovery
- Often only pulse oximetry needed
- Emergence delirium may occur
- Parents often join the child once staff are happy with the child's condition; their presence reassures and limits mild disorientation

## Other points
- Regional blocks usually done under GA (caudal and other neuraxial, peripheral nerve blocks)
- Sedation is usually deep sedation or GA without airway instrumentation

## Flashcards
- Q: Traditional age limit for uncuffed tubes?
  - A: Under 8y; Microcuff tubes now used
- Q: Blood volume in preschool children?
  - A: 75ml/kg
- Q: Thiopentone induction dose in young children?
  - A: 5-7mg/kg (painless injection)
- Q: Emergency drugs to draw up before a paediatric induction?
  - A: Atropine and suxamethonium
- Q: Usual induction without IV access?
  - A: Inhalational with sevoflurane and nitrous oxide
