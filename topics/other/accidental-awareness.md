# Accidental Awareness during General Anaesthesia

Rare but serious; most linked to neuromuscular blockade and dynamic phases (induction, emergence). Prevent by vigilance, adequate dosing and checking effect before paralysis.

## Incidence
- Traditionally reported at about 1–2:1000 general anaesthetics
  - Older reports higher in obstetrics (1:384), cardiac (1:43) and paediatrics (1:135)
- NAP5 (largest study): 1:19 000
  - With paralysis: 1 in 8,000
  - Without paralysis: 1 in 130,000
- Mostly at induction (intubation) or emergence; only 1/3rd of NAP5 cases during maintenance

## Consequences
- In NAP5 only one-third reported pain or distress; most felt no pain
- Memories are usually brief sounds or sensations, e.g. a tube in the mouth
- Psychological effects vary with the patient and experience
  - Anxiety, fear of surgery and anaesthesia, sleep disturbance
  - Flashbacks, nightmares, PTSD, depression

## Risk factors
- Anaesthetic
  - Neuromuscular blockade: by far the biggest risk
  - TIVA
    - Higher with neuromuscular blockade (4x risk)
    - Especially with manual (non-TCI) infusions
  - Induction
    - RSI: blocker given after a fixed hypnotic dose without checking loss of consciousness
    - Difficult airway: anaesthesia not maintained during prolonged airway attempts
  - Emergence
    - Planned awake extubation; almost one-fifth of NAP5 reports came from emergence, mostly distress from paralysis
    - Residual blockade at extubation may be perceived as awareness
  - Drug error: blocker given to an awake patient
- Patient
  - Frail, critically ill, higher ASA: lower anaesthetic doses used for cardiovascular stability
  - Personal or family history of awareness: NAP5 suggests a possible genetic factor; 25% of maintenance-phase cases had no explanation
  - Obesity: uncertain dosing
  - Young women
- Surgical
  - Emergency surgery: RSI, frail patients, out of hours
  - Obstetric, especially caesarean section (1:670, highest risk)
    - Pressure to shorten induction-to-incision time
    - RSI, neuromuscular blockade, thiopentone
    - Opioids omitted at induction
    - Urgent, out of hours
  - Cardiac surgery
    - Lower propofol or volatile doses used to protect MAP and coronary perfusion
    - Going on and off bypass alters plasma drug levels (haemodilution, changed volume of distribution)
  - Trauma: haemodynamic instability
  - Thoracic: lung isolation and hypoxia distract from depth monitoring
  - Transfer from anaesthetic room to theatre
    - Or switching to TIVA to move a paralysed patient after volatile maintenance
- Context: seniority of the anaesthetist

## Prevention
- Identify high-risk patients
  - NAP5: warn all patients of the risk of awareness when consenting, in proportion to the risk
  - NAP5: consent for sedation should stress that the patient will be awake and may recall parts of the procedure
- NAP5: at induction give an adequate IV dose and confirm effect before paralysis or airway instrumentation
- Constant vigilance: label all drugs; check equipment (pumps programmed and plugged in)
- NAP5: if awareness is suspected during maintenance, reassure the patient verbally, add analgesia and deepen anaesthesia
- NAP5: use the smallest blocker dose that gives adequate conditions, titrated with a nerve stimulator (essential)
- NAP5: follow published TIVA guidelines
- Processed EEG (BIS, SEDLine) may help by indicating level of consciousness
  - Controversial: the largest recent RCT did not show it prevents awareness
  - May be useful specifically for TIVA with neuromuscular blockade
  - Rarely used in the UK, so little role in NAP5: just 2.8% of general anaesthetics had depth monitoring
- NAP5: at emergence confirm surgery is finished, then confirm full reversal before waking; talk to the patient, especially during awake extubation

## Detecting awareness
- True incidence is hard to measure
  - Dreaming around surgery is common (3 to 6%) and is not awareness, but can confuse
  - Recall of sounds, voices or airway devices still in place on waking may be mistaken for awareness
- Patient reports
- Brice interview (standard tool)
  - Last thing remembered before going to sleep?
  - First thing remembered on waking?
  - Anything remembered during surgery?
  - Any dreams while asleep?
  - Modified version adds: worst thing about the surgery?

## Signs during anaesthesia
- Tachycardia, hypertension
- Patient movement
- High BIS

## Immediate management
- Stop surgery
- Reassure the patient that they are safe
- Deepen anaesthesia promptly with a propofol bolus
  - Consider a benzodiazepine
  - Opioids for the sympathetic surge
- Check equipment and switch technique: if TIVA may have failed (pump fault, extravasation), switch to a volatile at once
