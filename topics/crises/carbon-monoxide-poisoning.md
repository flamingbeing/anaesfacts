# Carbon monoxide poisoning

CO binds haemoglobin far more avidly than oxygen and blocks mitochondrial respiration, causing tissue hypoxia with a falsely normal SpO2.

## Background

- CO: colourless, odourless, tasteless, non-irritant gas, slightly lighter than air
- Toxic above about 35 ppm to animals that carry oxygen on haemoglobin; small amounts are made by normal metabolism
- Most common cause of fatal air poisoning in many countries
- 1865: Claude Bernard showed CO and oxygen compete for the same haemoglobin binding site

## Causes

- Smoke inhalation from fires (most cases)
- Smoking
  - Baseline COHb up to 3 percent in nonsmokers, 10 to 15 percent in smokers
- Volatile anaesthetics

## Pathophysiology

- Affinity for haemoglobin 240 times that of oxygen, forming carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb)
- Binding at one haem changes the molecule's shape so the other three sites release oxygen poorly
  - Oxygen dissociation curve shifts left; less oxygen released to tissues (anaemic-type hypoxia)
- About 10 to 15 percent of CO sits outside the blood, bound to myoglobin, cytochromes and NADPH reductase
  - Blocks cytochrome oxidase (cytochrome a3), impairing oxidative phosphorylation: histotoxic hypoxia
  - Similar to cyanide but clinically less important
- Most vulnerable brain areas: basal ganglia, hippocampus, cerebral cortex
- Neurological and psychiatric problems can appear after recovery

## Pharmacokinetics

- Absorbed rapidly across the lungs
- Eliminated almost only via the lungs, as oxygen displaces it from haemoglobin
- Elimination depends mainly on oxygenation, less on minute ventilation
- Half-life
  - Room air: about 250 to 320 minutes
  - High-flow oxygen by non-rebreathing mask: about 90 minutes
  - 100 percent hyperbaric oxygen: about 30 minutes

## Clinical features

- Mild to moderate: constitutional symptoms; headache is the most common
- Cherry-red lips and skin
- Neurological: seizures, syncope, coma
- Cardiac: myocardial ischaemia, ventricular arrhythmias, pulmonary oedema
- Severe lactic acidosis
- Delayed neuropsychiatric sequelae
  - Not explained by impaired oxygen delivery or use
  - Mechanism unclear; probably lipid peroxidation by oxygen radicals from xanthine oxidase

## Diagnosis

- Standard pulse oximetry cannot detect CO: COHb is read as oxyhaemoglobin, so SpO2 reads near 100%
- Arterial PO2 is normal, so carotid and aortic bodies do not sense hypoxia
- Yet haemoglobin-bound oxygen (normally 98 percent of arterial oxygen content) is greatly reduced
- Confirm raised COHb by co-oximetry on a blood gas; venous samples are accurate in haemodynamically stable patients
  - COHb <20%: neurological impairment unusual
  - COHb >50%: neurological and cardiac ischaemia, usually fatal

## Treatment

- High-flow oxygen
- Hyperbaric oxygen
