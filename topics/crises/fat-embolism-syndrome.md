# Fat Embolism Syndrome

Marrow or adipose fat enters the veins after trauma, blocking the lung vessels and triggering systemic inflammation; classic triad hypoxia, confusion and petechial rash.

## Key points
- Triad: hypoxia, altered mental state, rash
- Onset slow, over 1 or more days
- Diagnosed with the Gurd and Wilson criteria

## Definition
- Fat from necrotic bone marrow or fat cells is released into the venous circulation after trauma or tissue injury
- Leads to mechanical blockage of the pulmonary vascular bed
- Sets off a systemic inflammatory response

## Epidemiology
- Fat embolism is common; the syndrome is rare (below 1%)
- Isolated long bone injury: 0.25%-0.9% (1 in 111 to 1 in 385)
- Multiple closed fractures: 1.3% (1 in 78)
- Long bone plus pelvic injury: up to 11%

## Causes
- Usually traumatic: 90% follow blunt trauma with pelvic and long bone fractures
  - Risk rises with injury severity and the number of large marrow-containing bones involved
- Non-traumatic causes are rare: pancreatitis, sickle cell disease, liposuction, long-term steroids

## Pathophysiology
- Mechanical theory (Gauss)
  - High pressure in the marrow cavity pushes fat into the veins
  - Fat reaches the systemic circulation through a PFO, arteriovenous shunts, or by squeezing through lung capillaries
  - Large and small emboli block capillary beds, causing local ischaemia and organ dysfunction
  - Does not explain the delay before the syndrome, which usually appears 24-72 h after injury
- Biochemical theory
  - Serum lipase breaks neutral fat into free fatty acids, which damage endothelium directly
- Coagulation theory
  - Marrow fat sets off a prothrombotic and inflammatory cascade (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-alpha)
  - Leads to clumping of microemboli and thrombocytopenia
- Severity of shock depends on:
  - (1) Total amount and speed of fat embolisation
  - (2) Immunogenicity of the embolised material
  - (3) Strength of the host inflammatory response
  - (4) Pre-existing cardiopulmonary disease

## Clinical features
- Very variable; often a diagnosis of exclusion
  - Needs a high index of suspicion and early supportive care
- Timing: insidious onset 24-72 h after injury (median 48.5 h); rarely seen during surgery
- Mainly affects lungs, skin and brain
- Classic triad, seen in some patients:
  - Hypoxaemia (96%)
  - Altered mental state (59%)
  - Petechial rash on upper trunk and axillae (33%)
- Respiratory (major feature)
  - Classically sudden breathlessness, tachypnoea and hypoxia
  - Usually 72 hours after injury
  - Most clinically important feature
  - Cannot be told apart from ARDS in polytrauma
  - Tachycardia: early and persistent
- Neurological (major feature)
  - Cerebral symptoms without head injury
  - Confusion and agitation
- Skin (major feature)
  - Red-brown, non-palpable petechial rash (only in 25-50%)
  - Non-dependent areas: axillae, neck, conjunctiva
- Fever
- Jaundice
- Renal impairment
- Retinal changes: fat, petechiae

## Investigations
- May be normal; no specific diagnostic test
- Full blood count: falling haemoglobin, thrombocytopenia
- Raised ESR
- Chest X-ray: often diffuse interstitial infiltrates
- Chest CT: diffuse vascular congestion and pulmonary oedema
- CT pulmonary angiogram: emboli lodge distally, so no vessel occlusion may be seen; peripheral ground glass possible
- MRI brain: imaging of choice
  - "Starfield" pattern (diffuse microvascular ischaemia) on diffusion-weighted imaging, from as early as 1 h after symptom onset

## Gurd and Wilson criteria
- Major
  - Respiratory distress
  - Cerebral symptoms (without head injury)
  - Petechial rash
- Minor (clinical)
  - Tachycardia
  - Fever
  - Jaundice
  - Renal impairment
  - Retinal changes: fat, petechiae
- Minor (laboratory)
  - Falling haemoglobin
  - Thrombocytopenia
  - Raised ESR
  - Fat macroglobulinaemia
- Diagnosis: 2 major, or 1 major plus 4 minor criteria

## Management
- Prevention: early fixation of long bone fractures
  - External fixation or plating pushes out less marrow than intramedullary nailing
- Treatment is supportive, aiming to:
  - (1) Restore haemodynamic stability
  - (2) Maintain oxygenation to prevent end-organ damage
- Early resuscitation and stabilisation, including oxygen
- Mechanical ventilation needed in 10-40%
- Steroids are controversial
  - Some evidence for prophylaxis in high-risk patients
  - No evidence for use once FES has developed
- Usually resolves within 7 days

## Prognosis
- Early mortality 1-20%
- Serious long-term complications are uncommon
