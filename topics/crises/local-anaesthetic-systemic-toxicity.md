# Local Anaesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST)

High systemic local anaesthetic levels cause CNS then cardiovascular toxicity; treat with prompt lipid emulsion plus supportive care and ACLS.

## Quick card (LAST trolley)

- Intralipid 1.5ml/kg then 15ml/kg/hr (can repeat the bolus twice and double the infusion)
- Treat arrhythmias and seizures until the lipid works
- Avoid lignocaine, beta blockers, calcium channel blockers
- Expect prolonged CPR; use lower doses of adrenaline

## Mechanism

- Cardiotoxicity
  - Block cardiac Na+ channels and shorten the refractory period, causing arrhythmias
  - Direct myocardial depression (bupivacaine > lidocaine)
  - Bupivacaine is the most cardiotoxic
    - Dissociates from Na+ channels ten times more slowly, so depression persists; can lead to re-entrant arrhythmias and VF
    - Also higher tissue protein binding and higher lipophilicity
  - More potent agents (bupivacaine, tetracaine) are more cardiotoxic than less lipophilic ones (lignocaine, prilocaine, procaine)
- CNS toxicity
  - Rapid entry to the brain; biphasic effect
  - First, inhibitory interneurones are blocked: excitation (circumoral tingling, visual disturbance, tremor, dizziness), then convulsions
  - Then all central neurones are depressed: coma and apnoea

## Toxic dose

- Toxic doses are quoted for each agent
  - Vasoconstrictors change them
- Absorption varies so much by site that a toxic dose ignoring site means little
- Toxicity of several local anaesthetics given together is additive

## Risk factors

- Patient
  - Hypoxia and acidosis
  - Frailty: low muscle mass (skeletal muscle acts as a depot that blunts peaks in plasma level)
  - Elderly: less muscle, lower clearance, more end-organ sensitivity (reduce dose by 10% to 20%)
  - Neonates and infants <6mth: less alpha-1 acid glycoprotein (AAG), immature hepatic clearance
  - Liver or kidney dysfunction: reduced clearance
  - Pre-existing heart disease: more prone to cardiovascular effects
  - Mitochondrial disorders (e.g. carnitine deficiency)
- Drug
  - Choice of agent
    - CNS:CVS ratio (dose causing reversible CNS signs versus dose causing life-threatening, refractory cardiovascular collapse) measures safety: lignocaine 7, ropivacaine 4, bupivacaine 3
    - More lipophilic agents (e.g. bupivacaine) are more toxic
    - Higher tissue protein binding makes bupivacaine more toxic
    - All inactivate sodium channels, but bupivacaine leaves the channel ten times more slowly than lignocaine
    - Isomerism: levobupivacaine is less toxic than bupivacaine; lignocaine is achiral
  - Dose, plus any added vasoconstrictor
- Site of injection
  - Higher risk of direct intravascular injection: e.g. interscalene, stellate ganglion block
  - Faster absorption from vascular areas: e.g. scalp, bronchial mucosa, pleura
    - Absorption order: intravenous (or intra-arterial) > tracheal > intercostal/paravertebral > epidural > brachial plexus > sciatic > subcutaneous
  - Accidental IV injection gives a sudden spike in level; ultrasound guidance has reduced this risk
  - Rarely, a small intra-arterial dose during a neck block (interscalene, cervical plexus, stellate ganglion) causes LAST
    - Usually a seizure without cardiovascular effects, settling quickly

## Prevention

- Early recognition and lipid emulsion for anyone with signs or symptoms
- Consider lower doses in at-risk patients

## Timing of onset

- Can present at any time
- Intravascular injection: immediate
- After a bolus: any time in the next hour
- Catheter or IV infusion: hours or even days after starting, as drug builds up in tissues

## Clinical features

- Rising blood levels cause CNS toxicity first, then cardiovascular toxicity
- Early CNS symptoms (roughly in order)
  - Circumoral and tongue numbness, metallic taste
  - Tinnitus, lightheadedness, visual disturbance, irritability
  - Muscle twitching (2mcg/ml)
- Later: cardiovascular
  - Initial excitatory phase with hypertension and tachycardia
- Major CNS and cardiovascular signs: rapid decline over minutes is typical of severe LAST
  - Hypotension, myocardial depression, ventricular arrhythmias, bradycardia, conduction block
  - Seizures (10mcg/ml), drowsiness, loss of consciousness
  - Respiratory arrest (20mcg/ml), cardiac arrest (30mcg/ml)
- Large doses can cause cardiovascular collapse as the first sign
- After higher doses, monitor for at least 1hr, as onset at 1hr has been reported

## Management

Core: prompt lipid emulsion while supporting the circulation (ACLS).

- Stop giving local anaesthetic
- Call for help; declare an emergency
- Lipid emulsion (Intralipid 20%): bolus then infusion
  - Mechanism, "lipid sink": lipid binds the local anaesthetic (and other highly lipid-soluble drugs) and carries it from brain and heart to reservoir organs (skeletal muscle, liver)
  - Loading dose 1.5ml/kg over 1min, then infusion 15ml/kg/hr
    - The sink needs a large volume given fast (e.g. about 1.5 mL/kg over about 2 minutes) to create a lipid "bulk phase" in plasma
  - If still unstable after the first bolus (refractory LAST)
    - Up to 2 more boluses of 1.5ml/kg every 5 minutes, 3 in total
    - Double the infusion to 30ml/kg/hr
  - Maximum cumulative dose 12ml/kg, to avoid fat overload
  - Continue for at least 10 min after the circulation is stable
  - Example for a 70-kg adult
    - Bolus 100 ml over 1 min; continue CPR
    - Infusion 1000ml over 1hr
    - If circulation not restored: 2 further 100 ml boluses 5 min apart, and increase infusion to 1000ml/30mins
    - Maximum 12ml/kg (850ml total)
  - Propofol is not a substitute for lipid emulsion
- Supportive care
  - Airway: keep patent; intubate if needed
  - Breathing: 100% oxygen and adequate ventilation (hypoxia and acidosis worsen LAST)
  - Circulation: secure IV access
- Cardiac arrest: ACLS and CPR
  - Start CPR promptly
  - Hypotension
    - Smaller adrenaline doses (<1mcg/kg, e.g. 1/10th of the usual, or 10 to 100 mcg IV); high doses cause acidosis and arrhythmias, impair lipid rescue and give less sustained ROSC
      - Cardiovascular instability in LAST is best treated with lipid; the cause is drug toxicity, not ischaemia
    - Avoid vasopressin: raises afterload alone, with no benefit
  - Arrhythmias are often refractory
    - Amiodarone is the first-line agent for malignant arrhythmias
    - Do not use lignocaine (worsens LAST)
    - Avoid calcium channel blockers and beta blockers (may worsen conduction block)
  - Recovery from cardiac arrest may take >1 h; keep resuscitating during lipid treatment
  - Refractory arrest: may need mechanical circulatory support (CPB or ECMO) until toxicity wears off
    - Alert the perfusion team early in a severe event so extracorporeal support is ready if needed
- Seizures: small increments of midazolam, 1 to 5 mg (alternatives: propofol, thiopentone)
  - Refractory seizures: consider a small dose of suxamethonium to limit acidosis and hypoxia from lactate production, oxygen use and poor ventilation
- Bloods for local anaesthetic and triglyceride levels (plain and heparinised tubes), before and after lipid

## Vertebral artery injection

- Small volumes can cause seizures
- Management
  - Airway, breathing, circulation
  - Give an anticonvulsant
  - Be ready to treat as LAST (lipid) if seizures persist or arrhythmias or cardiovascular collapse occur

## Flashcards

- Q: Lipid emulsion loading dose and infusion in LAST?
  - A: 1.5ml/kg over 1min, then 15ml/kg/hr
- Q: Refractory LAST: how many extra boluses and what infusion rate?
  - A: Up to 2 more 1.5ml/kg boluses every 5 minutes (3 total); double the infusion to 30ml/kg/hr
- Q: Maximum cumulative lipid dose?
  - A: 12ml/kg (about 850ml for 70 kg)
- Q: Adrenaline dosing in LAST cardiac arrest?
  - A: Smaller doses, <1mcg/kg (e.g. 10 to 100 mcg IV)
- Q: Which antiarrhythmics to avoid, and which is first line?
  - A: Avoid lignocaine, beta blockers and calcium channel blockers; amiodarone first line
