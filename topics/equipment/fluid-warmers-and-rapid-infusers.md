# Fluid warmers and rapid infusers

Devices that warm IV fluids and blood, and (for rapid infusers) deliver them under pressure; choose by the flow rate needed.

## Indications

- **Fluid warming** (eg packed red cell transfusion)
  - **Do not warm platelets**: heat or rapid infusion makes them stick to the tubing
  - **Do not warm cryoprecipitate**
- **Pressurised fluid delivery**
  - Alternative: **pressure bag**

## Heat transfer

- 1 L of fluid at 21°C lowers core temperature by 0.25°C
- Low flow (below 500 mL/h): **long transit time**, so fluid cools a lot in the **tubing between warmer and patient**
  - Worse with lower flow and longer tubing
  - Needs **warming close to the patient** or coaxial insulation
- High flow (above 9 L/h): **short contact time** and limited surface limit heat exchange
  - Needs **large-bore, highly conductive** heat exchangers

## Device types

- **Prewarming: warming cabinets**
  - Pre-warm by convection or conduction
  - Poor at low flows, as fluid cools quickly in standard lines
- **1. Dry warming: eg Barkey S-line**
  - Low-flow **dry-heat sleeve** (1.5 m) around standard IV tubing; no special cassette
  - **Place close to the patient** to avoid distal heat loss
  - Pros: **compact, no consumables**, fits standard tubing
  - Cons: **low flow only** (below 1,500 mL/hr); not for large-volume or rapid transfusion
- **2. Counter-current coaxial water: eg Hotline**
  - Triple lumen: **warmed water jacket** around the fluid line up to the patient end
  - Pros: warms better than Barkey; keeps delivery normothermic at **moderate flows** (up to 5,000 mL/hour)
  - Cons: **costly disposables**; **less effective above 5 L/h**; risk of **cross-contamination** and **fluid breach**; do not use with pressure devices above 300 mmHg
- **3. Rapid infusers**
  - **Level 1**: counter-current water exchange through a large-bore aluminium exchanger, **driven by pneumatic pressure**
    - Pros: best heat transfer above 9 L/h, though rarely a true 37°C at maximum flow
    - Cons: costly disposables; air embolism risk; constant pressure can rupture veins
  - **Belmont Rapid Infuser**: electromagnetic **induction** heating, **integrated roller pump**, **3.0-liter reservoir**
    - Pros: highest flow, up to **1,000 mL/min (60,000 mL/hour)**; **instant induction heating** with no water bath or warm-up; ultrasonic air detection; automatic pressure control to protect veins
    - Cons: **very costly disposables**; complex, **slower to set up**
    - **Can run packed cells and FFP together; not platelets or cryoprecipitate**
    - **Calcium-free fluids only (eg Plasmalyte, Sterofundin, albumin)**

## Belmont setup

- 1. Load the machine
  - Open the door, **insert the giving set** matching the colour-coded tubing guides exactly
  - Close and lock the door firmly
- 2. Prime the main circuit
  - **Spike** the bag (Plasmalyte or red cells) and open all clamps to **fill the chamber**
  - Press **Prime** on the touch screen
  - If priming fails, check: bags fully spiked, enough fluid, all clamps open, no kinks
- 3. Prime the patient line
  - Make sure the line is not connected to the patient
  - Press **Pt line prime**
  - Inspect the whole patient line for air bubbles

## Complications

- **Fluid contamination** (Hotline)
- **Air embolism** (Level 1)
- **Haemolysis** and thermal injury to red cells: keep blood warmers at **43 °C maximum**; water baths and microwaves are not advised
- **Shear-force haemolysis** with rapid infusion
- **Vein rupture**

## Flashcards

- Q: How much does 1 L of fluid at 21°C lower core temperature?
  - A: About 0.25°C
- Q: Which blood products should not be warmed?
  - A: Platelets and cryoprecipitate
- Q: Maximum flow rate of the Belmont rapid infuser?
  - A: 1,000 mL/min (60,000 mL/hour)
- Q: Flow limits of Barkey and Hotline?
  - A: Barkey below 1,500 mL/hr; Hotline up to 5,000 mL/hour
- Q: Maximum blood warmer temperature?
  - A: 43 °C
