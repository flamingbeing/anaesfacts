# Flexible Bronchoscope

Flexible fibreoptic or video scope for airway inspection, intubation and lung isolation checks; scope must be under 0.9 of the tube ID.

## How it works
- Coated glass fibres carry light and images by total internal reflection
  - Light enters a fibre, bounces repeatedly off its outer glass layer and leaves the far end unchanged
- Fibres are 5-20 micrometres across, so they transmit light yet stay flexible
- Each fibre has a thin outer glass coat of lower refractive index, which optically insulates it from its neighbours
- A typical bundle holds up to 10000 fibres
- Image fibres are arranged "coherently": the same layout at both ends
  - Each fibre carries a tiny part of the picture, so the layout must match for a clear image

## Components
- Control unit
  - Tip deflection knob: bends 60-180 degrees in the vertical plane
  - Ports for suction and instrument channels
  - Dioptre (focusing) ring
  - Eyepiece or video
- Insertion cord: outer diameter must be below 0.9 of the tracheal tube ID
- Glass fibre bundles (2)
  - Light (incoherent) bundle: carries light from the source, which is external or in the handle
  - Image (coherent) bundle: gives a high-resolution image
- Angulation wires in the cord steer the tip
  - 120° field of view; 180° up and 130° down angulation
- Working channel: suction of secretions; instillation of lignocaine or saline
  - Clamp the suction before giving drugs
  - Paediatric scopes may be too small to have a working channel
- Light source: for example a light cable from an external source

## Indications
- Diagnostic
  - Airway inspection
  - Biopsy and brushings
- Therapeutic
  - Bronchial toilet (mucus plugging, collapse)
  - Treatment of haemoptysis
  - Stent insertion
- Airway management
  - Awake intubation
  - Aid to intubation: unstable cervical spine, difficult airway
  - Confirm position of tracheal, endobronchial, double-lumen or tracheostomy tubes
  - Placing and positioning DLTs and bronchial blockers

## Contraindications
- Respiratory and airway
  - High oxygen or ventilation requirements
  - Active bronchospasm
  - Pneumothorax
- Uncontrolled coagulopathy
- Systemic
  - Unstable arrhythmias
  - Raised ICP
  - Severe acidosis

## Complications
- Related to local anaesthetic
- Related to bronchoscopy
  - Airway obstruction, hypoxia
  - Laryngospasm, bronchospasm
  - Bleeding
  - Arrhythmias, hypertension, raised ICP

## Choosing a size
- Larger scope
  - Larger suction channel, better suction
  - Less scope-tube gap when railroading, so the tube tip is less likely to catch on the epiglottis or cords
- Smaller scope
  - Less friction inside the tube
    - Scope OD must be below 90% of tube ID: OD scope / ID tube below 0.9
  - Better ventilation around the scope (especially in children)
    - Scope cross-sectional area should be no more than 50% of the tube lumen (ratio below 0.5)
    - For a circle in a circle this works out as OD scope / ID tube below 0.7

## Single-use scopes (outer diameter / working channel)
- Ambu
  - Large: 5.8/2.8 mm
  - Regular: 5.0/2.2 mm
  - Slim: 3.8/1.2 mm
- Vathin
  - Slim: 3.2/1.2 mm
  - Zero: 2.2/0 mm (no working channel)

## Bronchoscopic examination
- Enter via the catheter mount adaptor
- Tracheal tube
  - Clear plastic with markings
  - Murphy eye
- Trachea
  - Mucosa
  - Anterior and lateral walls: C-shaped cartilage rings
  - Posterior wall: trachealis muscle
  - Length 10-15 cm in adults (cricoid to bifurcation)
  - Diameter 19-22 mm
- Carina (C1)
  - Cartilage ridge running front to back between the main bronchi
  - Lumen narrows a little towards the carina
  - Divides into right and left main bronchi

## Right bronchial tree
- Right main bronchus
  - More vertical and shorter than the left
  - Divides at the secondary carina (C2) into:
    - Bronchus intermedius straight ahead
    - Right upper lobe bronchus at 0300, 1-2 cm in
- Right upper lobe bronchus (3 segments)
  - Turn the scope 90 degrees to the right to enter
  - Apical RB1, posterior RB2 and anterior RB3, clockwise ("Mercedes-Benz" trifurcation)
    - The only place with 3 openings
  - In 1/250 people the right upper lobe bronchus arises directly off the carina
- Return to the right main bronchus
- Bronchus intermedius
  - Divides into middle and lower lobe bronchi (tertiary carina, C3)
  - Turn the scope 90 degrees to the left so the tertiary carina is vertical and the membranous part is inferior
    - Arrow pattern: middle lobe at 1200, common basal straight ahead, apical basal at 0600
    - Ridges of the membranous wall always lead to the lower lobe
- Right middle lobe bronchus (2 segments)
  - Seen at 1200; D-shaped
  - Lateral RB4 and medial RB5
- Right lower lobe (1 + 4 = 5 segments)
  - Divides at a quaternary carina (C4)
  - Superior (apical) RB6 at 0600
  - Enter the right common basal bronchus
    - Medial basal RB7 ("D'Artagnan")
    - Then anterior basal RB8, lateral basal RB9 and posterior basal RB10, clockwise ("3 Musketeers")
- Withdraw to the trachea

## Left bronchial tree
- Left main bronchus
  - More horizontal and longer than the right
  - Divides at the secondary carina (C2) into upper and lower lobe bronchi
  - Again, membranous ridges lead to the lower lobe
- Left upper lobe bronchus (2 + 2 = 4 segments)
  - Divides into upper and lingular divisions at 0900 (tertiary carina, C3)
  - Upper division: apicoposterior LB1/2 and anterior LB3 (quaternary carina, C4)
  - Lingula: superior LB4 and inferior LB5 (quaternary carina, C4)
- Return to the left main bronchus
- Left lower lobe bronchus
  - Divides at a further carina (noted as C4, and as tertiary C3 for the apical segment)
  - Apical segment LB6 at 0600
  - Enter the left common basal bronchus
    - Anteromedial LB8, lateral LB9 and posterior basal LB10 ("PAN PAN PAN")

## Anaesthesia for flexible bronchoscopy
- Techniques
  - Topical local anaesthetic with or without sedation: usually possible (unlike rigid bronchoscopy)
  - General anaesthesia: topical anaesthesia still matters, to prevent bucking
- Local anaesthetic and sedation
  - Conscious sedation may help tolerance
  - Careful short-acting opioids for cough suppression
  - High-flow nasal oxygen can support oxygenation
  - The scope partly blocks the airway and increases work of breathing
  - Cannot give positive pressure through the scope: remove it and bag the patient, with or without an LMA or ETT
- General anaesthesia
  - Tracheal tube or supraglottic airway
    - LMA gives access to the proximal trachea (unlike an ETT)
  - Tubeless options: spontaneous ventilation, jet ventilation
  - Good topical anaesthesia still needed: less coughing, laryngospasm and sympathetic surge
  - Muscle relaxation not needed
- High-grade airway obstruction: extreme caution
  - GA and paralysis can collapse the airway (loss of muscle tone, negative pressure)
  - Keep the patient breathing spontaneously; assess dynamic obstruction with the scope before giving a neuromuscular blocker
  - Rigid bronchoscopy or emergency VV ECMO must be immediately available

## Flashcards
- Q: What is the maximum ratio of scope OD to tube ID for the scope to pass smoothly?
  - A: Below 0.9
- Q: What OD/ID ratio still allows ventilation around the scope?
  - A: Below 0.7 (scope area no more than 50% of the lumen)
- Q: At what position is the right upper lobe bronchus seen, and how far in?
  - A: At 0300, 1-2 cm into the right main bronchus
- Q: How many segments does the right lower lobe have?
  - A: 5 (superior RB6 plus 4 basal RB7-RB10)
- Q: What must be available when scoping a patient with high-grade airway obstruction?
  - A: Rigid bronchoscopy or emergency VV ECMO
