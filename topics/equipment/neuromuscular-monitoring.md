# Neuromuscular Monitoring with a Nerve Stimulator

Depth of neuromuscular block is judged from the muscle response to stimulation of its motor nerve.

## Uses

- Mainly near the end of surgery:
  - to decide whether the block is ready for reversal (e.g. with neostigmine)
  - a few minutes after reversal, to check how well it has worked

## Factors potentiating neuromuscular blockade

- Renal or liver failure (see neuromuscular blockers overview for the full list)

## Differential muscle sensitivity

- Some muscles resist both depolarising and non-depolarising blockers
  - Most resistant: diaphragm
  - Less resistant: laryngeal muscles and corrugator supercilii
  - Most sensitive, in order: abdominal > orbicularis oris > geniohyoid (upper airway) > peripheral limb muscles (including adductor pollicis)
- Smaller muscles are more sensitive
  - Hand muscles are small next to the diaphragm, so peripheral monitoring does not show the true depth of diaphragmatic block
- Implications
  - Intubation: deep block at adductor pollicis or flexor hallucis usually means the pharyngeal muscles are blocked too, giving good intubating conditions
  - Surgery: no twitch at adductor pollicis (APM) does not guarantee a paralysed diaphragm; a post-tetanic count of 1 or 2 at the APM is a deep block, yet the diaphragm can still move
  - Recovery: train-of-four ratio (TOFR) >0.9 at the APM means full recovery, including airway muscles and diaphragm
  - Facial muscles overestimate recovery, as muscles around the eye resist blockers; if the arms are inaccessible, monitor the face but switch to the arm once it is available

## Nerves that may be monitored

- Ulnar nerve (preferred)
  - Stimulated at the forearm; response is thumb adduction (adductor pollicis)
  - Easy to reach, and results are not confused by direct muscle stimulation
  - One ulnar branch crosses to the radial side of the hand to supply adductor pollicis
  - Best response: negative electrode 1 cm proximal to the wrist crease, positive electrode 4–5 cm further proximal along the ulnar side of the volar forearm
- Posterior tibial nerve
  - Electrodes above the medial malleolus; response is great toe flexion
  - Next best site if neither ulnar nerve is available
  - Recovery is generally faster at the big toe
- Facial nerve
  - Stimulated at the ear; watch orbicularis oculi (eyelid) or corrugator supercilii (eyebrow)
  - Less accurate than ulnar; if used during surgery, confirm reversal at the ulnar nerve before extubation, ideally objectively
    - Return of response around the eye may not mean other muscles have recovered enough for safe extubation
    - Corrugator supercilii tracks the diaphragm and larynx; orbicularis oculi tracks adductor pollicis
    - The two are hard to tell apart in practice, so facial monitoring may overestimate recovery
  - May be needed when positioning blocks access to hands and feet (e.g. arms tucked under drapes in laparoscopic pelvic surgery)
  - Electrodes: positive on the mastoid process, negative just in front of the tragus, to limit direct muscle stimulation
    - Or both electrodes in front of the ear along the facial nerve
  - Quantitative (acceleromyographic) monitoring is possible and recommended at both corrugator supercilii and orbicularis oculi

## Other factors

- Access to a limb during surgery
- Paralysed limb: do not monitor it
  - Denervation upregulates acetylcholine receptors, giving resistance to non-depolarising blockers and a variably exaggerated TOFR in paretic limbs

## Equipment

- Peripheral nerve stimulator
- Electrodes over the ulnar or facial nerve
  - Two surface electrodes (small ECG electrodes) over the nerve, leads to the stimulator
  - Or ball electrodes mounted on the stimulator casing

## Technique

- Electrode placement
  - Common sites: ulnar nerve at the wrist, common peroneal nerve at the fibular neck, posterior tibial nerve at the ankle, facial nerve
  - Negative electrode (black) directly over the most superficial part of the nerve: current is denser around it than around the positive electrode (Brull, 1995)
    - Black lead distal; for the ulnar nerve, in line with the 5th finger, 1-2cm below the skin crease
  - Positive electrode (red) proximal along the nerve, to avoid direct muscle stimulation
- Supramaximal stimulus, so every motor fibre depolarises
  - Ulnar nerve: 40-60mA (may need more in obese patients)
- Stimulus is short, usually 0.1ms (less than 0.2-0.3ms), monophasic square wave to avoid repetitive firing
- Response can be watched, felt, measured with a force transducer, or recorded as electrical activity (EMG)
- Ulnar nerve stimulation
  - Response: contraction of the adductor pollicis brevis
  - Current 40-50mA
  - Count thumb twitches
  - Give reversal at TOF count of 3 or more (not when TOF count <3)
- Advanced devices monitor continuously, with graphic and numeric display of the train-of-four and its trend

## Patterns of stimulation

Five main patterns; the response pattern describes the block.

### Single twitch

- 0.1ms square wave at 1 Hz to 0.1 Hz (one every second to one every 10 seconds)
- Best used at onset of block
  - 1 Hz (one per second) to find the supramaximal current
  - Then 0.1 Hz (one per 10 s) to watch the block develop
- Main drawback: needs a control twitch before the blocker is given
  - Post-tetanic count also uses single twitches but needs no control
- Simplest pattern; needs a baseline twitch height
  - Partial block with depolarising or non-depolarising relaxants reduces twitch height
  - Alone it only shows paralysed or not, not the degree; least precise for partial block
- Twitch height does not fall until 75% of receptors are occupied
  - Margin of safety: few receptors are needed to reach the summated end-plate potential that fires the muscle
- With fade present, single twitch response is proportional to frequency

### Tetanic stimulation

- Usually 0.1 ms stimuli at 50 Hz (most sensitive)
  - Above 30 Hz twitches fuse into a sustained contraction (tetany)
  - Response can exceed a single twitch, as muscle elastic forces need not be overcome for each twitch
- Partial non-depolarising block: tetanic response fades
  - Fade comes from block of presynaptic ACh receptors, which removes the positive feedback that mobilises ACh at peak activity
  - Partial depolarising block: reduced response but no fade
- Painful, so used in anaesthetised patients

### Train of four (TOF)

- Four 0.1 ms stimuli at 2 Hz (0.5 seconds apart); do not repeat within 10 seconds
- Shows depth of block (1-2 twitches suits abdominal surgery) and readiness for reversal
  - Older teaching: reversal can be given at TOF count 1-2 with intermediate-acting blockers
  - No baseline twitch needed
- As block deepens: fade first, then T4 disappears, then T3, T2, and finally T1
- Twitch count
  - Reverse only at 3 twitches (traditionally 1-2)
- Fade: TOF ratio = T4:T1 height
  - Gives an objective measure of residual block
  - Residual block = TOF ratio <0.9
- Receptor occupancy
  - Above 70% occupancy, T4 starts to shrink (75% at TOF ratio 0.75)
  - 3 twitches: T4 lost when T1 is about 25% of baseline; occupancy over 80%
  - 2 twitches: T3 lost at 85%
  - 1 twitch: T2 lost at 90%
  - No twitch: block >95%

### Post-tetanic count (PTC)

- 50 Hz tetanus for 5 seconds, 3 second pause, then single twitches at 1 Hz
- Twitch count is inversely related to depth of block
  - Based on post-tetanic potentiation: twitches after a tetanus are larger, perhaps from ACh mobilisation and/or more Ca2+ in the nerve terminal
- Used for deep block when TOF count is zero (block >95%, no response to single twitch)
  - e.g. neurosurgery, microsurgery, ophthalmic surgery, where small movements disturb the field
  - When diaphragm movement must be prevented
  - Best with infusions, aiming for PTC of 2
- Predicts time to return of single twitch, so measures blocks too deep for other methods
  - PTC of 2 (palpated): no twitch for about 20-30 minutes
    - Very deep block; virtually ensures immobility (e.g. some neurosurgical and open eye procedures)
    - PTC = 0 may follow an intubating dose, but a block deeper than PTC = 1 is rarely needed
  - PTC of 5: about 10-15 minutes
  - T1 of the TOF usually returns at PTC of 9-10 (range 6–16)
- Tetanic effects can last up to 6 minutes and falsely suggest inadequate block on single twitch or TOF; do not repeat within 6mins

### Double burst stimulation (DBS)

- Two 50 Hz bursts 750 msec apart; each burst is three 0.2 ms stimuli 20 ms apart (i.e. 50 Hz)
- Designed for better visual or tactile detection of small residual non-depolarising block than TOF
  - Tactile DBS ratio is more accurate than tactile TOF ratio
- DBS ratio behaves much like TOF ratio; measured mechanically, DBS is no more sensitive than TOF

## Qualitative monitoring

- Visual or tactile
- Observers cannot reliably detect fade at adductor pollicis once TOF ratio exceeds 0.4
- Reverse only at 3 twitches (traditionally 1-2)
  - If no fade is seen, reduce the neostigmine dose: no more than 20–25 mcg/kg
- Allow time before extubation: neostigmine can take up to 10 min to peak

## Quantitative monitoring

- Monitors use electromyography (EMG), acceleromyography (AMG) or, less often, kinemyography (KMG)
- Readings vary, so repeat until two in a row are within 10 % and average them

## Clinical tests

- Not accurate

## Flashcards

- Q: What TOF ratio at adductor pollicis defines full recovery?
  - A: TOF ratio >0.9 (residual block is <0.9)
- Q: Which is the most resistant muscle to neuromuscular blockers, and what does that mean for monitoring?
  - A: The diaphragm; no twitch at adductor pollicis does not guarantee a still diaphragm
- Q: What does a PTC of 2 predict?
  - A: No twitch response for about 20-30 minutes (very deep block)
- Q: Above what TOF ratio can observers no longer reliably feel or see fade?
  - A: 0.4
- Q: Maximum neostigmine dose when no fade is seen on qualitative TOF?
  - A: 20–25 mcg/kg
